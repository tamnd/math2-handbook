Some algebraic theories can be analysed completely: their complete extensions can be listed by invariants, and their definable sets described. Modules are the main example. A positive primitive formula in the language of $R$-modules says that a system of linear equations has a solution, $\exists \bar y\, (A\bar x + B\bar y = 0)$, and in every module $M$ it defines a subgroup of $M^n$, where $n$ is the length of $\bar x$. The Baur–Monk theorem says that every formula is equivalent, in the theory of $R$-modules, to a Boolean combination of positive primitive formulas and sentences that bound the index of one positive primitive subgroup in another. It follows that the theory of every module is stable. Ziegler built on this a topological space of indecomposable pure-injective modules, now called the Ziegler spectrum, which links model theory with the representation theory of rings.

Abelian groups were the first case. Wanda Szmielew proved in 1955 that the [theory of abelian groups](/FWP8NY5VE7/) is decidable, and [Szmielew's theorem](/Y0753DF5R9/) says that two abelian groups are elementarily equivalent exactly when they have the same invariants, dimensions of vector spaces over $\F_p$ built from the group. Tarski proved that the theory of [Boolean algebras](/0XADJKM699/) is decidable, with invariants of the same kind; the [atomless Boolean algebra](/NFEEGWJFCK/) is the unique countable model of its complete theory. The [elementary theory of groups](/RX9MB386C5/) in general is undecidable, but Tarski asked about free groups, and Sela and, independently, Kharlampovich and Myasnikov [answered him](/NCWBT0BZC3/): all free groups of rank at least $2$ are elementarily equivalent, and their common theory is decidable.

These theories show what a complete classification looks like. They supply examples and counterexamples for stability theory, and their methods, such as the [Feferman–Vaught theorem](/RYMZZ2AWFT/) for products, apply to other algebraic structures.
