First-order logic cannot say that a group is a torsion group, that a graph is connected, or that an ordered field is Archimedean; the entry [properties that are not first-order](/T1S9T0RKHJ/) in Chapter 4.1 proves results of this kind with compactness. An [infinitary logic](/PQG7VAZ1R2/) can. The logic $L_{\omega_1\omega}$ allows countable conjunctions and disjunctions with finite strings of quantifiers, so "every element $x$ satisfies $x = 0$ or $2x = 0$ or $3x = 0$ or …" is one sentence. The price is compactness: the sentences $c \ne 0$, $c \ne 1$, $c \ne 2$, … together with "every element is one of $0, 1, 2, \dots$" are finitely satisfiable but have no model. Other properties survive. The downward Löwenheim–Skolem theorem still holds for $L_{\omega_1\omega}$, Scott proved that every countable structure in a countable signature is described up to isomorphism among countable structures by a single sentence of $L_{\omega_1\omega}$, and [Karp's theorem](/KAWWZ2DF0G/) in Chapter 4.4 matches equivalence in $L_{\infty\omega}$ with [back-and-forth systems](/EWB8KXZMCV/). Barwise found that compactness returns in a weaker form for countable admissible fragments, the formulas that lie in a countable model of a weak set theory.

A second way to strengthen first-order logic adds [generalized quantifiers](/MFV98WTF4G/), such as "there exist uncountably many $x$". Keisler gave a complete set of axioms for this quantifier and proved that the logic is countably compact. A third way is [second-order logic](/G6X7SZSNC5/), which describes $\N$ and $\R$ up to isomorphism but has neither compactness nor a complete proof system.

Abstract model theory compares such logics by the theorems they satisfy. [Lindström's theorem](/FYZ3CJGZYV/) says that first-order logic is a maximal logic with both the compactness theorem and the downward Löwenheim–Skolem property: every regular logic that extends it and has both properties says no more than it. The theorem is a standard explanation of the central place of first-order logic.
