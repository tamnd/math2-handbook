## History

Preservation theorems began with products. Alfred Horn studied in 1951 the sentences now named after him and proved that they are preserved under direct products. In the mid-1950s Jerzy Łoś and Alfred Tarski proved the theorem that carries both their names: a theory whose models are closed under substructures has universal axioms. Łoś and Roman Suszko announced the corresponding theorem for unions of chains and later published it jointly, and Chen Chung Chang gave a proof in his 1959 paper "On unions of chains of models". In the same year Roger Lyndon published "Properties preserved under homomorphism", where he proved the theorem on surjective homomorphisms and positive sentences with the help of his interpolation theorem.

The characterization of Horn sentences took longer. Chang and Anne Morel showed that preservation under direct products does not single out the Horn sentences. H. Jerome Keisler proved in 1965, under the continuum hypothesis, that the sentences preserved under reduced products are exactly the Horn sentences, and Fred Galvin later removed the hypothesis. On the algebraic side, Anatoly Mal'cev characterized quasivarieties by closure under subalgebras, products and ultraproducts, which gives the model-theoretic setting of [universal Horn classes](/9A4CYWXPEA/).

Definability has an older root. Alessandro Padoa described around 1900 the method that bears his name for showing that a notion cannot be defined from others. Evert Willem Beth proved the converse direction for first-order logic in 1953, and William Craig soon derived [Beth's theorem](/RJBDSA2ETP/) from his interpolation theorem. Lars Svenonius published his characterization by automorphisms in 1959, and Chang and Michael Makkai, in work published in 1964, gave the count of automorphic images in saturated models. Pseudo-elementary classes arose with the study of reducts; Tarski showed in 1955 that the representable relation algebras, which form such a class, are even a variety.

Over finite structures the picture is different, and finite model theory has asked which theorems survive. William Tait showed in 1959 that the Łoś–Tarski theorem fails. Miklós Ajtai and Yuri Gurevich showed in 1987 that Lyndon's theorem fails, and Alexei Stolboushkin gave a simpler counterexample in 1995. Benjamin Rossman proved in 2008 that the homomorphism preservation theorem, for all homomorphisms and existential positive sentences, holds over finite structures.

## Further reading

- C. C. Chang and H. J. Keisler, *Model Theory*, third edition (1990). The classical source for the preservation theorems for substructures, chains, homomorphisms and reduced products, including the Horn sentence theorem.
- Wilfrid Hodges, *Model Theory* (1993). A full treatment of preservation, interpolation and definability theorems.
- Wilfrid Hodges, *A Shorter Model Theory* (1997). The shorter edition of the same book, the source of several entries of this chapter.
- Leonid Libkin, *Elements of Finite Model Theory* (2004). Explains which classical theorems fail over finite structures and why.
- Stanley Burris and H. P. Sankappanavar, *A Course in Universal Algebra* (1981). Varieties, quasivarieties and the algebraic side of the Horn classes.
