Some properties of a class of structures can be read off from the shape of its axioms. A universal sentence that holds in a structure holds in each of its [substructures](/676H7EGTKJ/), so a class defined by universal axioms is closed under substructures. The [Łoś–Tarski theorem](/9RN0KYXNT1/) gives the converse: a theory whose class of models is closed under substructures is equivalent to a set of universal sentences. The group axioms show both sides. With multiplication, inverse and identity in the signature they are universal, and the substructures of a group are its subgroups. With multiplication alone the substructures are subsemigroups, such as $\set{1, 2, 3, \dots}$ inside $(\Z, +)$, and the axioms of groups need an existential quantifier. Theorems of this kind are preservation theorems. Others match unions of chains with $\forall\exists$ axioms (the Chang–Łoś–Suszko theorem, a relative of the [elementary chain theorem](/NHCRZ74JHH/)), homomorphic images with positive sentences (Lyndon's theorem), and [reduced products](/F4WRD396PB/) with Horn sentences.

Definability theorems compare two ways in which a theory can determine a relation. A relation symbol $P$ is implicitly defined by $T$ when any two models of $T$ that agree on the other symbols agree on $P$, and explicitly defined when some formula without $P$ is equivalent to it modulo $T$. The [Beth definability theorem](/RJBDSA2ETP/) says that the two agree in first-order logic. In real closed fields the order is implicitly defined by the field operations, and $x \le y \leftrightarrow \exists z \, (y = x + z^2)$ is an explicit definition. Beth's theorem follows from the [Craig interpolation theorem](/E2CBFNAVZY/), and most preservation theorems are proved by compactness with the [diagram lemmas](/EP5E317TNW/). Many of them fail when only finite structures are allowed, and which ones survive is a question of finite model theory.
