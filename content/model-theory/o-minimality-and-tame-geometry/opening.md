An expansion of a dense linear order is [o-minimal](/DTWHAW1N0V/) when every definable subset of the line, with parameters, is a finite union of points and open intervals. These are the sets that the order alone defines, so the extra structure adds nothing new in one variable. The real field is o-minimal: by [Tarski's quantifier elimination](/FSJ9NGRRPA/), a definable subset of $\R$ is a [semialgebraic set](/BX5WSK8ZCT/) in one variable, and these are finite unions of points and intervals. Dense linear orders and ordered divisible abelian groups are also examples. Adding the sine function destroys the property, since its zero set $\pi\Z$ is infinite and discrete, and with it the field defines $\Z$ and all of arithmetic.

The one-variable condition controls all dimensions. A definable function on an interval is continuous and monotone on each of finitely many subintervals, and every definable set in $\R^n$ is a finite union of cells, pieces built inductively from graphs and bands. Knight, Pillay and Steinhorn proved this in 1986, and showed that every structure elementarily equivalent to an o-minimal one is o-minimal. So definable sets have finitely many connected components, a well-behaved dimension and triangulations, which is the tame topology that Grothendieck asked for. The main examples are expansions of the real field: by the restricted analytic functions, whose definable sets are the globally [subanalytic sets](/K1HTQF26MA/); by the exponential function, o-minimal by Wilkie's theorem of 1996; and by both together, by van den Dries, Macintyre and Marker. Macintyre and Wilkie proved that the theory of the real exponential field is decidable if [Schanuel's conjecture](/65PV4VFP9Y/) holds. In 2006 Pila and Wilkie bounded the number of rational points of height at most $T$ on the transcendental part of a set definable in an o-minimal expansion of the real field by $O(T^\varepsilon)$ for every $\varepsilon > 0$. Pila and Zannier used this bound to reprove the Manin–Mumford conjecture, and Pila used it to prove the André–Oort conjecture for products of modular curves.
