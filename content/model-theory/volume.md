[Model theory](/HFTBG5PMXC/) studies mathematical structures through the first-order sentences that are true in them. A [structure](/983GZBQYT4/) is a set with functions, relations and constants, such as a group, a field, an ordered set or a graph, and a [model](/MVCH6N9SNW/) of a theory is a structure in which every sentence of the theory is true. The subject asks which classes of structures a theory can describe and how many models of each size it has, which structures no first-order sentence can tell apart, and which sets a formula can define inside one structure. Chang and Keisler called model theory universal algebra plus logic; Hodges called it algebraic geometry minus fields, because it treats the [definable sets](/CY1TXHF6KQ/) of a structure as algebraic geometry treats the zero sets of polynomials.

The results reach well beyond logic. The [Ax–Grothendieck theorem](/M2XAN2HGZH/), that an injective polynomial map $\C^n \to \C^n$ is surjective, follows from the finite fields by a transfer principle. The [Ax–Kochen theorem](/PHT3TJCS90/) transfers statements between $p$-adic fields and fields of power series by ultraproducts. Hrushovski's proof of the Mordell–Lang conjecture for function fields and Pila's proof of cases of the André–Oort conjecture use geometric stability and o-minimality, respectively. [Nonstandard analysis](/WM62GKCBWN/) gives rigorous infinitesimals, and [finite model theory](/4XRVJE0KAN/) ties logic to computational complexity. Volume 2 holds the syntax of first-order logic and its completeness and compactness theorems; this volume starts from them and studies the structures. The [history of model theory](/JNBJW8RTJD/) traces the subject from Löwenheim and Skolem to the present.

## What the volume teaches

- **A structure has a theory, and a theory has models.** The [theory of a structure](/X9HV9C6X4F/) is the set of sentences it satisfies. Structures with the same theory are [elementarily equivalent](/P3B1V182N4/), and a [complete theory](/655K8Y7VS8/) decides every sentence.
- **First-order logic cannot fix the size of an infinite structure.** By the [Löwenheim–Skolem theorem](/484M0TZ1S7/), a theory with an infinite model has models of every infinite cardinality at least the size of its language, which is the source of [Skolem's paradox](/68QZWDXAQ4/).
- **Some properties are not first-order.** Finiteness, torsion, well-ordering and connectedness cannot be axiomatized, as [properties that are not first-order](/T1S9T0RKHJ/) shows with compactness.
- **Elementary substructures are tested by witnesses.** The [Tarski–Vaught test](/60SA99BER7/) reduces elementarity to finding witnesses for existential formulas, and Skolem hulls build small elementary substructures.
- **Ultraproducts combine many structures into one.** By [Łoś's theorem](/AGYBSTD7BG/), a sentence holds in an [ultraproduct](/AX9Q86GB8M/) exactly when it holds in almost all factors. This gives a second proof of compactness, and the [Keisler–Shelah theorem](/A37HPCKCE2/) says that two structures are elementarily equivalent exactly when they have isomorphic ultrapowers.
- **Games measure what a sentence can say.** By the [Ehrenfeucht–Fraïssé theorem](/72BMYXGFA8/), two structures agree on sentences of [quantifier rank](/FVEVRE5XNZ/) $n$ exactly when Duplicator wins the $n$-round game, and the [back-and-forth method](/6F2DB4HTJR/) proves isomorphisms such as Cantor's theorem on dense orders.
- **Definable sets carry the geometry.** [Quantifier elimination](/7FDDXV3JK7/) for algebraically closed and real closed fields describes their definable sets, and the later chapters classify theories by how tame these sets are.

## The chapters

**Chapter 4.1, Basic notions of model theory**, fixes the vocabulary: [substructures](/676H7EGTKJ/), [embeddings](/SCRB74SWQN/), [reducts and expansions](/EY20BF96SJ/), theories of structures, elementary equivalence, complete theories, [elementary classes](/MEC3ZMC4F1/) and [finitely axiomatizable theories](/JR6XPJ7X56/). Definable sets and the automorphism test come last, with [examples of first-order theories](/AWASYPREVT/) and properties that are not first-order.

**Chapter 4.2, Elementary extensions and the Löwenheim–Skolem theorems**, compares a structure with its extensions. [Elementary embeddings](/CGH8C8EA9N/) and [elementary substructures](/8WZF9SBP39/) preserve every formula, the Tarski–Vaught test and the [diagram lemmas](/EP5E317TNW/) build them, and the Löwenheim–Skolem theorem produces them in every infinite size. [Skolem functions](/NKQY9NB955/), the [elementary chain theorem](/NHCRZ74JHH/), [elementary amalgamation](/P2VMSM4H4Y/) and [partial elementary maps](/XTNYY0WRQ5/) are the tools of later constructions, and [elementary equivalence versus isomorphism](/4WGKBXG068/) shows that the two notions differ.

**Chapter 4.3, Ultraproducts and product constructions**, starts from [reduced products](/F4WRD396PB/) and ultraproducts. Łoś's theorem gives [compactness via ultraproducts](/EY09JT7270/), and [ultrapowers](/5ZVJK0V0GF/) give elementary extensions. The Keisler–Shelah theorem, [Frayne's theorem](/QPX9HG9BH4/) and the [characterization of elementary classes](/4HNAJQQ8Z0/) describe elementary equivalence and elementary classes by ultraproducts. The [Feferman–Vaught theorem](/RYMZZ2AWFT/) computes the theory of a product, and the [saturation of ultraproducts](/DYXQP8WS63/) leads to [Keisler's order](/TD5D2G5PYP/) on theories.

**Chapter 4.4, Back-and-forth methods and Ehrenfeucht–Fraïssé games**, proves isomorphism and equivalence step by step. The back-and-forth method and [back-and-forth systems](/EWB8KXZMCV/) give isomorphisms, the [Ehrenfeucht–Fraïssé game](/2RE7YAZK8R/) and its theorem give equivalence up to quantifier rank, and the games on [finite linear orders](/ZZPXVVZARC/) show that orders with at least $2^n - 1$ elements agree up to rank $n$. [Karp's theorem](/KAWWZ2DF0G/) matches back-and-forth systems with the infinitary logic $L_{\infty\omega}$, and [non-definability proofs by games](/VT2PSXKM50/) show that even size, connectivity and Hamiltonicity are not first-order.

## How to read this volume

If this is your first course in model theory, read these entries in this order: [model theory](/HFTBG5PMXC/), [theory of a structure](/X9HV9C6X4F/), [elementary equivalence](/P3B1V182N4/), [complete theory](/655K8Y7VS8/), [definable set](/CY1TXHF6KQ/), [properties that are not first-order](/T1S9T0RKHJ/), [elementary substructure](/8WZF9SBP39/), the [Tarski–Vaught test](/60SA99BER7/), the [Löwenheim–Skolem theorem](/484M0TZ1S7/), [ultraproduct](/AX9Q86GB8M/), [Łoś's theorem](/AGYBSTD7BG/), [compactness via ultraproducts](/EY09JT7270/), the [back-and-forth method](/6F2DB4HTJR/), the [Ehrenfeucht–Fraïssé game](/2RE7YAZK8R/) and the [Ehrenfeucht–Fraïssé theorem](/72BMYXGFA8/). Do the ◦ and • exercises of Chapters 4.1 to 4.4.

After Chapters 4.1 to 4.4, the core course continues with the openings of Chapters 4.5, 4.6 and 4.8. After it, three lines lead on. Pure model theory runs through Chapters 4.7, 4.9 and 4.10, then 4.12, 4.13 and 4.15. Tame geometry and algebra run through Chapters 4.16, 4.18 and 4.20. Logic and arithmetic run through Chapters 4.22, 4.24 and 4.27.

Each entry stands on its own; its facts box lists what it needs, with the number of the entry that explains it.

## What you need first

You need the syntax and semantics of first-order logic from Volume 2: [first-order logic](/086QKPF0A6/), [signatures](/WDVVEYD6AT/) and [formulas](/HYJ10AF6RT/) in Chapter 2.4; [satisfaction](/2RBQPV02AS/) and truth in a structure in Chapter 2.5; and [Gödel's completeness theorem](/WQ1ZE255SF/) and the [compactness theorem](/1NR3HAWG9D/) in Chapter 2.7. You should know the definitions of a [group](/28PKCPYA2V/), a [field](/0QA8CHH8MG/) and a [vector space](/000AYPA9AJ/), as in Chapter 3.5. Several proofs use [cardinal numbers](/0EBACQFAZW/), [Zorn's lemma](/04RXTQA5ZA/) and [ultrafilters](/J64Y3JC0RE/) from set theory.

## The exercises

Each chapter with entries ends with exercises in three bands. Exercises marked ◦ test close reading, such as checking whether a map is an embedding; those marked • use an idea in a new place, such as proving that a class is not elementary; those marked ★ are harder, and some ask for a known theorem in your own words. Many have hints in the appendix *Hints to the exercises*.
