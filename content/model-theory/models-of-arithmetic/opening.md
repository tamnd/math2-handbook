The [compactness theorem](/1NR3HAWG9D/) gives [Peano arithmetic](/HACXBCM7SS/) models other than $\N$: add a constant $c$ and the axioms $c > 0$, $c > 1$, $c > 2$, and so on. Every such nonstandard model begins with a copy of $\N$, followed by elements larger than every standard number. The [non-standard model of arithmetic](/1QWN5CV4CG/) in Chapter 2.5 shows that every countable one has order type $\omega + (\omega^* + \omega) \cdot \eta$, where $\eta$ is the order type of $\Q$. Models of arithmetic are the study of these structures: how they are ordered, how they extend one another, and what their initial segments look like.

The simple order type hides how complicated these models are. Tennenbaum proved that in a countable nonstandard model of $\mathsf{PA}$ neither addition nor multiplication is computable. Overspill is the basic tool: if a formula holds of every standard number in a nonstandard model, it also holds of some nonstandard number, since otherwise the formula would define the standard part and induction would fail. MacDowell and Specker proved that every model of $\mathsf{PA}$ has a proper elementary end extension, and Harvey Friedman proved that every countable nonstandard model is isomorphic to a proper initial segment of itself.

The subject also explains independence. Paris and Harrington proved their combinatorial statement unprovable by building initial segments of a nonstandard model that satisfy $\mathsf{PA}$, and Kirby and Paris proved the same for Goodstein's theorem and the hydra game. The fragments of $\mathsf{PA}$, with induction only for formulas of bounded complexity, have their own model theory, which links with bounded arithmetic and computational complexity.
