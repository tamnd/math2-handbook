## History

The word "categorical" comes from Oswald Veblen, who in 1904 called an axiom system categorical when all its models are isomorphic. Dedekind's axioms for the natural numbers and Hilbert's axioms for geometry are categorical in this sense, but they are not first-order. Georg Cantor had shown in 1895 that every countable dense linear order without endpoints is isomorphic to the rationals; the back-and-forth form of his argument came later and became the standard way to build isomorphisms between countable models. Once the Löwenheim–Skolem theorem was known, it was clear that no first-order theory with an infinite model is categorical in Veblen's sense, and the question had to be asked one cardinality at a time.

That step was taken in 1954, independently, by Jerzy Łoś and Robert Vaught. Both introduced categoricity in a cardinal and proved the [completeness test](/ZR7Y5C235P/) that now bears their names, and Vaught used it to prove completeness and decidability of several theories. Łoś asked whether a countable theory that is categorical in one uncountable cardinal is categorical in all of them. Michael Morley answered yes in "Categoricity in power" (1965), and his proof introduced $\omega$-stability and Morley rank; Saharon Shelah extended the theorem to uncountable languages in 1974. John Baldwin and Alistair Lachlan described the uncountably categorical theories by strongly minimal sets in 1971.

Countable categoricity has a different answer. In 1959 Erwin Engeler, Czesław Ryll-Nardzewski and Lars Svenonius found, independently, that a complete countable theory is $\aleph_0$-categorical exactly when it has finitely many $n$-types for each $n$. The theorem joined model theory to the study of permutation groups, and the oligomorphic groups of Peter Cameron's later work are the automorphism groups of countable $\aleph_0$-categorical structures. Gregory Cherlin, Leo Harrington and Alistair Lachlan showed in 1985 that an $\aleph_0$-categorical, $\omega$-stable theory is not finitely axiomatizable, a result that Boris Zilber had proved for totally categorical theories.

The general theory of countable models of a complete theory comes from Vaught's paper "Denumerable models of complete theories", in the proceedings of the Warsaw symposium on infinitistic methods (1961). It treats prime models, countable saturated models and the theories with countably many types, proves that the number of countable models is never two, and gives Andrzej Ehrenfeucht's example with exactly three. Morley and Vaught developed saturated models of uncountable size in "Homogeneous universal models" (1962). In the same 1961 paper Vaught asked whether a complete theory could have exactly $\aleph_1$ countable models without the continuum hypothesis; this question became the [Vaught conjecture](/YVBHE2SEE6/).

Morley proved in "The number of countable models" (1970) that the number is at most $\aleph_1$ or exactly $2^{\aleph_0}$, with methods from descriptive set theory. The conjecture was proved for $\omega$-stable theories by Shelah, Harrington and Michael Makkai (1984) and for o-minimal theories by Laura Mayer (1988), and it is still open for first-order theories in general. For sentences of the infinitary logic $L_{\omega_1 \omega}$, a preprint of 2026 by Nathanael Ackerman, Cameron Freer and Robin Knight gives a counterexample.

## Further reading

- David Marker, *Model Theory: An Introduction* (2002). Chapter 4 covers prime, atomic and saturated models, the Ryll-Nardzewski theorem, Vaught's never-two theorem and Ehrenfeucht's example, with full proofs and many examples.
- Wilfrid Hodges, *A Shorter Model Theory* (1997). Its chapter on the countable case treats omitting types and $\aleph_0$-categoricity, with the automorphism groups of countable structures in view.
- C. C. Chang and H. J. Keisler, *Model Theory*, third edition (1990). Chapter 2 is the classical account of countable models, prime models and the number of countable models.
- Katrin Tent and Martin Ziegler, *A Course in Model Theory* (2012). A modern course with a chapter on countable models and a clear route from there to Morley's categoricity theorem.
- Peter J. Cameron, *Oligomorphic Permutation Groups* (1990). The permutation-group side of $\aleph_0$-categoricity, with many examples.
