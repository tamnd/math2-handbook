## History

The combinatorics behind the chapter came first. Frank Ramsey proved his theorem on homogeneous sets in 1930, in a paper on a case of the decision problem for first-order logic. Paul Erdős and Richard Rado extended it to uncountable sets in 1956, in their partition calculus, and the [Erdős–Rado theorem](/S1BHE358VX/) is the tool that later gave indiscernibles of every length with control over types.

Andrzej Ehrenfeucht and Andrzej Mostowski brought indiscernibles into model theory in 1956. They used Ramsey's theorem and compactness to show that every theory with infinite models has models generated by indiscernible sequences, and so has models with many automorphisms. Their construction is the Ehrenfeucht–Mostowski model of the chapter, and the functoriality of the construction, an order embedding giving an elementary embedding, is what made it useful.

Michael Morley turned the method into a tool for counting types. In his proof of the categoricity theorem in 1965, he used Ehrenfeucht–Mostowski models over well-orders, which realize only countably many types over each countable set, to show that a countable theory categorical in an uncountable cardinal has only countably many types over each countable set of parameters. With the Erdős–Rado theorem in place of Ramsey's theorem, he computed the Hanf number of $L_{\omega_1\omega}$ and the Hanf number for omitting types, both $\beth_{\omega_1}$. William Hanf had shown earlier that a logic whose sentences form a set has a Hanf number at all.

The two-cardinal theorems are due to Robert Vaught and Chen Chung Chang. Vaught's theorem, proved with Vaughtian pairs and countable homogeneous models, moves any gap down to $(\aleph_1, \aleph_0)$; Chang's theorem moves it up to $(\kappa^+, \kappa)$ when $\kappa^{<\kappa} = \kappa$. The questions they left open turned out to depend on set theory. Ronald Jensen invented morasses to prove two-cardinal transfer theorems in the constructible universe, and Jack Silver proved the consistency of Chang's conjecture from the consistency of an $\omega_1$-Erdős cardinal, while the axiom of constructibility refutes it.

Generalized indiscernibles came from classification theory. Saharon Shelah used sequences indexed by trees in classification theory, and Lynn Scow later tied the existence of such indiscernibles to structural Ramsey theory: for ordered index structures, the modeling property holds exactly when the age of the index structure is a Ramsey class. Scow also characterized NIP theories by the collapse of indiscernibles indexed by ordered graphs to order-indiscernibles.

## Further reading

- C. C. Chang and H. J. Keisler, *Model Theory*. The classical source for this chapter: Ehrenfeucht–Mostowski models, both two-cardinal theorems and Hanf numbers, with complete proofs.
- David Marker, *Model Theory: An Introduction* (2002). Indiscernibles and Ehrenfeucht–Mostowski models as preparation for Morley's theorem, and Vaughtian pairs in the study of categoricity.
- Katrin Tent and Martin Ziegler, *A Course in Model Theory* (2012). A short modern treatment of indiscernibles, Ehrenfeucht–Mostowski models and Vaughtian pairs.
- Wilfrid Hodges, *Model Theory* (1993). Indiscernibles and Ehrenfeucht–Mostowski models in full combinatorial detail, with much on infinitary logic.
- John T. Baldwin, *Categoricity* (2009). The theory of $L_{\omega_1\omega}$ sentences and abstract elementary classes, where Hanf numbers and Ehrenfeucht–Mostowski models are central tools.
