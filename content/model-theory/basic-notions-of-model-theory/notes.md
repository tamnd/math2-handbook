## History

The first theorems of model theory are older than its name. Leopold Löwenheim proved in 1915 that a first-order sentence with an infinite model has a countable model. Thoralf Skolem extended the result to countable sets of sentences in 1920, and in 1922 he gave a proof without the axiom of choice. Kurt Gödel proved the completeness theorem in his thesis of 1929, published in 1930, and the compactness theorem for countable languages follows from it. Anatoly Mal'cev proved compactness for languages of any size in 1936, and in 1941 he used it to prove local theorems in group theory, an early use of logic to obtain results in algebra.

Alfred Tarski gave a precise definition of the definable sets of real numbers in 1931, and in 1933 an exact definition of truth in a formal language, the relation $\mathcal{M} \models \sigma$ on which the subject rests. His decision method for the real field, published in 1948, shows that the axioms of real closed fields give the whole theory $\operatorname{Th}(\R, +, \cdot, <)$. It also gave an early natural example of two structures that are elementarily equivalent but not isomorphic: the real field and the field of real algebraic numbers. Abraham Seidenberg published an independent proof of the elimination in 1954. In the other direction, Gödel's incompleteness theorem of 1931 showed that Peano arithmetic, written down as an axiomatization of one structure, is not a complete theory.

The notions of substructure and embedding came from algebra. Garrett Birkhoff studied subalgebras of algebras of any signature in 1935, in the paper where he characterized the classes defined by equations as those closed under homomorphic images, subalgebras and products. Abraham Robinson's book *On the Metamathematics of Algebra* (1951) used expansions by constants for all elements of a structure, the basis of the method of diagrams. In the same years Czesław Ryll-Nardzewski proved, in 1952, that Peano arithmetic is not finitely axiomatizable, and Jerzy Łoś and Robert Vaught found, independently, the categoricity test for completeness in 1954. Tarski and Vaught introduced elementary substructures in 1957.

Definable sets moved to the centre of the subject later. Roland Fraïssé and Andrzej Ehrenfeucht characterized elementary equivalence by back-and-forth systems and games, which give direct proofs that properties such as the parity of a finite order are not first-order. Peter Cameron determined the reducts of $(\Q, <)$ up to interdefinability in 1976. After Michael Morley's categoricity theorem of 1965 and Saharon Shelah's *Classification Theory and the Number of Non-isomorphic Models* (1978), the study of definable sets became the main concern of the subject, and the textbooks of Chang and Keisler (1973), Hodges (1993) and Marker (2002) record the change.

## Further reading

- David Marker, *Model Theory: An Introduction* (Springer, 2002). Its first chapter covers structures, embeddings, theories and definable sets with many examples from algebra, close to the order of this chapter.
- Wilfrid Hodges, *A Shorter Model Theory* (Cambridge University Press, 1997). Careful about signatures, substructures, reducts and definability, with many exercises; the abridged form of his *Model Theory* (1993).
- C. C. Chang and H. Jerome Keisler, *Model Theory* (North-Holland, 1973; third edition 1990). The classical reference, with a full treatment of elementary classes and of compactness arguments.
- Katrin Tent and Martin Ziegler, *A Course in Model Theory* (Cambridge University Press, 2012). A short modern course that reaches stability theory quickly; its first chapters give the basic notions in compact form.
