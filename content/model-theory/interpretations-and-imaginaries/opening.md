Many structures are built from others by taking a definable set of tuples and dividing it by a definable equivalence relation. The integers come from pairs of natural numbers, with $(a, b)$ and $(c, d)$ identified when $a + d = b + c$. The rationals come from pairs of integers with non-zero second entry, and the complex field is the plane $\R^2$ with addition and multiplication given by polynomial formulas. An interpretation of a structure $\mathcal{N}$ in a structure $\mathcal{M}$ makes this precise: the universe of $\mathcal{N}$ is a [definable set](/CY1TXHF6KQ/) of $n$-tuples of $\mathcal{M}$ modulo a definable equivalence relation, and the relations and functions of $\mathcal{N}$ are definable too. Each sentence about $\mathcal{N}$ then translates into a sentence about $\mathcal{M}$. So when the theory of $\mathcal{M}$ is [decidable](/HWC687ES0Q/), so is the theory of $\mathcal{N}$: the theory of the complex field is decidable because the theory of the real field is. Undecidability passes the other way, from the interpreted structure to the one that interprets it.

Inside one structure, the quotient of a definable set by a definable equivalence relation need not be a definable set. Shelah's construction $\mathcal{M}^{eq}$ adds a new sort for each such quotient, and its elements are called imaginaries. Every definable set then gets a canonical parameter: an imaginary that is fixed by exactly those automorphisms that fix the set. A theory eliminates imaginaries when every imaginary can be replaced by an ordinary tuple with the same automorphisms fixing it. In a pure infinite set this fails: the unordered pair $\set{a, b}$ is coded by no tuple of elements, since the automorphism swapping $a$ and $b$ fixes the pair but moves every candidate. In a field a finite set $\set{a_1, \dots, a_k}$ is coded by the coefficients of $(x - a_1) \cdots (x - a_k)$, and Poizat proved in 1983 that algebraically closed fields eliminate imaginaries. Real closed fields do too.
