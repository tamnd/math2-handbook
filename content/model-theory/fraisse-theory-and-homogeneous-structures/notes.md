## History

Roland Fraïssé introduced ages, amalgamation and the limit construction in "Sur l'extension aux relations de quelques propriétés des ordres", published in the Annales scientifiques de l'École Normale Supérieure in 1954. His starting point was Cantor's theorem that $(\Q, <)$ is the unique countable dense linear order without endpoints, and he asked which properties of orders carry over to arbitrary relations. The theorem that bears his name turns Cantor's back-and-forth argument into a statement about classes of finite structures.

The Rado graph was found several times. Ackermann's coding of the hereditarily finite sets in 1937 already gives it. Erdős and Rényi showed in 1963 that a countable random graph is, with probability $1$, isomorphic to one fixed graph, and Rado gave the explicit binary description in "Universal graphs and universal functions" in 1964. Henson described the triangle-free and more generally $K_n$-free homogeneous graphs in "A family of countable homogeneous graphs" in 1971. Gardiner classified the finite homogeneous graphs in 1976, and Lachlan and Woodrow classified the countably infinite ones in "Countable ultrahomogeneous undirected graphs", published in 1980. Schmerl classified the countable homogeneous partial orders in 1979, Lachlan the countable homogeneous tournaments in 1984, and Cherlin the countable homogeneous directed graphs in a memoir of 1998.

The study of automorphism groups as topological groups grew alongside. Dixon, Neumann and Thomas proved the small index property for the full symmetric group of a countable set in 1986, and Truss proved it for $\operatorname{Aut}(\Q, <)$. Hrushovski proved the extension property for partial automorphisms of finite graphs in "Extending partial isomorphisms of graphs", in Combinatorica in 1992, and Hodges, Hodkinson, Lascar and Shelah used it in 1993 to prove the small index property for the Rado graph. Herwig, and later Herwig and Lascar, extended EPPA to wider classes of relational structures.

Structural Ramsey theory began with the theorem of Nešetřil and Rödl of 1977 that the finite ordered graphs, and more generally the finite ordered structures in a relational signature, form a Ramsey class; Abramson and Harrington proved the same result independently in 1978. Pestov showed that $\operatorname{Aut}(\Q, <)$ is extremely amenable, and in 2005 Kechris, Pestov and Todorcevic published "Fraïssé limits, Ramsey theory, and topological dynamics of automorphism groups", which turned this into an equivalence for all ordered Fraïssé classes. The search for Ramsey expansions of known Fraïssé classes has been active since.

Hrushovski's other amalgamation method came from geometric stability theory. In "A new strongly minimal set", published in the Annals of Pure and Applied Logic in 1993, he used a predimension and self-sufficient embeddings to build a strongly minimal set that is not locally modular and interprets no infinite field. This refuted Zilber's trichotomy conjecture, and the method has since produced many structures with prescribed geometric behaviour.

## Further reading

- Wilfrid Hodges, *A Shorter Model Theory* (Cambridge University Press, 1997). A textbook source for Fraïssé's construction and for the automorphism groups of countable structures, cited by the entries of the chapter.
- Peter J. Cameron, *Oligomorphic Permutation Groups* (London Mathematical Society Lecture Note Series 152, Cambridge University Press, 1990). The automorphism groups of $\aleph_0$-categorical structures, with many homogeneous examples and counting problems for orbits.
- Dugald Macpherson, "A survey of homogeneous structures", Discrete Mathematics 311 (2011). A wide survey of the classifications, the model theory of Fraïssé limits and their automorphism groups.
- Alexander S. Kechris, Vladimir G. Pestov and Stevo Todorcevic, "Fraïssé limits, Ramsey theory, and topological dynamics of automorphism groups", Geometric and Functional Analysis 15 (2005). The original paper on the correspondence, readable with little background in topological dynamics.
- Jan Hubička and Jaroslav Nešetřil, "All those Ramsey classes". A modern account of the methods for proving that a class has a Ramsey expansion.
