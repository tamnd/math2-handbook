## History

The Löwenheim–Skolem theorem is the oldest theorem of model theory. Leopold Löwenheim proved in 1915 that a satisfiable first-order sentence has a countable model. Thoralf Skolem proved in 1920, with the axiom of choice, that a countable set of sentences true in a structure is true in a countable substructure of it; his proof chose witnesses by what are now called Skolem functions. In 1922 he gave a proof without choice that such a set has a countable model, and he drew from the theorem the consequence for axiomatic set theory now known as Skolem's paradox: if the axioms of set theory have a model, they have a countable one, although they prove that uncountable sets exist.

The upward direction and the general form came next. Anatoly Mal'cev proved the theorem for languages of any size in 1936. He cited a note of Skolem according to which Alfred Tarski had proved the upward theorem in a seminar in 1928, and the general form is sometimes called the Löwenheim–Skolem–Tarski theorem.

The notions of elementary extension and elementary substructure were introduced by Tarski and Robert Vaught in their 1957 paper "Arithmetical extensions of relational systems". The same paper proved the test that bears their names and the elementary chain theorem. Tarski's earlier work on real closed fields had shown in effect that the real algebraic numbers form an elementary substructure of the real field. Abraham Robinson introduced the name "diagram" and was the first to use diagrams systematically, from his book of 1951 onwards; the proofs of the amalgamation theorem and of Robinson's joint consistency theorem are of this kind.

Partial elementary maps grew out of older back-and-forth arguments, such as Cantor's proof that any two countable dense linear orders without endpoints are isomorphic. Roland Fraïssé and Andrzej Ehrenfeucht used systems of partial isomorphisms to characterize elementary equivalence, and the theory of saturated and homogeneous models made partial elementary maps the standard way to build elementary embeddings one element at a time. Per Lindström proved in 1969 that first-order logic is the strongest logic with the compactness property and the downward Löwenheim–Skolem property for countable models, so the theorem of this chapter is one of the two properties that characterize first-order logic.

## Further reading

- David Marker, *Model Theory: An Introduction* (Springer, 2002). Chapter 2 covers elementary embeddings, the Tarski–Vaught test, Skolem functions and the Löwenheim–Skolem theorems with many examples.
- Wilfrid Hodges, *A Shorter Model Theory* (Cambridge University Press, 1997). A careful account of Skolem hulls, diagrams and elementary amalgamation, with the cardinal bounds worked out.
- C. C. Chang and H. Jerome Keisler, *Model Theory* (North-Holland, 1973; third edition 1990). The classical reference for elementary chains, diagrams and the many forms of the Löwenheim–Skolem theorem.
- Katrin Tent and Martin Ziegler, *A Course in Model Theory* (Cambridge University Press, 2012). A short modern treatment that leads from these constructions to saturated models and the monster model.
- Bruno Poizat, *A Course in Model Theory: An Introduction to Contemporary Mathematical Logic* (Springer, 2000). Develops elementary extensions and partial elementary maps from the back-and-forth point of view.
