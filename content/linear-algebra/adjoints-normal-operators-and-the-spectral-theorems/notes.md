## History

The real spectral theorem is older than matrices. It began as the principal axis theorem: a quadric surface, or the inertia of a rigid body, has three perpendicular axes, and Euler found the principal axes of inertia in the eighteenth century. Augustin-Louis Cauchy proved the general case in 1829. He showed that a real symmetric matrix of any size has real eigenvalues, and that a quadratic form in $n$ variables becomes a sum of squares after an orthogonal change of variables. In 1846 Carl Gustav Jacobi gave a method that diagonalizes a symmetric matrix by a sequence of plane rotations, and versions of it are still used.

Charles Hermite introduced the forms with complex coefficients that now carry his name, and in 1855 he showed that their eigenvalues are real, as for real symmetric matrices. Issai Schur proved in 1909 that every complex square matrix is unitarily similar to a triangular one, the result that drives the complex spectral theorem of this chapter. The class of normal matrices, which commute with their adjoints, is the exact setting of that theorem, and the 1987 survey "Normal matrices" by Robert Grone, Charles Johnson, Eduardo Sá and Henry Wolkowicz collects dozens of equivalent conditions.

The Rayleigh quotient comes from physics. Lord Rayleigh used it in *The Theory of Sound* (1877) to estimate the frequencies of vibrating systems, and Walther Ritz later made it a method of computation. Ernst Fischer described every eigenvalue of a quadratic form by a min-max principle in 1905, and Richard Courant used the same principle for differential operators in 1920, so the theorem carries both names.

The word "spectrum" comes from David Hilbert's work on integral equations, from 1904 on, where the spectral theorem moved to infinite dimensions. Around 1929 John von Neumann extended it to unbounded self-adjoint operators for the mathematical foundations of quantum mechanics, in which observables are self-adjoint operators.

## Further reading

- Sheldon Axler, *Linear Algebra Done Right*, fourth edition (2024). Adjoints, self-adjoint and normal operators and both spectral theorems, developed without determinants and in the operator language of this chapter.
- Stephen H. Friedberg, Arnold J. Insel and Lawrence E. Spence, *Linear Algebra*, fifth edition (2019). A careful treatment of adjoints, Schur's theorem, normal operators and spectral decompositions with many worked examples.
- Roger A. Horn and Charles R. Johnson, *Matrix Analysis*, second edition (2013). The standard reference for unitary similarity, normal matrices and the variational description of the eigenvalues of Hermitian matrices, including interlacing.
- Beresford N. Parlett, *The Symmetric Eigenvalue Problem* (1980, reprinted 1998). Rayleigh quotients, min-max theorems and the algorithms that compute eigenvalues of symmetric matrices.
- Paul R. Halmos, *Finite-Dimensional Vector Spaces*, second edition (1958). A short classic that treats adjoints and the spectral theorem with Hilbert space in view.
