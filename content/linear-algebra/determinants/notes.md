## History

Determinants appeared before matrices. Seki Takakazu in Japan used them in 1683 to eliminate unknowns from systems of equations, and Leibniz, in a letter to L'Hôpital of 1693, wrote down the condition for three linear equations in two unknowns to be consistent, with a double-index notation for the coefficients that is close to the modern one. Neither line of work had much influence at the time.

The rule that now carries his name was published by Gabriel Cramer in 1750, in an appendix to his *Introduction à l'analyse des lignes courbes algébriques*, where he needed to find a curve through given points. He described the numerators and the common denominator by a rule for the sign of each product of coefficients, which amounts to the sign of a permutation. In the 1770s Vandermonde treated these expressions as functions in their own right, and Laplace, in 1772, gave the expansion along a row or a set of rows that is now called Laplace expansion.

The word "determinant" comes from Gauss, who in 1801 used it in the *Disquisitiones Arithmeticae* for the discriminant of a quadratic form. Cauchy gave it its present meaning in a long memoir of 1812. There he set out the theory systematically, proved the product rule $\det(AB) = \det A \det B$, and gave the formula for the minors of a product. Binet found the same product formula at the same time, and the general statement is now called the Cauchy-Binet formula. Jacobi wrote a series of papers on determinants in 1841, including the determinant of the partial derivatives of a map, and in the same year Cayley introduced the notation of the array between two vertical bars.

The second half of the nineteenth century added the classical identities. Sylvester proved the identity that bears his name for determinants of bordered minors, and Charles Dodgson, better known as Lewis Carroll, published his condensation method in 1866. Cayley showed in 1849 that the determinant of a skew-symmetric matrix of even size is the square of a polynomial in its entries, and named that polynomial after Pfaff, who had met it in his work on differential equations. Thomas Muir collected the whole history in a work of several volumes, which lists the papers in the order of their dates.

In the twentieth century the emphasis shifted. With matrices and linear maps at the centre of the subject, the determinant became the unique alternating multilinear function of the columns with value $1$ at the identity, and its geometric meaning as a signed volume was made precise through the exterior power. Some modern texts, such as Axler's, postpone determinants and develop eigenvalues without them.

## Further reading

- Thomas Muir, *The Theory of Determinants in the Historical Order of Development*: the standard history, with summaries of the original papers in the order they appeared.
- Kenneth Hoffman and Ray Kunze, *Linear Algebra*: a careful treatment of determinants over commutative rings, with the alternating multilinear definition and the uniqueness proof.
- Roger A. Horn and Charles R. Johnson, *Matrix Analysis*: the reference for Schur complements, the Cauchy-Binet formula and many determinant identities.
- David M. Bressoud, *Proofs and Confirmations: The Story of the Alternating Sign Matrix Conjecture*: Dodgson condensation and how it led to a modern problem in combinatorics.
- Sheldon Axler, *Linear Algebra Done Right*: a course in which eigenvalues are developed first, without determinants, and determinants come at the end.
