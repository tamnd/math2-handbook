## History

Coordinates go back to Descartes and Fermat in the seventeenth century, but the idea of a space of vectors with its own algebra is younger. In 1844 Hermann Grassmann published *Die lineale Ausdehnungslehre*, in which he formed linear combinations of abstract "extensive quantities", defined linear dependence, worked with the spans of finitely many quantities and their joins and meets, and counted the independent units of a system. He published a reworked version in 1862. His contemporaries found the book hard to read, and it had little influence for decades; the dimension formula for sums of subspaces now carries his name.

Giuseppe Peano read Grassmann carefully. In his *Calcolo geometrico secondo l'Ausdehnungslehre di H. Grassmann* of 1888 he gave an axiomatic definition of a real vector space, close to the one used today, and his axioms allowed infinite-dimensional spaces such as spaces of functions. In the 1830s William Rowan Hamilton had defined the complex numbers as pairs of real numbers, the construction that complexification applies to a whole real vector space.

The exchange argument behind the invariance of dimension is named after Ernst Steinitz, who used it in 1913. Hassler Whitney took the exchange property as an axiom in his 1935 paper "On the abstract properties of linear dependence", which began the theory of matroids. The abstract theory of vector spaces over arbitrary fields became standard in algebra in the first half of the twentieth century.

Infinite dimension raised new questions. Georg Hamel used a well-ordering of $\R$ in 1905 to build a basis of $\R$ over $\Q$, and used it to find discontinuous solutions of the functional equation $f(x + y) = f(x) + f(y)$; such bases are now called Hamel bases. The modern proof that every vector space has a basis uses Zorn's lemma. Andreas Blass showed in 1984 that the converse holds: if every vector space has a basis, then the axiom of choice is true.

## Further reading

- Sheldon Axler, *Linear Algebra Done Right*, fourth edition (2024). Chapters 1 and 2 develop vector spaces, span, independence, bases and dimension in finite dimension with complete proofs, avoiding determinants.
- Stephen H. Friedberg, Arnold J. Insel and Lawrence E. Spence, *Linear Algebra*, fifth edition (2019). Chapter 1 covers the same ground with many examples, and includes the proof by Zorn's lemma that every vector space has a basis.
- Kenneth Hoffman and Ray Kunze, *Linear Algebra*, second edition (1971). Chapter 2 treats vector spaces, subspaces, bases and coordinates, closely tied to row reduction.
- Paul R. Halmos, *Finite-Dimensional Vector Spaces*, second edition (1958). A short classic that treats finite-dimensional spaces with the methods that also work in infinite dimension.
- Steven Roman, *Advanced Linear Algebra*, third edition (2008). Chapter 1 handles bases and dimension in arbitrary dimension, with the cardinal arithmetic that the infinite case needs.
