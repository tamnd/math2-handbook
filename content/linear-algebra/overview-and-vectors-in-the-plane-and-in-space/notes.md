## History

Coordinates came first. Descartes and Fermat introduced them into geometry in the 17th century, and with coordinates the Pythagorean theorem became the formula for the length of a segment. Newton stated the parallelogram rule for forces in the *Principia* of 1687, and mechanics has split forces into components ever since. Lagrange used what are now the dot and cross products, written out in coordinates, in a study of the volume of a tetrahedron in 1773.

The word "vector" is Hamilton's. In 1843 he found the quaternions, numbers of the form $a + b\mathbf{i} + c\mathbf{j} + d\mathbf{k}$, and in the 1840s he called the part $b\mathbf{i} + c\mathbf{j} + d\mathbf{k}$ a vector. For two such pure quaternions, the real part of the product is minus the dot product and the vector part is the cross product, so both products were present from the start. In 1844 Grassmann published a general theory of quantities with direction in any number of dimensions, which was read by few at the time.

Gibbs and Heaviside separated the vector algebra from the quaternions in the 1880s. Gibbs made the dot and cross products separate operations and introduced the notations $u \cdot v$ and $a \times b$, and the scalar triple product and the vector equations of lines and planes took their present form in this vector analysis. Their notation is the one that physics uses today. Peano gave the axioms of an abstract vector space in 1888, and Cauchy (for sums, 1821) and Schwarz (for integrals, 1888) proved the inequality that makes the angle between two vectors meaningful in every dimension. Much later, Massey showed in 1983 that a bilinear product with the perpendicularity and length properties of the cross product exists only in dimensions $3$ and $7$, apart from trivial cases.

The essays at the start of the chapter come from more recent work. The Linear Algebra Curriculum Study Group met in 1990, and its recommendations, published in 1993, moved many American first courses towards matrices and computation. Axler's article "Down with determinants!" in the *American Mathematical Monthly* (1995) and his book *Linear Algebra Done Right* made the determinant-free route to eigenvalues popular in proof-based courses.

## Further reading

- Gilbert Strang, *Introduction to Linear Algebra*, 6th edition (2023). It starts with vectors, lengths and dot products in $\R^n$ and moves to matrices at once, with many pictures.
- David C. Lay, Steven R. Lay and Judi J. McDonald, *Linear Algebra and Its Applications*, 5th edition. A matrix-first course with careful definitions and many computed examples.
- Sheldon Axler, *Linear Algebra Done Right*, 4th edition (2024). The abstract-first course described in the essay on linear algebra without determinants.
- Jim Hefferon, *Linear Algebra*, a free textbook. It starts with linear systems and proves the results from the beginning, with a short section on the geometry of vectors.
- Michael J. Crowe, *A History of Vector Analysis* (1967). The story of quaternions, Grassmann's theory and the vector analysis of Gibbs and Heaviside.
