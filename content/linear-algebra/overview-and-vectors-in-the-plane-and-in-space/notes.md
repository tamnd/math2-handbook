## History

Coordinates came first and vectors much later. The idea of computing with directed quantities grew out of two separate inventions in the 1840s. William Rowan Hamilton found the quaternions in 1843, numbers of the form $a + bi + cj + dk$ with $i^2 = j^2 = k^2 = ijk = -1$. The product of two quaternions with $a = 0$ contains, as its two parts, the negative of the dot product and the cross product of the corresponding vectors of space. In 1844 Hermann Grassmann published *Die lineale Ausdehnungslehre*, a theory of extension in any number of dimensions that already contained linear combinations, linear independence and the exterior product, though it found few readers in his lifetime.

The vector algebra of this chapter, with separate dot and cross products and no quaternions, was shaped in the 1880s by Josiah Willard Gibbs and, independently, by Oliver Heaviside, both working for physics and electromagnetism. Gibbs's lecture notes became the book *Vector Analysis* by Edwin Bidwell Wilson in 1901, which fixed much of the notation still in use. Michael J. Crowe's *A History of Vector Analysis* (1967) tells the story in detail.

Several results of the chapter are older than the vectors. Lagrange, in 1773, computed the area of triangles and the volume of tetrahedra from the coordinates of their vertices, with expressions that are the components of a cross product and a scalar triple product. Cauchy proved the inequality $\left(\sum a_i b_i\right)^2 \le \sum a_i^2 \sum b_i^2$ for finite sums in his *Cours d'analyse* of 1821, and Hermann Amandus Schwarz proved the version for integrals in 1888. The fact that a product of vectors with the properties of the cross product exists only in dimensions $3$ and $7$ was given a short proof by W. S. Massey in "Cross products of vectors in higher dimensional Euclidean spaces", *American Mathematical Monthly* 90 (1983).

The essays at the start of the chapter rest on two later documents. The Linear Algebra Curriculum Study Group, formed in 1990, published its recommendations for a first course in the *College Mathematics Journal* in 1993, and it asked for a first course built around matrices. Sheldon Axler's article "Down with determinants!", *American Mathematical Monthly* 102 (1995), gave the determinant-free route to eigenvalues that grew into his textbook.

## Further reading

- Gilbert Strang, *Introduction to Linear Algebra*. Starts from vectors, lengths and dot products exactly as this chapter does, with many pictures and computed examples.
- David C. Lay, Steven R. Lay and Judi J. McDonald, *Linear Algebra and Its Applications*. A matrix-first course of the kind the essay on teaching describes, with careful worked examples.
- Tom M. Apostol, *Calculus, Volume 1*. Its chapters on vector algebra and its applications to analytic geometry treat lines, planes, the cross product and the triple product with full proofs.
- Jerrold E. Marsden and Anthony Tromba, *Vector Calculus*. The first chapter covers the geometry of Euclidean space, including the cross product, distances and the Cauchy-Schwarz inequality, with an eye to physics.
- Sheldon Axler, *Linear Algebra Done Right*. The book-length version of the determinant-free approach to eigenvalues that one of the essays describes.
- Michael J. Crowe, *A History of Vector Analysis: The Evolution of the Idea of a Vectorial System*. The standard history of how quaternions and Grassmann's algebra turned into the vector algebra of Gibbs and Heaviside.
