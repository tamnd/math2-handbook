The curve $xy = 1$ is a hyperbola, but its equation hides the fact. Rotate the axes by $45^\circ$, putting $x = (u - v)/\sqrt 2$ and $y = (u + v)/\sqrt 2$, and the equation becomes $u^2 - v^2 = 2$, the normal form of a hyperbola with axes along the diagonals. The left side $xy$ is a quadratic form, and the rotation has turned it into $\frac12 u^2 - \frac12 v^2$, a sum of squares with one positive and one negative coefficient. The same question, how simple a quadratic form becomes after a change of variables, runs through geometry (conics and quadric surfaces), analysis (the second derivative test, where the Hessian is a quadratic form), physics (kinetic energy, the Minkowski form of special relativity) and number theory (which numbers are sums of two squares).

The answer depends on what changes of variables are allowed and on the field. Over any field of characteristic not $2$, an invertible linear change makes every form diagonal. Over $\R$, Sylvester's law of inertia says that the numbers of positive, negative and zero coefficients are then fixed, so $xy$ and $u^2 - v^2$ are equivalent but neither is equivalent to $u^2 + v^2$. Over $\C$ only the rank survives, and over a finite field of odd order the rank and the discriminant of the nondegenerate part. If only rotations are allowed, the coefficients themselves are fixed: they are the eigenvalues of the symmetric matrix of the form, by the principal axis theorem.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in five groups, read in order.

1. **Quadratic forms and polar forms.** The [quadratic form](/0RBQS1JJCR/) and its [polar form](/0HYFWKKY3C/), which links it to symmetric bilinear forms, with the special behaviour of characteristic $2$.
2. **Diagonalization and the law of inertia.** The [diagonalization](/0Z3FEWZ8HA/) of a form by completing squares, [Sylvester's law of inertia](/0WFED3K3DQ/), and the [signature](/07EZA80NEH/) $(p, q, r)$.
