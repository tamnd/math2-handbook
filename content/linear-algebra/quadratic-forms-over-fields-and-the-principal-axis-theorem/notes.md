## History

Quadratic forms entered mathematics through number theory. Fermat asked which primes are sums of two squares, and Lagrange proved that every positive integer is a sum of four squares. Gauss made binary forms $ax^2 + bxy + cy^2$ with integer coefficients the main subject of the *Disquisitiones Arithmeticae* (1801). He defined the equivalence of forms under integer changes of variables, the discriminant, and the composition of forms. The theory over fields in this chapter is the simpler part of that subject, where any invertible change of variables is allowed.

Completing the square is the oldest method of diagonalization. Lagrange used it to decide when a function of several variables has a maximum or a minimum, and the method of this chapter still carries his name in many texts. The principal axis theorem has its roots in mechanics and geometry: Euler found the principal axes of a rotating body, and Cauchy proved in 1829 that the roots of the characteristic equation of a real symmetric matrix are real. The name "principal axes" comes from the axes of conics and quadric surfaces, which the theorem finds.

Sylvester published the law of inertia in 1852 and gave it its name. Jacobi had found the same result independently, and the law is sometimes attributed to both. Sylvester also introduced much of the language of the subject, including the words "discriminant" and "matrix".

The study of forms over arbitrary fields began in the twentieth century. Minkowski and then Hasse found the local-global principle for forms over $\Q$: a form represents zero over $\Q$ exactly when it does so over $\R$ and over every $p$-adic field. Ernst Witt's paper of 1937 set up the algebraic theory for any field of characteristic not $2$, with the cancellation theorem, the decomposition into hyperbolic planes and an anisotropic part, and the group now called the Witt ring. The fact that every form in at least three variables over a finite field has an isotropic vector is a case of the Chevalley-Warning theorem from the 1930s.

## Further reading

- T. Y. Lam, *Introduction to Quadratic Forms over Fields* (2005). The standard modern account of the algebraic theory, from diagonalization to the Witt ring and beyond.
- Jean-Pierre Serre, *A Course in Arithmetic* (1973). Its chapters on forms over $\mathbb F_q$, $\Q_p$ and $\Q$ give a short and complete proof of the Hasse-Minkowski theorem.
- J. W. S. Cassels, *Rational Quadratic Forms* (1978). A thorough treatment of forms over the rationals and the integers.
- Winfried Scharlau, *Quadratic and Hermitian Forms* (1985). A wide survey of the theory over general fields, with Hermitian forms treated alongside quadratic ones.
- David C. Lay, Steven R. Lay and Judi J. McDonald, *Linear Algebra and Its Applications*, 5th ed. (2016). Its section on quadratic forms covers the principal axis theorem, constrained extremes and conics with many worked examples.
