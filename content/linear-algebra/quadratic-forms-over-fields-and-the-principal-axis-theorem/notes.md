## History

Quadratic forms began in number theory. Fermat stated which primes are sums of two squares, and Lagrange proved in 1770 that every positive integer is a sum of four squares. Lagrange also studied binary forms $ax^2 + bxy + cy^2$ with integer coefficients and the changes of variables that preserve them. Gauss's *Disquisitiones Arithmeticae* of 1801 turned this into a theory of equivalence and reduction of binary forms. Gauss wrote a form as $ax^2 + 2bxy + cy^2$ and called $b^2 - ac$ its determinant, which is $-\det A$ in the language of this chapter. Forms over the integers and over number fields still belong to number theory.

The real theory grew out of geometry and mechanics. The principal axes of a conic, of a quadric surface and of a rotating rigid body all come from the same symmetric matrix. Cauchy proved in 1829 that the roots of the characteristic equation of a real symmetric matrix are real. Jacobi showed that every real quadratic form can be brought to a diagonal form by an orthogonal change of variables, which is the [principal axis theorem](/0PR5P9Z4KM/). Sylvester published a proof of the [law of inertia](/0WFED3K3DQ/) in 1852 and gave it its name. The [extreme values on unit vectors](/YS4HT1EYE5/) lead to the Rayleigh quotient, named after Lord Rayleigh, and to the min–max theorem of Ernst Fischer and Richard Courant.

The study of forms over an arbitrary field came later. Ernst Witt's paper *Theorie der quadratischen Formen in beliebigen Körpern* of 1937 proved the cancellation theorem and introduced the ring of classes of forms now named after him. Claude Chevalley and Ewald Warning proved in 1935 their theorems on zeros of polynomials over finite fields, answering a question of Emil Artin. The case of degree $2$ is the fact that every form in three or more variables over a [finite field](/2HBR7137AD/) is isotropic. For the rationals, the local–global principle of Hermann Minkowski and Helmut Hasse reduces equivalence over $\Q$ to equivalence over $\R$ and over every field of $p$-adic numbers. In characteristic $2$, where the polar form is alternating and the methods of this chapter fail, Cahit Arf found in 1941 the invariant now called the Arf invariant.

## Further reading

- T. Y. Lam, *Introduction to Quadratic Forms over Fields*. The standard text on forms over general fields: diagonalization, Witt's theorems, the Witt ring and the classification over finite, local and global fields.
- Jean-Pierre Serre, *A Course in Arithmetic*. Its first part classifies forms over finite fields, over the $p$-adic numbers and over $\Q$, ending with the Hasse–Minkowski theorem.
- Steven Roman, *Advanced Linear Algebra*. Its chapters on metric vector spaces treat symmetric bilinear and quadratic forms over general fields, orthogonal bases and Witt's theorems at the level of this chapter.
- Stephen H. Friedberg, Arnold J. Insel and Lawrence E. Spence, *Linear Algebra*. A careful treatment of bilinear and quadratic forms with Sylvester's law of inertia and the second derivative test.
- David C. Lay, Steven R. Lay and Judi J. McDonald, *Linear Algebra and Its Applications*. Clear worked examples of the principal axis theorem, conics and constrained optimization on the unit sphere.
- Roger A. Horn and Charles R. Johnson, *Matrix Analysis*. The reference for Rayleigh quotients, the Courant–Fischer theorem and the inertia of Hermitian matrices.
