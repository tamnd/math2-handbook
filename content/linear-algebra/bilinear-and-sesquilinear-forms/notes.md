## History

Forms entered mathematics through number theory. In the *Disquisitiones Arithmeticae* of 1801, Gauss studied binary quadratic forms $a x^2 + 2bxy + c y^2$ with integer coefficients and called two forms equivalent when an invertible integer substitution of the variables turns one into the other. In matrix language this is the congruence rule $P^{\mathsf{T}} A P$ of this chapter, with $P$ an integer matrix of determinant $\pm 1$. Over the real numbers, Sylvester published his law of inertia in 1852: the numbers of positive and negative squares in a diagonal form do not depend on the substitution used to reach it.

Complex forms with conjugated variables were studied by Charles Hermite, and the Hermitian matrices and forms carry his name. Bilinear forms in two separate sets of variables became a subject of their own in the 1870s. Frobenius wrote a long paper of 1878 on linear substitutions and bilinear forms, which treated the transformation of a bilinear form by substitutions in its two sets of variables and helped to build the matrix calculus that the chapter uses throughout.

Alternating forms come from geometry and mechanics. The Pfaffian, the square root of the determinant of an alternating matrix, is named after Johann Friedrich Pfaff, and Darboux's theorem in differential geometry is the non-linear form of the existence of a symplectic basis. The word "symplectic" is due to Hermann Weyl, who used it in his book *The Classical Groups* of 1939 for the group of a nondegenerate alternating form. He built the word from Greek as a counterpart to "complex", the older name of the group, which came from line complexes.

The modern algebraic theory of symmetric forms over an arbitrary field begins with Ernst Witt. His paper "Theorie der quadratischen Formen in beliebigen Körpern" of 1937 proved the cancellation theorem and the extension theorem that bear his name, gave the decomposition into hyperbolic planes and an anisotropic part, and introduced the ring of classes of forms now called the Witt ring. Emil Artin's book *Geometric Algebra* of 1957 put symmetric, alternating and Hermitian forms and their groups into one framework, and that is the framework of this chapter.

## Further reading

- Steven Roman, *Advanced Linear Algebra*, Graduate Texts in Mathematics 135. Its chapters on metric vector spaces cover bilinear and sesquilinear forms, orthogonality, symplectic geometry and Witt's theorem at the level of this chapter.
- Emil Artin, *Geometric Algebra*. A short classic that treats symplectic, orthogonal and unitary geometry side by side and gives a clean proof of Witt's theorem.
- Larry C. Grove, *Classical Groups and Geometric Algebra*. Goes from forms to the groups that preserve them, with careful attention to characteristic $2$.
- T. Y. Lam, *Introduction to Quadratic Forms over Fields*. The standard text for what comes after the Witt decomposition: the Witt ring and the classification of forms over particular fields.
- Nathan Jacobson, *Basic Algebra I*. Its chapter on bilinear forms gives a complete and readable account of the basic theory, including alternating forms and the Witt theorems.
- Irving Kaplansky, *Linear Algebra and Geometry: A Second Course*. A short book that does bilinear and Hermitian forms carefully over general fields.
