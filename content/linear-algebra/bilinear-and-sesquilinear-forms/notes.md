## History

Bilinear and quadratic forms were studied long before vector spaces were defined. Number theorists of the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries asked which integers a form such as $x^2 + y^2$ represents and when two forms are equivalent by an integer change of variables, and the rule $A \mapsto P^T A P$ is the matrix version of such a change. James Joseph Sylvester published his law of inertia in 1852: a real symmetric matrix is congruent to exactly one diagonal matrix with entries $1$, $-1$ and $0$ in a fixed order. This is the first classification of forms up to congruence, and it is proved in the next chapter.

Charles Hermite introduced the forms and matrices that carry his name. In 1855 he showed that Hermitian matrices share a basic property of real symmetric ones: their eigenvalues are real. The word "sesquilinear" comes from the Latin "sesqui", one and a half, since such a form is linear in one slot and only half linear in the other. Physicists usually make the first slot conjugate-linear, following the bra-ket notation of Paul Dirac; this book makes the second slot conjugate-linear.

Alternating forms entered through geometry and mechanics. Hermann Weyl introduced the name "symplectic group" for the group of a nondegenerate alternating form, as a Greek replacement for older names such as "line complex group". The phase space of classical mechanics, with positions and momenta paired by the form $\sum (x_i y_{m+i} - x_{m+i} y_i)$, is the basic symplectic vector space.

The theory over an arbitrary field is due largely to Ernst Witt, whose 1937 paper on quadratic forms over arbitrary fields introduced the decomposition into hyperbolic planes and an anisotropic part, the cancellation theorem, and the group now called the Witt group. In 1936 Garrett Birkhoff and John von Neumann related the correlations of a projective geometry to nondegenerate sesquilinear forms, which explains why reflexive forms are the natural ones; Reinhold Baer later extended sesquilinear forms to vector spaces over division rings.

## Further reading

- Steven Roman, *Advanced Linear Algebra*, third edition (2008). Its chapters on metric vector spaces cover symmetric, alternating and Hermitian forms, symplectic bases, the Witt decomposition and Witt's theorem, at the level of this chapter.
- Emil Artin, *Geometric Algebra* (1957). The classical account of symplectic and orthogonal geometry over arbitrary fields, with the groups that preserve the forms.
- Larry C. Grove, *Classical Groups and Geometric Algebra* (2002). A modern short course on forms, Witt's theorem and the symplectic and orthogonal groups.
- T. Y. Lam, *Introduction to Quadratic Forms over Fields* (2005). The standard graduate text on Witt's theory of symmetric forms, the Witt group and the classification over many fields.
- Nathan Jacobson, *Basic Algebra I*, second edition (1985). Its chapter on metric vector spaces treats bilinear forms, Witt's theorem and the classical groups within a general algebra course.
