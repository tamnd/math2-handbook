## History

Eigenvalues were found in problems before they had a name. The roots of a polynomial equation built from a symmetric array of coefficients appeared in the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries in mechanics and astronomy, wherever a quadratic form had to be brought to principal axes or a system of linear differential equations had to be solved. Augustin-Louis Cauchy called these roots "racines caractéristiques", characteristic roots, and the name survives in the [characteristic polynomial](/0VSSPAFM2C/). The English word "eigenvalue" is half German: *eigen* means "own", and an eigenvalue is a value that belongs to the map itself. The relations between the coefficients of a polynomial and the elementary symmetric functions of its roots, which give the [trace](/0NMXDCNNKR/) and the [determinant](/0C2F40MEBC/) as the sum and the product of the eigenvalues, go back to François Viète.

The [Cayley–Hamilton theorem](/0F765KFC2J/) has two names for two different reasons. William Rowan Hamilton found a form of it for linear functions of quaternions, before matrices were studied as objects of their own. Arthur Cayley's *A Memoir on the Theory of Matrices* of 1858, one of the first papers to treat matrices as an algebra, stated the theorem for square matrices of every size. Cayley proved it only for $2 \times 2$ matrices and reported that he had checked the $3 \times 3$ case; he saw no need for a general proof. Ferdinand Georg Frobenius gave the first general proof in 1878.

The approach of this chapter, which starts from eigenvalues and invariant subspaces and treats determinants as a tool rather than the foundation, has a modern advocate in Sheldon Axler. His article "Down with Determinants!" in the American Mathematical Monthly gave the proof of [existence of an eigenvalue](/08CJB2NQ92/) that uses only the fact that $n + 1$ vectors in an $n$-dimensional space are dependent and that a complex polynomial factors into linear factors. His textbook *Linear Algebra Done Right* develops the whole subject in this order. The other route, through the [adjugate](/07W9H8QF38/) and the identity $(tI - A) \operatorname{adj}(tI - A) = p_A(t) I$, is the one that proves Cayley–Hamilton over every commutative ring.

## Further reading

- Sheldon Axler, *Linear Algebra Done Right*. Eigenvalues, invariant subspaces and the minimal polynomial without determinants; the clearest account of why every complex operator has an eigenvalue.
- Kenneth Hoffman and Ray Kunze, *Linear Algebra*. Characteristic and minimal polynomials, annihilating ideals and the Cayley–Hamilton theorem, treated carefully over a general field.
- Paul R. Halmos, *Finite-Dimensional Vector Spaces*. A short, coordinate-free treatment of proper values, multiplicities and polynomials in a linear transformation.
- Gilbert Strang, *Introduction to Linear Algebra*. Computing eigenvalues and eigenvectors by hand, with many applications to differential equations and Markov matrices.
- Roger A. Horn and Charles R. Johnson, *Matrix Analysis*. The reference for the spectral facts used later: $AB$ and $BA$, left and right eigenvectors, and the location of eigenvalues.
