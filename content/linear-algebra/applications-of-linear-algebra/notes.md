## History

Many of these applications are older than matrices. The interpolation formula now named after Lagrange was published by Edward Waring in 1779 and by Lagrange in 1795. Legendre published the method of least squares in 1805 to fit the orbits of comets, and Gauss, who had used the method before, gave it a probabilistic justification in 1809. Binet published his formula for the Fibonacci numbers in 1843, though de Moivre had found it more than a century earlier. Gustav Kirchhoff stated his laws for currents and voltages in 1845, and in 1847 he solved the resulting linear system and found the count of spanning trees that is now called the matrix-tree theorem. Fourier's *Théorie analytique de la chaleur* (1822) expanded functions in sines and cosines, the continuous ancestor of the discrete Fourier basis.

The models of evolution came in the twentieth century. Andrei Markov began the study of the chains named after him in 1906. P. H. Leslie introduced his age-structured population model in 1945, and the dominant eigenvalue of the Leslie matrix became the standard measure of the growth rate of a population. Wassily Leontief built the input-output model of the economy of the United States in the 1930s and 1940s, and received the Nobel Prize in economics for it in 1973. Eckart and Young proved in 1936 that the truncated singular value decomposition gives the best approximation of lower rank in the Frobenius norm, and Mirsky extended the result to all unitarily invariant norms in 1960.

Computers made these models practical and added new ones. Cooley and Tukey published the fast Fourier transform in 1965, which applies the Fourier matrix of size $n$ in $O(n \log n)$ operations instead of $O(n^2)$. Alfréd Haar had introduced his orthonormal system of step functions in 1910, and it became the first example of a wavelet basis. The JPEG standard for still images, based on the discrete cosine transform on $8 \times 8$ blocks, was published in 1992. Computer graphics took homogeneous coordinates from projective geometry, where Möbius and Plücker had introduced them in the nineteenth century. Sergey Brin and Larry Page described PageRank in 1998 as the ranking method of their search engine Google. The puzzle Lights Out was sold by Tiger Electronics from 1995, and its analysis over $\mathbb F_2$ soon became a standard classroom example.

## Further reading

- David C. Lay, Steven R. Lay and Judi J. McDonald, *Linear Algebra and Its Applications*, 5th ed. (2016). Each chapter closes with applications, among them most of the models of this chapter.
- Stephen Boyd and Lieven Vandenberghe, *Introduction to Applied Linear Algebra: Vectors, Matrices, and Least Squares* (2018). A first course built around applications, with least squares at its centre.
- Gilbert Strang, *Linear Algebra and Learning from Data* (2019). The SVD, low-rank approximation and the matrices of graphs as tools for data science.
- Amy N. Langville and Carl D. Meyer, *Google's PageRank and Beyond: The Science of Search Engine Rankings* (2006). The mathematics of PageRank, from stochastic matrices to computing the ranking at scale.
- Gilbert Strang and Kai Borre, *Linear Algebra, Geodesy, and GPS* (1997). Least squares and networks in the setting of surveying and satellite positioning.
