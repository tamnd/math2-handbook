## History

Many of these applications are older than the theory of matrices. Legendre published the method of least squares in 1805 to fit the orbits of comets, and Gauss, who had used the method earlier, gave its probabilistic justification in 1809. Gustav Kirchhoff stated his current and voltage laws in 1845, and in 1847 he solved the resulting linear system for a network and found the count of spanning trees that is now the matrix-tree theorem. Fourier's *Théorie analytique de la chaleur* (1822) expanded functions in sines and cosines, the continuous ancestor of the Fourier basis used in signal processing.

Andrei Markov began the study of the chains named after him in 1906, with dependent trials whose next state depends only on the present one. The use of matrix powers for populations came later: P. H. Leslie introduced his age-structured model in 1945, and the dominant eigenvalue of the Leslie matrix became the standard measure of the growth rate of a population. Wassily Leontief built the input-output model of the United States economy in the 1930s and 1940s, and received the Nobel Prize in economics in 1973 for it.

The computer made these models practical and added new ones. Cooley and Tukey published the fast Fourier transform in 1965, which computes the change to the Fourier basis in $O(n \log n)$ operations instead of $O(n^2)$. Alfréd Haar had introduced his orthonormal system of step functions in 1910, and it became the first example of a wavelet basis. The JPEG standard for images, based on the discrete cosine transform on $8 \times 8$ blocks, was published in 1992. Computer graphics adopted homogeneous coordinates from projective geometry, where Möbius and Plücker had introduced them in the nineteenth century.

Sergey Brin and Larry Page described PageRank in 1998 as the ranking method of their search engine Google. The ranking is the steady state of a Markov chain on the web, and it is computed by power iteration on a matrix with billions of rows and columns. The puzzle Lights Out was sold by Tiger Electronics from 1995, and its solution by linear algebra over $\mathbb F_2$ is now a common classroom example.

## Further reading

- David C. Lay, Steven R. Lay and Judi J. McDonald, *Linear Algebra and Its Applications*, 5th ed. (2016). Each chapter ends with applications, including most of the models in this chapter.
- Stephen Boyd and Lieven Vandenberghe, *Introduction to Applied Linear Algebra: Vectors, Matrices, and Least Squares* (2018). A first course built around applications, with least squares at its centre.
- Gilbert Strang, *Linear Algebra and Learning from Data* (2019). The SVD, low-rank approximation and graph matrices as tools for data science.
- Amy N. Langville and Carl D. Meyer, *Google's PageRank and Beyond* (2006). The mathematics of PageRank, from stochastic matrices to the computation of the ranking at scale.
- Gilbert Strang and Kai Borre, *Linear Algebra, Geodesy, and GPS* (1997). Least squares, networks and Kalman filtering in the setting of positioning and surveying.
