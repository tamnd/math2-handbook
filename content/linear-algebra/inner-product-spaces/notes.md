## History

The inequality $\left(\sum a_i b_i\right)^2 \le \sum a_i^2 \sum b_i^2$ for real numbers is in Cauchy's *Cours d'analyse* of 1821. Bunyakovsky proved the version for integrals in 1859, and Schwarz found it again for double integrals in the 1880s, which is why the inequality carries two or three names depending on the country.

Orthogonal expansions came from analysis before they came from geometry. Fourier expanded functions in sines and cosines at the start of the nineteenth century, and Parseval stated an identity for trigonometric series around 1800 of the kind that now bears his name. Bessel's inequality is named after Friedrich Wilhelm Bessel, who met it in the study of such expansions.

Jørgen Pedersen Gram used the process of successive orthogonalization in an 1883 paper on expansions and least squares, and Erhard Schmidt stated it in its modern form for functions in 1907, in his work on integral equations. A form of the same computation appears earlier in Laplace. The Gram determinant is also named after Gram.

In 1907 Frigyes Riesz and Maurice Fréchet showed, independently, that the bounded linear functionals on the space of square-integrable functions are exactly the maps $f \mapsto \int f g$. The finite-dimensional statement of this chapter is the easy case of their theorem. Hilbert's work on integral equations in the first decade of the twentieth century led to the spaces now named after him, and von Neumann gave the abstract axioms of a Hilbert space in the late 1920s.

Which norms come from an inner product was settled by Pascual Jordan and John von Neumann in 1935: a norm comes from an inner product exactly when it satisfies the parallelogram law, and then the polarization identity gives the inner product.

## Further reading

- Sheldon Axler, *Linear Algebra Done Right*. Its chapter on inner product spaces covers the same ground with the same convention, linear in the first slot, and goes on to orthogonal complements and minimization problems.
- Paul R. Halmos, *Finite-Dimensional Vector Spaces*. A compact treatment that already thinks of the finite-dimensional theory as the model for Hilbert space.
- Kenneth Hoffman and Ray Kunze, *Linear Algebra*. A careful chapter on inner product spaces, with many examples of inner products on spaces of matrices and functions.
- Gilbert Strang, *Introduction to Linear Algebra*. The computational side: orthogonal matrices, Gram-Schmidt written as the factorization $A = QR$, and least squares.
- Roger A. Horn and Charles R. Johnson, *Matrix Analysis*. For norms on vector spaces in general and for Gram matrices as the positive semidefinite matrices.
