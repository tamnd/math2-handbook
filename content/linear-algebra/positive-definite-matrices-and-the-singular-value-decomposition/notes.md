## History

Positivity of quadratic forms is older than matrices. Gauss, in the *Disquisitiones Arithmeticae* of 1801, sorted binary quadratic forms into definite and indefinite ones, and Cauchy proved in 1829 that the eigenvalues of a real symmetric matrix are real, which is the base of the eigenvalue test. The determinant test by leading minors carries the name of James Joseph Sylvester, who worked on quadratic forms and their invariants for most of his life. The Gram matrix is named after the Danish mathematician Jørgen Pedersen Gram, who studied such matrices of inner products in connection with least squares and orthogonalization.

The factorization $A = L L^{\mathsf{T}}$ is due to André-Louis Cholesky, a French artillery officer and surveyor, who used it to solve the normal equations of least-squares problems in geodesy. He died in the First World War, and the method was published after his death, in 1924, by a fellow officer. It is still the standard way to solve a linear system with a positive definite matrix.

The singular value decomposition was found for real square matrices by Eugenio Beltrami in 1873 and independently by Camille Jordan in 1874, both in the language of bilinear forms, and Sylvester found it again in 1889. Léon Autonne studied the polar decomposition of matrices in 1902 and derived the decomposition from it in 1915. In analysis, Erhard Schmidt introduced the singular values of integral operators in 1907 and solved the problem of best approximation by operators of finite rank. Carl Eckart and Gale Young proved the matrix form of that result in 1936, in "The approximation of one matrix by another of lower rank", published in the journal Psychometrika; Leon Mirsky later extended it to all unitarily invariant norms.

The generalized inverse has a parallel history. Ivar Fredholm used a pseudoinverse of integral operators in 1903. E. H. Moore described the matrix version in 1920, Arne Bjerhammar found it again in 1951, and Roger Penrose characterized it in 1955 by the four conditions that now carry his name, in "A generalized inverse for matrices". The SVD became a working tool after Gene Golub and William Kahan published a stable method to compute it in 1965, "Calculating the singular values and pseudo-inverse of a matrix". It is now a routine step in statistics, signal processing and numerical computation.

## Further reading

- Sheldon Axler, *Linear Algebra Done Right*, fourth edition. Treats positive operators, square roots, the polar decomposition and the SVD as statements about operators on inner product spaces, with short proofs.
- Roger A. Horn and Charles R. Johnson, *Matrix Analysis*, second edition. The standard reference for positive definite matrices, the SVD, the polar decomposition and the inequalities among them.
- Rajendra Bhatia, *Positive Definite Matrices*. A graduate monograph on positive definite matrices, their means, and the matrix inequalities they satisfy.
- Lloyd N. Trefethen and David Bau III, *Numerical Linear Algebra*. Starts with the SVD and explains Cholesky, least squares and the pseudoinverse from the point of view of computation and stability.
- Gene H. Golub and Charles F. Van Loan, *Matrix Computations*, fourth edition. The reference for algorithms: the Cholesky factorization, the computation of the SVD and the generalized symmetric eigenvalue problem.
