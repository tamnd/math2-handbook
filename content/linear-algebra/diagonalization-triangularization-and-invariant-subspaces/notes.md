## History

Diagonal forms first appeared through quadratic forms and mechanics, where Euler and Lagrange reduced problems to principal axes. Cauchy proved in 1829 that the characteristic roots of a real symmetric system are real. For symmetric systems a diagonal form always exists, so the question of which maps can be diagonalized did not arise there.

It arose with general linear substitutions. Weierstrass classified pairs of bilinear forms by their elementary divisors in 1868. Camille Jordan, in his *Traité des substitutions et des équations algébriques* of 1870, reduced a linear substitution to blocks with a single eigenvalue each. Both saw that a matrix need not be similar to a diagonal one, and that what blocks it is the part of each block off the diagonal, the nilpotent part in present terms. In the same year Benjamin Peirce introduced the words "nilpotent" and "idempotent" in his *Linear Associative Algebra*. Frobenius introduced the minimal polynomial in 1878. Issai Schur proved in 1909 that every complex matrix is similar to an upper triangular one by a unitary change of basis.

The abstract view came in the twentieth century. A linear map $T$ makes $V$ a module over $F[t]$, the invariant subspaces are the submodules, and the kernels lemma and the primary decomposition become the Chinese remainder theorem and the splitting of a torsion module into primary parts. In this form the decompositions of the chapter hold over every field. Hans Fitting proved the decomposition that carries his name for modules of finite length in the 1930s. Claude Chevalley made systematic use of the splitting of a map into commuting semisimple and nilpotent parts in his work on Lie algebras and algebraic groups, and the theorem now carries his name beside Jordan's.

Textbooks later split into two routes. One route, followed by Hoffman and Kunze, derives the decompositions from polynomials in $T$ and the minimal polynomial. The other, set out in Sheldon Axler's article "Down with Determinants!" of 1995 and in his book, proves the existence of eigenvalues, triangularization and the generalized eigenspace decomposition without determinants and defines the characteristic polynomial afterwards.

## Further reading

- Sheldon Axler, *Linear Algebra Done Right*. Invariant subspaces, triangularization, generalized eigenspaces and nilpotent maps, all without determinants.
- Kenneth Hoffman and Ray Kunze, *Linear Algebra*. Chapter 6 is the classical account of invariant subspaces, simultaneous triangularization and diagonalization, the primary decomposition and the semisimple part.
- Paul R. Halmos, *Finite-Dimensional Vector Spaces*. A short, coordinate-free treatment of invariant subspaces, nilpotent maps and the decompositions, in the language later used for operators on Hilbert space.
- Gilbert Strang, *Introduction to Linear Algebra*. Diagonalization as a computing tool, with powers of matrices, difference equations and Markov matrices.
- Roger A. Horn and Charles R. Johnson, *Matrix Analysis*. Similarity, commuting families, simultaneous diagonalization and triangularization, and Schur's theorem, at a more advanced level.
