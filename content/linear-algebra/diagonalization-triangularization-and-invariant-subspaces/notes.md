## History

Diagonalization began with quadratic forms, long before matrices had a name. In the eighteenth century Euler and Lagrange reduced quadratic forms and the equations of mechanics to principal axes, and in 1829 Cauchy proved that the characteristic roots of a real symmetric system are real, which is the heart of the principal axis theorem. In this setting every form can be diagonalized, so the question of which maps can be diagonalized did not yet arise.

It arose with general linear substitutions. Karl Weierstrass, in 1868, classified pairs of bilinear forms by their elementary divisors, and Camille Jordan, in his *Traité des substitutions et des équations algébriques* of 1870, reduced a linear substitution over a finite field to a block form with a single eigenvalue in each block. Both found that a matrix need not be similar to a diagonal one, and that the obstruction is the off-diagonal part of a block: in today's language, the nilpotent part. Benjamin Peirce introduced the word "nilpotent" in his *Linear Associative Algebra* of the same year. Georg Frobenius, in 1878, introduced the minimal polynomial and proved the Cayley-Hamilton theorem in general, and he later studied commuting matrices and showed that their eigenvalues can be paired so that sums and products behave as on a common triangular form. James Joseph Sylvester's formula $f(A) = \sum_i f(\lambda_i) E_i$ for a matrix with distinct eigenvalues dates from the 1880s. Issai Schur proved in 1909 that every complex matrix is unitarily similar to a triangular one.

The abstract point of view came in the twentieth century. A map $T$ makes $V$ a module over $F[t]$, its invariant subspaces are the submodules, and the kernels lemma and the primary decomposition are the Chinese remainder theorem and the splitting of a torsion module into primary parts. This view, developed in the algebra of the 1920s and set out in van der Waerden's *Moderne Algebra*, made the decompositions of this chapter work over any field. Hans Fitting proved the module form of the Fitting decomposition in the 1930s. Claude Chevalley used the splitting of a map into commuting semisimple and nilpotent parts systematically in his work on Lie algebras and algebraic groups, and the theorem now carries his name beside Jordan's. The imperfect-field examples show why semisimple, and not diagonalizable over the algebraic closure, is the right notion there.

Textbooks then settled on two routes to the results. Hoffman and Kunze's *Linear Algebra* derives diagonalization, the primary decomposition and the semisimple part from polynomials in $T$. Sheldon Axler's article "Down with Determinants!" (1995) and his book *Linear Algebra Done Right* avoid determinants: they prove the existence of eigenvalues, triangularization and the decomposition into generalized eigenspaces directly, and define the characteristic polynomial afterwards.

## Further reading

- Sheldon Axler, *Linear Algebra Done Right*, fourth edition (2024). Chapters 5 and 8 treat invariant subspaces, triangularization, generalized eigenspaces and nilpotent maps without determinants.
- Kenneth Hoffman and Ray Kunze, *Linear Algebra*, second edition (1971). Chapter 6 is the classical account of invariant subspaces, simultaneous triangularization and diagonalization, the primary decomposition and the semisimple part.
- Stephen H. Friedberg, Arnold J. Insel and Lawrence E. Spence, *Linear Algebra*, fifth edition. Chapter 5 covers diagonalization and the test by multiplicities with many computed examples and an application to Markov chains.
- David C. Lay, Steven R. Lay and Judi J. McDonald, *Linear Algebra and Its Applications*, fifth edition. Chapter 5 develops diagonalization through discrete dynamical systems and long-term behaviour.
- Roger A. Horn and Charles R. Johnson, *Matrix Analysis*, second edition (2013). Chapters 1 and 2 cover similarity, commuting families, simultaneous diagonalization and triangularization, and Schur's theorem, at a more advanced level.
