## History

The two normal forms of the chapter grew from two different problems. Karl Weierstrass, in 1868, classified pairs of bilinear forms and introduced elementary divisors to do it. Camille Jordan, in his *Traité des substitutions et des équations algébriques* of 1870, studied linear substitutions over finite fields and reduced each one to blocks with a single eigenvalue and ones beside the diagonal: the form that now carries his name. The two approaches give the same answer when the field contains the eigenvalues.

The rational form came from arithmetic. Henry John Stephen Smith, in a paper of 1861 on systems of linear indeterminate equations, reduced integer matrices to a diagonal form by row and column operations. Georg Frobenius took up the same ideas for matrices of polynomials in the late 1870s, and the rational canonical form is often called the Frobenius normal form. The word "rational" refers to operations inside the field of the entries: nothing has to be factored and no roots adjoined. Because of this, similarity of two rational matrices can be decided over $\Q$, and similarity over a larger field implies similarity over the smaller one.

Eduard Weyr described in 1885 a second arrangement of the Jordan basis, ordered by height rather than by chain. It was little used for a century, and was taken up again by Kevin O'Meara, John Clark and Charles Vinsonhaler, whose book of 2011 uses it for problems about commuting matrices. Olga Taussky and Hans Zassenhaus showed in 1959 that the matrix carrying a matrix to its transpose can be chosen symmetric.

The module language, in which $T$ turns $V$ into an $F[x]$-module and both normal forms become the structure theorem for finitely generated modules over a principal ideal domain, was set out in the algebra of the 1920s and 1930s, in the school of Emmy Noether. It explains why the same proof classifies finite abelian groups and similarity classes of matrices.

## Further reading

- Kenneth Hoffman and Ray Kunze, *Linear Algebra*. Chapter 7 proves the cyclic decomposition theorem with elementary tools and derives the rational and Jordan forms from it.
- Sheldon Axler, *Linear Algebra Done Right*. The Jordan form through generalized eigenspaces and nilpotent maps, without determinants.
- Paul R. Halmos, *Finite-Dimensional Vector Spaces*. A short, coordinate-free proof of the Jordan form for nilpotent maps and then in general.
- Roger A. Horn and Charles R. Johnson, *Matrix Analysis*. Chapter 3 treats the Jordan form and its consequences, including the real Jordan form, with many matrix examples.
- F. R. Gantmacher, *The Theory of Matrices*. The classical reference for the characteristic matrix, invariant polynomials, elementary divisors and the normal forms.
- Kevin C. O'Meara, John Clark and Charles I. Vinsonhaler, *Advanced Topics in Linear Algebra: Weaving Matrix Problems through the Weyr Form*. The Weyr form and its use for commuting matrices.
