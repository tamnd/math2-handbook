## History

The classification began with pairs of forms rather than with single matrices. Karl Weierstrass introduced elementary divisors in 1868, in a paper on pairs of bilinear and quadratic forms, and showed that they decide when two pencils $xB - A$ with $\det B \ne 0$ are equivalent. Camille Jordan published his normal form in 1870, in the *Traité des substitutions et des équations algébriques*, for linear substitutions over a finite prime field, and later used it over the complex numbers for systems of linear differential equations. In 1874 Jordan and Leopold Kronecker disputed in print whose approach to these questions was the right one, Jordan's normal form or the invariants of Weierstrass and Kronecker.

The arithmetic side came from number theory. Henry John Stephen Smith reduced integer matrices to a diagonal form in 1861, in a paper on systems of linear equations and congruences. Ferdinand Georg Frobenius, in papers of 1878 and 1879, proved the same reduction for matrices of polynomials. Applied to $xI - A$, it gives the invariant factors without factoring any polynomial, and with them the rational form that bears his name. He also proved the first general results on the matrices that commute with a given one. Eduard Weyr found the dual normal form now named after him in 1885. It stayed little known for a long time; the book of Kevin O'Meara, John Clark and Charles Vinsonhaler (2011) brought it back as a tool for commuting matrices.

In the 1920s the abstract algebra of Emmy Noether and her school recast the theory in terms of modules. An endomorphism $T$ makes $V$ a module over $F[x]$, similarity becomes isomorphism of modules, and the rational and Jordan forms become the invariant factor and elementary divisor forms of the structure theorem over a principal ideal domain. Bartel van der Waerden's *Moderne Algebra* (1930–1931) gave this treatment to a wide readership, and it is the standard one in algebra courses today. Olga Taussky and Hans Zassenhaus showed in 1959 that a matrix is similar to its transpose by a symmetric matrix, and that every matrix doing this is symmetric exactly when the matrix is cyclic.

The limits of the method became clear later. In 1969 Israel Gelfand and Vladimir Ponomarev showed that classifying pairs of commuting matrices up to simultaneous similarity contains the problem for arbitrary pairs, and Yuriy Drozd later proved that every finite-dimensional algebra over an algebraically closed field is either tame or wild. Over finite fields the classification gives exact counts: J. A. Green's work of 1955 on the characters of $GL_n(\mathbb{F}_q)$ uses its conjugacy classes, described by assigning partitions to irreducible polynomials as in the last entry of the chapter.

## Further reading

- Kenneth Hoffman and Ray Kunze, *Linear Algebra*, second edition (1971). Chapters 6 and 7 prove the cyclic decomposition theorem with elementary tools and derive the rational and Jordan forms; the source for most entries of this chapter.
- David S. Dummit and Richard M. Foote, *Abstract Algebra*, third edition (2004). Chapter 12 proves the structure theorem over a principal ideal domain and obtains the rational and Jordan canonical forms from it, with many computed examples.
- Roger A. Horn and Charles R. Johnson, *Matrix Analysis*, second edition. Chapter 3 treats the Jordan form, the real Jordan form, similarity to the transpose and the Weyr characteristic from the side of matrix analysis.
- F. R. Gantmacher, *The Theory of Matrices*, Volume 1. The classical account of $\lambda$-matrices, elementary divisors and normal forms, with the theory of pencils.
- Kevin C. O'Meara, John Clark and Charles I. Vinsonhaler, *Advanced Topics in Linear Algebra: Weaving Matrix Problems through the Weyr Form* (2011). The Weyr form, its centralizers and its use for commuting matrices.
