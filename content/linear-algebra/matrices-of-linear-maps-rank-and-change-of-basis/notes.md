## History

Arrays of coefficients are much older than matrices. The Chinese text *The Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art*, compiled about two thousand years ago, solves systems of linear equations by laying the coefficients out on a counting board and eliminating column by column, which is row reduction in all but name. In Europe, determinants came first, with Leibniz, Cramer and later Cauchy, and for a long time a square array was studied only through its determinant.

The word "matrix" is due to James Joseph Sylvester, who used it in 1850 for an array from which determinants (minors) are formed. Arthur Cayley's "A memoir on the theory of matrices" of 1858 treated matrices as objects in their own right. He defined the product so that it matches the composition of the linear substitutions the matrices stand for, which is the theorem that the matrix of a composition is the product of the matrices, and he wrote down the Cayley–Hamilton theorem. Sylvester later studied the nullity of a matrix and the way it behaves under products, which gave the inequality now named after him.

Rank as a single invariant of a matrix was made precise by Georg Frobenius in the 1870s, in his work on linear equations and bilinear forms; he used it to state when a system of equations has a solution. Camille Jordan's *Traité des substitutions et des équations algébriques* of 1870 gave the normal form under similarity that now carries his name, the first full answer to the question of which matrices describe the same endomorphism over the complex numbers. The theory of elementary divisors and invariant factors, due mainly to Karl Weierstrass and Frobenius, gave a complete answer that does not need the complex numbers.

The Fredholm alternative takes its name from Ivar Fredholm's 1903 paper in *Acta Mathematica* on integral equations, where he showed that a linear integral equation of a certain type behaves like a finite system: either the homogeneous equation has only the zero solution and the equation is solvable for every right-hand side, or the right-hand side must satisfy finitely many conditions. The matrix version in this chapter is the finite-dimensional model. The picture of the four fundamental subspaces as a single theorem was made popular in teaching by Gilbert Strang, in his textbooks and in his article "The fundamental theorem of linear algebra" in the *American Mathematical Monthly* (1993).

## Further reading

- Gilbert Strang, *Introduction to Linear Algebra*. Built around the four fundamental subspaces and the factorization $A = CR$, with many computed examples.
- Stephen H. Friedberg, Arnold J. Insel and Lawrence E. Spence, *Linear Algebra*. Careful on the matrix of a linear map, change of coordinates and similarity, with proofs and many exercises.
- Sheldon Axler, *Linear Algebra Done Right*. Treats matrices as a tool for linear maps and defines the trace of an operator before the trace of a matrix.
- David C. Lay, Steven R. Lay and Judi J. McDonald, *Linear Algebra and Its Applications*. A computational introduction to column spaces, null spaces, rank and change of basis, with applications.
- Roger A. Horn and Charles R. Johnson, *Matrix Analysis*. The reference for rank inequalities, similarity and the finer structure of matrices.
