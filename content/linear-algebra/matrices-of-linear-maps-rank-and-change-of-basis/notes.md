## History

Tables of coefficients are much older than matrices. The Chinese *Nine Chapters on the Mathematical Art*, compiled in the Han period, solves systems of linear equations by elimination on a counting board, column by column. Gauss used elimination in his work on least squares in the early nineteenth century, and the method now carries his name. For a long time the table was only a record of a system or of a determinant, not an object with its own algebra.

James Joseph Sylvester introduced the word "matrix" in 1850, for an array from which determinants are formed. Arthur Cayley's "A memoir on the theory of matrices" of 1858 treated matrices as quantities in their own right. He defined the product so that it matches the composition of linear substitutions, which is the content of the theorem that the matrix of a composition is the product of the matrices. Cayley and Sylvester also studied how the coefficients of a quadratic form change under a change of variables, the origin of the change of basis formula. Georg Frobenius made the rank a standard notion in his work on linear equations and bilinear forms, and Sylvester's law of nullity gave the lower bound for the rank of a product.

The classification of matrices up to similarity grew out of the same work. Karl Weierstrass developed the theory of elementary divisors for pairs of bilinear forms, and Camille Jordan gave his normal form in the *Traité des substitutions et des équations algébriques* of 1870. These results showed that the trace, the determinant and the characteristic polynomial do not decide similarity on their own.

Ivar Fredholm studied integral equations around 1900, and his paper "Sur une classe d'équations fonctionnelles" in *Acta Mathematica* (1903) proved the alternative that now bears his name: either the equation has a unique solution for every right-hand side, or the homogeneous equation has nonzero solutions, and then a solution exists exactly when the right-hand side satisfies finitely many conditions. The finite-dimensional version in this chapter is the simplest case. Gilbert Strang's article "The fundamental theorem of linear algebra" in the *American Mathematical Monthly* (1993) organized the rank, the four subspaces and their orthogonality into the picture used in many courses today, and he later promoted the factorization $A = CR$ as a first step in teaching.

## Further reading

- Gilbert Strang, *Introduction to Linear Algebra*, sixth edition (2023). The four fundamental subspaces and the factorization $A = CR$ from the source, with many computed examples.
- David C. Lay, Steven R. Lay and Judi J. McDonald, *Linear Algebra and Its Applications*, fifth edition. Column space, null space, rank and change of basis, done carefully with matrices and many exercises.
- Sheldon Axler, *Linear Algebra Done Right*, fourth edition (2024). The matrix of a linear map, the isomorphism $\operatorname{Hom}(V, W) \cong M_{m \times n}(F)$ and the trace from the side of linear maps.
- Jim Hefferon, *Linear Algebra*. A free textbook with a full treatment of the representation of maps by matrices and of change of basis, and answers to its exercises.
- Roger A. Horn and Charles R. Johnson, *Matrix Analysis*, second edition (2013). The rank inequalities of Sylvester and Frobenius, similarity and canonical forms, as a reference.
