## History

Linear expressions $a_1x_1 + \dots + a_nx_n$ are as old as linear equations, and for a long time a linear form was simply such an expression, or the row of its coefficients. Geometers of the nineteenth century used the duality between points and lines of the projective plane, in which a line is given by the coefficients of its equation; the dual space is the algebraic form of this idea. Giuseppe Peano's axiomatic definition of a vector space in 1888 came with linear maps, and so with linear forms as maps into the field.

The dual basis appeared before the dual space. Josiah Willard Gibbs, in his vector analysis, used the reciprocal system of three vectors in space, which is the dual basis carried into space by the dot product; it entered the textbook *Vector Analysis* that Edwin Bidwell Wilson wrote from Gibbs's lectures (1901). Around 1900 Gregorio Ricci-Curbastro and Tullio Levi-Civita set out the absolute differential calculus, in which covariant and contravariant components of a vector are its coordinates in a dual basis and in a basis, written with lower and upper indices.

Duality took its modern shape in analysis. In 1907 Frigyes Riesz and Maurice Fréchet showed, independently, that each continuous linear form on the space of square-integrable functions is an inner product with a fixed function. Hans Hahn and Stefan Banach proved the extension theorem for linear forms in the late 1920s, and Banach's *Théorie des opérations linéaires* (1932) made the dual space and the dual map standard tools. In infinite dimension the algebraic dual, with all linear forms, is much larger than the space; the theorem that fixes its dimension is credited to Paul Erdős and Irving Kaplansky, and it appears in Nathan Jacobson's *Lectures in Abstract Algebra*.

The canonical map $V \to V^{**}$ played a part in the birth of category theory. Samuel Eilenberg and Saunders Mac Lane, in their 1945 paper "General theory of natural equivalences", used the contrast between $V \cong V^*$, which needs a basis, and $V \cong V^{**}$, which does not, to explain what a natural isomorphism is. Paul Halmos's *Finite-Dimensional Vector Spaces* (1942) taught linear algebra with dual spaces and dual maps in the foreground, with infinite-dimensional analysis in view.

## Further reading

- Paul R. Halmos, *Finite-Dimensional Vector Spaces*. The classic account of duals, annihilators, the double dual and the dual map, written to prepare for operator theory.
- Sheldon Axler, *Linear Algebra Done Right*. Its section on duality gives the dual basis, annihilators and the dual map with full proofs, and uses them to prove that row rank equals column rank.
- Kenneth Hoffman and Ray Kunze, *Linear Algebra*. Treats linear functionals, annihilators, the double dual and the transpose of a linear map, with hyperplanes in arbitrary vector spaces.
- Steven Roman, *Advanced Linear Algebra*. Covers the algebraic dual in infinite dimension, including the dimension of the dual and the failure of reflexivity.
- Nathan Jacobson, *Lectures in Abstract Algebra, Volume II: Linear Algebra*. An older text that treats dual spaces of infinite-dimensional spaces and proves the theorem on the dimension of the dual.
