## History

Linear forms are as old as linear equations: each equation $a_1 x_1 + \cdots + a_n x_n = b$ sets a form equal to a number. What came later was the idea that the forms themselves make up a space. Giuseppe Peano gave the axioms of a vector space and of a linear map in 1888, in his *Calcolo geometrico secondo l'Ausdehnungslehre di H. Grassmann*, and with them the forms on a space became one more example of linear maps. In the vector analysis that Josiah Willard Gibbs developed in the 1880s, the reciprocal system of three vectors in space plays the role of a dual basis.

Gregorio Ricci-Curbastro and Tullio Levi-Civita set out their absolute differential calculus in 1900, in "Méthodes de calcul différentiel absolu et leurs applications" in *Mathematische Annalen*. Their covariant and contravariant components, written with lower and upper indices, are coordinates in a dual basis and in a basis, and the rule that the two kinds of index change in opposite ways is the change of a dual basis by $(P^{-1})^\top$. In analysis, Frigyes Riesz and Maurice Fréchet showed in 1907 that the continuous linear forms on the space of square-integrable functions are given by inner products with fixed functions. Hans Hahn and Stefan Banach proved the extension theorem for continuous forms in the late 1920s, and Banach's *Théorie des opérations linéaires* of 1932 made the dual space a central object of functional analysis.

For spaces without topology, the algebraic dual became standard in the textbooks of the twentieth century. Samuel Eilenberg and Saunders Mac Lane used the map from a space to its double dual as their first example of a natural transformation in "General theory of natural equivalences" (1945): it is defined for every space at once, with no choice of basis, while the isomorphism $V \cong V^*$ in finite dimension depends on one. The rule $(g \circ f)^* = f^* \circ g^*$ says that taking duals is a contravariant functor, and the transpose of a matrix, much older than any of this, became the matrix of the dual map.

The theorem on the dimension of the dual of an infinite-dimensional space is named after Paul Erdős and Irving Kaplansky. Bourbaki's *Algebra* gives the formula $\dim F^I = \abs{F^I}$ for infinite sets $I$, from which it follows. It shows that for infinite-dimensional spaces the dual is always strictly larger, so the finite-dimensional picture of $V$ and $V^*$ as spaces of the same size breaks down entirely.

## Further reading

- Sheldon Axler, *Linear Algebra Done Right*, fourth edition (2024). Section 3F treats dual spaces, dual bases, annihilators and the dual map, and proves row rank equals column rank through them.
- Stephen H. Friedberg, Arnold J. Insel and Lawrence E. Spence, *Linear Algebra*, fifth edition. Section 2.6 covers dual spaces and the transpose with many exercises, including the double dual.
- Paul R. Halmos, *Finite-Dimensional Vector Spaces*, second edition (1958). The classic account of duality, annihilators and the double dual, written in the language later used in functional analysis.
- Steven Roman, *Advanced Linear Algebra*, third edition (2008). Duality in infinite dimension, including the dimension of the dual and the failure of reflexivity.
- Saunders Mac Lane and Garrett Birkhoff, *Algebra*, third edition (1988). Dual spaces and dual maps as functors, and the double dual as a natural isomorphism.
