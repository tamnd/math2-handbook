## History

Sums of subspaces and their dimensions go back to Hermann Grassmann's *Die lineale Ausdehnungslehre* of 1844, which treats spans of vectors in any number of dimensions and contains the relation between the dimensions of a sum and an intersection that is still called Grassmann's formula. Grassmann's work was read by few at the time. Giuseppe Peano gave the axioms of a real vector space in 1888, in his *Calcolo geometrico secondo l'Ausdehnungslehre di H. Grassmann*, and with them linear maps; his examples already include spaces of functions of infinite dimension.

Projections appeared under other names well before the abstract theory. Benjamin Peirce introduced the word "idempotent" for an element equal to its own square in his *Linear Associative Algebra* of 1870. In the theory of algebras and of matrices, decompositions of the identity into sums of idempotents became a standard tool, and they are the algebraic form of a direct sum decomposition.

Quotient constructions grew first in group theory and number theory, with residue classes and factor groups, and moved to vector spaces when algebra was recast in terms of structures. Emmy Noether's work in the 1920s placed homomorphisms, kernels and quotients at the centre of algebra, and the isomorphism theorems are often named after her. In finite dimension complements and quotients do the same job, which is why older textbooks often avoid quotients.

For infinite dimension, Georg Hamel showed in 1905 with the well-ordering theorem that $\R$ has a basis over $\Q$, and the same method gives a basis, and so a complement of every subspace, in every vector space. Max Zorn's lemma of 1935 became the usual tool. Andreas Blass proved in 1984 that the converse holds: if every vector space has a basis, the axiom of choice is true.

Exact sequences come from algebraic topology. Samuel Eilenberg and Saunders Mac Lane gave the language of categories in 1945, and Eilenberg and Norman Steenrod built exact sequences into the axioms of homology in *Foundations of Algebraic Topology* (1952). Henri Cartan and Eilenberg's *Homological Algebra* (1956) made them the standard language of algebra. For vector spaces every short exact sequence splits, so the theory there is short, but it is the model case.

## Further reading

- Sheldon Axler, *Linear Algebra Done Right*. Its sections on products and quotients of vector spaces give the quotient, the isomorphism theorems in finite dimension and the dimension formulas with full proofs.
- Paul R. Halmos, *Finite-Dimensional Vector Spaces*. A classic short treatment of direct sums, complements, quotients and projections, written with infinite-dimensional analysis in mind.
- Kenneth Hoffman and Ray Kunze, *Linear Algebra*. The chapter on elementary canonical forms treats direct-sum decompositions and projections as the route to diagonalization.
- Steven Roman, *Advanced Linear Algebra*. Covers external direct sums and products of infinite families, quotients, the isomorphism theorems and exact sequences, with the infinite-dimensional cases spelled out.
- Stephen H. Friedberg, Arnold J. Insel and Lawrence E. Spence, *Linear Algebra*. A gentle source of worked problems on direct sums, quotients and projections.
