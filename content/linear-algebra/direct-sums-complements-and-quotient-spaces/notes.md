## History

Hermann Grassmann studied sums and intersections of subspaces in his *Ausdehnungslehre* of 1844, and the formula $\dim(U + W) + \dim(U \cap W) = \dim U + \dim W$ still carries his name. His work was read by few at the time. Giuseppe Peano gave the axioms of a vector space in 1888, in his *Calcolo geometrico secondo l'Ausdehnungslehre di H. Grassmann*, and the abstract notion slowly replaced spaces of coordinates.

The question of complements is a question about bases. Georg Hamel showed in 1905 that $\R$ has a basis as a vector space over $\Q$, using the well-ordering theorem, and Ernst Steinitz proved the exchange lemma in 1913. Max Zorn published his maximum principle in 1935, and it became the standard tool for proving that every vector space has a basis, and so that every subspace has a complement. Andreas Blass showed in 1984 that the converse holds: if every vector space has a basis, the axiom of choice is true.

Quotients came first for groups. Camille Jordan used quotient groups in his work on permutation groups, and Otto Hölder defined the quotient group in 1889, in his proof of the uniqueness part of the Jordan–Hölder theorem; the second isomorphism theorem is often credited to him. Richard Dedekind formed quotients of rings by ideals. Emmy Noether stated the isomorphism theorems for modules in her 1927 paper on the abstract construction of ideal theory, and Bartel van der Waerden's *Moderne Algebra* of 1930–1931 made them, and the notation $\oplus$ for direct sums, standard. The same theorems then hold for vector spaces as a special case.

Exact sequences come from algebraic topology. Witold Hurewicz introduced them in 1941, Samuel Eilenberg and Norman Steenrod made them part of the language in *Foundations of Algebraic Topology* (1952), and Henri Cartan and Eilenberg built homological algebra on them in *Homological Algebra* (1956). Over a field every short exact sequence splits, which is why vector spaces are the simplest case of that theory. The idea of a universal property, which describes the direct sum, the product and the quotient by the maps into or out of them, was made explicit in the category theory of the 1940s and 1950s.

## Further reading

- Sheldon Axler, *Linear Algebra Done Right*, fourth edition (2024). Chapter 3 treats products, quotients and the first isomorphism theorem in the same spirit as this chapter, without determinants.
- Stephen H. Friedberg, Arnold J. Insel and Lawrence E. Spence, *Linear Algebra*, fifth edition. Direct sums and quotients with many worked exercises, at a level just above a first course.
- Paul R. Halmos, *Finite-Dimensional Vector Spaces*, second edition (1958). A short classic that treats direct sums, quotients and projections as the basic operations on spaces.
- Steven Roman, *Advanced Linear Algebra*, third edition (2008). Complements, quotients, exact sequences and the infinite-dimensional case, with the module-theoretic view.
- Saunders Mac Lane and Garrett Birkhoff, *Algebra*, third edition (1988). Universal properties, products, coproducts and exact sequences for modules, with vector spaces as the first example.
