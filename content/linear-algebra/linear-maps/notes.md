## History

Linear substitutions of variables were in use long before linear maps had a name. Gauss, in his *Disquisitiones Arithmeticae* of 1801, transformed binary quadratic forms by substitutions $x = \alpha x' + \beta y'$, $y = \gamma x' + \delta y'$ and composed such substitutions. Arthur Cayley's "A memoir on the theory of matrices" of 1858 took the array of coefficients of a linear substitution as an object in its own right and defined the product of two matrices so that it matches the composite of the two substitutions. This is why matrix multiplication is defined the way it is.

The kernel and the image were studied first through linear systems. The kernel of $x \mapsto Ax$ is the solution set of $Ax = 0$, and the question whether $b$ lies in the image is the question whether $Ax = b$ is solvable, which the nineteenth-century theorem now called the Rouché-Capelli theorem answers by ranks; in different countries it also carries the names of Kronecker, Frobenius and Fontené. In 1884 James Joseph Sylvester proved his law of nullity, which bounds the nullity of a product of matrices by the nullities of the factors.

Giuseppe Peano gave the axioms of a vector space and the definition of a linear map in his *Calcolo geometrico* of 1888. The abstract point of view, with maps between spaces in place of matrices, spread in the twentieth century. Paul Halmos's *Finite-Dimensional Vector Spaces* of 1942 presented finite-dimensional linear algebra in this basis-free style, with the results of this chapter proved without choosing coordinates.

Infinite dimension showed which results depend on finiteness. Ivar Fredholm studied integral equations $\lambda\varphi - K\varphi = f$ at the start of the twentieth century and found that, for suitable operators $K$, the alternative "either the homogeneous equation has a nonzero solution, or the inhomogeneous one is solvable for every $f$" survives in infinite dimension; in finite dimension it is the equivalence of injective and surjective endomorphisms. Semilinear maps come from projective geometry, where the fundamental theorem of projective geometry shows that the collineations of a projective space of dimension at least $2$ come from semilinear bijections.

## Further reading

- Sheldon Axler, *Linear Algebra Done Right*, fourth edition (2024). Chapter 3 treats linear maps, the fundamental theorem of linear maps (rank-nullity), matrices, invertibility and $\operatorname{Hom}(V, W)$, in the same order as this chapter.
- Stephen H. Friedberg, Arnold J. Insel and Lawrence E. Spence, *Linear Algebra*, fifth edition (2019). Chapter 2 covers linear transformations, null spaces and ranges, matrix representations and isomorphisms, with many exercises.
- Jim Hefferon, *Linear Algebra*, a free textbook. Chapter Three starts with isomorphisms and only then turns to general homomorphisms, rank and nullity, which gives a different first view.
- Kenneth Hoffman and Ray Kunze, *Linear Algebra*, second edition (1971). Chapter 3 develops linear transformations, the algebra of linear transformations and isomorphisms with full proofs.
- Paul R. Halmos, *Finite-Dimensional Vector Spaces*, second edition (1958). The classic coordinate-free account of linear maps and their algebra.
