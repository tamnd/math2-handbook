## History

Linear maps entered algebra as linear substitutions of variables, used in the theory of invariants of forms. Arthur Cayley's *A Memoir on the Theory of Matrices* of 1858 treated a matrix as a quantity in its own right and defined the product of two matrices so that it corresponds to the composite of the two substitutions. That definition is the reason for the order rule of composition in this chapter. In 1884 James Joseph Sylvester stated his law of nullity for square matrices: the nullity of a product is at least the nullity of each factor and at most the sum of their nullities.

The abstract linear map, defined on any vector space and not only on columns of numbers, came with the axiomatic vector space and with analysis. Ivar Fredholm's work on integral equations, published in 1903, showed that an equation $x - Kx = y$ with an integral operator $K$ behaves in many ways like a finite linear system; this is the Fredholm alternative. Stefan Banach's *Théorie des opérations linéaires* of 1932 made the bounded linear maps between normed spaces a subject of its own. In infinite dimension some finite-dimensional facts fail, as the shift operators of this chapter show.

Semilinear maps arise in projective geometry. The [fundamental theorem of projective geometry](/GKV4RVXBRE/) says that, for a vector space $V$ of dimension at least $3$, every bijection of the projective space of $V$ that sends lines to lines is induced by a semilinear bijection of $V$.

## Further reading

- Sheldon Axler, *Linear Algebra Done Right*. Chapter 3 treats linear maps, null spaces and ranges, the fundamental theorem of linear maps and isomorphisms without determinants.
- Kenneth Hoffman and Ray Kunze, *Linear Algebra*. Chapter 3 covers linear transformations, the algebra of linear transformations and isomorphisms over a general field.
- Paul R. Halmos, *Finite-Dimensional Vector Spaces*. A compact account of linear transformations and their algebra, written with infinite-dimensional analysis in mind.
- Gilbert Strang, *Introduction to Linear Algebra*. The computational view of kernels and images as the null space and column space, with many worked examples.
- Stephen H. Friedberg, Arnold J. Insel and Lawrence E. Spence, *Linear Algebra*. Chapter 2 has linear transformations, their matrices and isomorphisms, with a large stock of exercises.
