## History

Matrices were used as arrays of coefficients long before they were treated as objects in their own right. Gauss composed linear substitutions in the *Disquisitiones Arithmeticae* of 1801, while studying quadratic forms, and the rule he used to combine two substitutions is the rule for the [product of matrices](/032Q9FB8MT/). Binet and Cauchy proved the product formula for determinants in 1812, still without a separate notion of a matrix. Sylvester introduced the word "matrix" in 1850, for an array from which determinants (minors) can be formed.

Cayley's "A memoir on the theory of matrices" of 1858 is where matrices become a calculus. Cayley defined the sum, the scalar multiple and the product, the zero and identity matrices, and the [transpose](/09DXWYRD43/). He noted that the product is [associative](/073E4XC016/) but [not commutative](/0P506SPZP2/). He also stated the theorem now named after him and Hamilton, verified it for $2 \times 2$ matrices, and said he had checked the $3 \times 3$ case.

Symmetric matrices came first from quadratic forms and mechanics. In 1829 Cauchy proved that the characteristic roots of a real [symmetric matrix](/0VR671PHE5/) are real. Hermite extended the result to the complex forms whose matrices are now called [Hermitian](/0P17NGQQMQ/). Skew-symmetric matrices appear in the theory of the Pfaffian, a name Cayley gave in 1852 to a polynomial studied earlier by Pfaff. The Pfaffian is a square root of the determinant of a skew-symmetric matrix of even order.

Benjamin Peirce's *Linear Associative Algebra*, circulated around 1870, studied algebras with an associative product and introduced the words [nilpotent](/07BSPZPSMF/) and [idempotent](/0KCE8CG86P/). Sylvester returned to matrices in the 1880s and studied, among other things, the equation $px = xq$ in matrices. Wedderburn's 1908 paper on hypercomplex numbers showed that a simple algebra of finite dimension over a field is a full matrix ring over a division algebra. This made $M_n(D)$, with its [matrix units](/A4HTT7JFD1/) and its [centre](/DVZ1V4A884/), the basic example of a noncommutative ring.

## Further reading

- Gilbert Strang, *Introduction to Linear Algebra*. Explains the product row by row, column by column and as a sum of column-times-row pieces, with many worked examples.
- David C. Lay, Steven R. Lay and Judi J. McDonald, *Linear Algebra and Its Applications*. A first course that covers matrix operations, partitioned matrices and the transpose with many short exercises.
- Kenneth Hoffman and Ray Kunze, *Linear Algebra*. A rigorous text that treats matrices over a general field and connects them early to linear maps.
- Roger A. Horn and Charles R. Johnson, *Matrix Analysis*. The standard reference for Hermitian, normal and nilpotent matrices, the trace and block methods, at a graduate level.
- Sheldon Axler, *Linear Algebra Done Right*. Puts linear maps first and shows why the matrix product is defined as it is.
