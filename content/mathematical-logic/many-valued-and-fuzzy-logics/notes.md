## History

Jan Łukasiewicz published his three-valued logic in 1920, in a short Polish paper "O logice trójwartościowej". His motive was Aristotle's sea battle in chapter 9 of *De Interpretatione*: a sentence about a contingent future event should be neither true nor false now. Emil Post's thesis, published in 1921 as "Introduction to a general theory of elementary propositions" in the *American Journal of Mathematics*, took $m$ values with a cyclic negation and proved that his connectives define every function. Łukasiewicz and Alfred Tarski surveyed the finite-valued and the infinite-valued systems in 1930. Kurt Gödel used finite chains in 1932 to show that intuitionistic logic has no finite characteristic matrix. Donald Webb found a Sheffer function for every $m$ in 1935, and Jerzy Słupecki gave his completeness criterion in 1939.

The completeness of Łukasiewicz's axioms for the unit interval stayed open for almost thirty years. Robert McNaughton described the functions defined by formulas in 1951. In 1958 Alan Rose and J. Barkley Rosser gave a syntactic completeness proof, and C. C. Chang introduced MV-algebras in 1958 and gave an algebraic proof in 1959. Daniele Mundici connected MV-algebras with lattice-ordered abelian groups in 1986 and proved in 1987 that the tautology problem is coNP-complete. On the first-order side, Andrzej Mostowski asked in 1961 which many-valued predicate calculi are axiomatizable, and Bruno Scarpellini proved in 1962 that first-order Łukasiewicz logic is not. Alfred Horn axiomatized a first-order Gödel logic in 1969.

Dmitry Bochvar read the third value as "meaningless" in 1938, and in the same year Stephen Kleene used it for partial recursive predicates; his *Introduction to Metamathematics* of 1952 gives both the strong and the weak tables. Sören Halldén's *The Logic of Nonsense* appeared in 1949. Graham Priest proposed LP in "The logic of paradox" in 1979, as a logic in which some contradictions are true. Alan Anderson and Nuel Belnap studied first-degree entailment in their work on relevance logic. J. Michael Dunn gave its four-valued semantics in 1976, and Belnap described it in 1977 as the logic a computer should use when it receives conflicting data.

Lotfi Zadeh's paper "Fuzzy sets" of 1965 started fuzzy set theory, and Joseph Goguen gave a logic of inexact concepts in 1969. Jan Pavelka's "On fuzzy logic I, II, III" of 1979 introduced graded consequence with truth constants. Karl Menger introduced triangular norms in 1942 for probabilistic metric spaces, and Paul Mostert and Allen Shields described the continuous ones in 1957. Petr Hájek's book of 1998 defined BL and made mathematical fuzzy logic a field of its own. Francesc Esteva and Lluís Godo introduced MTL in 2001, and Sándor Jenei and Franco Montagna proved its standard completeness in 2002. Alfréd Rényi introduced the search game in 1961, and Stanisław Ulam posed the version with lies in his autobiography *Adventures of a Mathematician* of 1976. Nils Nilsson's "Probabilistic logic" of 1986 and the 1990 paper of Ronald Fagin, Joseph Halpern and Nimrod Megiddo brought probability logic into computer science, and Eduardo Mizraji introduced vector logic in 1992.

## Further reading

- Graham Priest, *An Introduction to Non-Classical Logic*, second edition (2008). It treats $\mathsf{K}_3$, $\mathsf{LP}$, $\mathrm{Ł}_3$, FDE and fuzzy logic side by side, with tableaux for each.
- Grzegorz Malinowski, *Many-Valued Logics* (1993). A short survey of matrices, the classical finite-valued systems and their history.
- R. L. O. Cignoli, I. M. L. D'Ottaviano and D. Mundici, *Algebraic Foundations of Many-valued Reasoning* (2000). The standard account of MV-algebras, Chang's completeness theorem and McNaughton's theorem.
- Petr Hájek, *Metamathematics of Fuzzy Logic* (1998). The book that defines BL and develops Łukasiewicz, Gödel and product logic, their first-order versions and Pavelka logic.
- E. P. Klement, R. Mesiar and E. Pap, *Triangular Norms* (2000). The reference on t-norms, their residua and the Mostert–Shields theorem.
