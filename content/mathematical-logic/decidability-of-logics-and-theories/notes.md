## History

The decision problem for first-order logic took its classical form in Hilbert and Ackermann's *Grundzüge der theoretischen Logik* (1928), which called it the main problem of mathematical logic. Positive results came first. Löwenheim showed in 1915 that the monadic predicate calculus is decidable, and Behmann gave a decision procedure by quantifier elimination in 1922. Bernays and Schönfinkel settled the prefix class $\exists^*\forall^*$ in 1928, Ackermann the class $\exists^*\forall\exists^*$ in the same year, and Ramsey extended the first to logic with equality in 1930, in the paper where Ramsey's theorem first appeared.

The negative answer came in 1936, from Church in "A note on the Entscheidungsproblem" and from Turing in "On computable numbers, with an application to the Entscheidungsproblem". Both had to make precise what an algorithm is before they could show that none exists. Trakhtenbrot proved in 1950 that validity over finite structures is not even computably enumerable, a result now counted as the start of finite model theory. The method of proving theories undecidable by interpretation was set out by Tarski, Mostowski and Raphael Robinson in *Undecidable Theories* (1953), with Robinson arithmetic as the base case. Julia Robinson showed in 1949 that the integers are definable in the field of rationals, so the theory of fields is undecidable.

Arithmetic split early into decidable and undecidable parts. Presburger proved in 1929 that the theory of the natural numbers with addition admits quantifier elimination and is decidable. Skolem studied the theory of multiplication, and Mostowski proved its decidability in 1952 by a theorem on weak direct powers that Feferman and Vaught later generalized. Fischer and Rabin showed in 1974 that every decision procedure for Presburger arithmetic needs doubly exponential time on some inputs.

The link between logic and automata appeared around 1960. Büchi, Elgot and Trakhtenbrot proved independently that monadic second-order logic on finite words defines exactly the regular languages. Büchi extended this to infinite words in a lecture at the 1960 Congress for Logic, Methodology and Philosophy of Science, published in 1962, and McNaughton proved in 1966 that the automata involved can be made deterministic with a Muller condition. Rabin's tree theorem of 1969 decided the monadic theory of two successors and became a source of decidability results for many other theories, from the order of the rationals to modal and temporal logics.

Later work turned these results toward computer science. Courcelle showed in 1990 that monadic second-order properties can be checked in linear time on graphs of bounded treewidth, and Seese showed in 1991 that bounded treewidth is necessary for a decidable theory of this kind. On the side of first-order fragments, Mortimer proved in 1975 that two-variable logic has the finite model property, and Grädel, Kolaitis and Vardi showed in 1997 that its satisfiability problem is NEXPTIME-complete. Andréka, van Benthem and Németi introduced the guarded fragment in 1998 to explain why modal logics are decidable, and Grädel determined its complexity in 1999.

## Further reading

- Egon Börger, Erich Grädel and Yuri Gurevich, *The Classical Decision Problem* (1997). The reference work on prefix classes and other fragments of first-order logic, with complete classifications and proofs.
- Alfred Tarski, Andrzej Mostowski and Raphael M. Robinson, *Undecidable Theories* (1953). A short book that introduced essential undecidability and the method of interpretations; still the clearest source for both.
- George S. Boolos, John P. Burgess and Richard C. Jeffrey, *Computability and Logic*, 5th edition (2007). A gentle route from Turing machines to Church's theorem, with a chapter on decidable theories such as Presburger arithmetic.
- Erich Grädel, Wolfgang Thomas and Thomas Wilke (eds.), *Automata, Logics, and Infinite Games* (2002). Survey chapters on Büchi and Rabin automata, parity games and the decidability of S1S and S2S.
- Jörg Flum and Martin Grohe, *Parameterized Complexity Theory* (2006). Contains a careful proof of Courcelle's theorem and its limits, in the setting of parameterized algorithms.
