[Intuitionistic logic](/Q11N8X99FK/) is the logic of constructive mathematics. It grew from L. E. J. Brouwer's view that a mathematical statement is true only when there is a construction that proves it, and Arend Heyting gave it axioms in 1930. Its meaning is described by the [Brouwer–Heyting–Kolmogorov interpretation](/7B53ZGXXEQ/): a proof of $\varphi \to \psi$ is a method that turns any proof of $\varphi$ into a proof of $\psi$, and a proof of $\exists x\, \varphi(x)$ provides a particular $x$ and a proof of $\varphi(x)$. On this reading the [law of excluded middle](/0RYYK8PG4S/) is not valid, since it would require a uniform method for deciding every statement, and the [double negation law](/0NFAGWH84K/) holds in one direction only.

The logic has several semantics. In [Kripke semantics](/NGHWZ9Y7ZV/), given by Kripke in 1965, worlds are states of knowledge ordered by time, and a statement once established stays established. In algebraic semantics, formulas take values in a [Heyting algebra](/NG943XNQWH/), such as the lattice of open sets of a topological space. The metatheory has features that classical logic lacks. If $\varphi \lor \psi$ is provable, then $\varphi$ or $\psi$ is provable; this is the disjunction property. Glivenko showed in 1929 that a propositional formula is a classical tautology exactly when its double negation is intuitionistically provable, and the [double-negation translation](/HGJVM49PA4/) of Gödel and Gentzen extends this to first-order logic and arithmetic. The Gödel–McKinsey–Tarski translation embeds intuitionistic logic in the modal logic $\mathrm{S4}$.

Between intuitionistic and classical propositional logic lie the intermediate logics. Examples are the Gödel–Dummett logic $\mathrm{LC}$, with the axiom $(p \to q) \lor (q \to p)$, the logic of weak excluded middle $\mathrm{KC}$, and Medvedev's logic of finite problems. There are continuum many intermediate logics. Johansson's minimal logic of 1937 is weaker still, with no special rule for falsity.
