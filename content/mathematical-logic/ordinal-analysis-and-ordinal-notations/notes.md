## History

Georg Cantor's normal form of the 1890s gave the first finite names for the ordinals below $\varepsilon_0$, and Oswald Veblen's fixed-point functions of 1908 named much larger ones. Alonzo Church and Stephen Kleene studied notations for the computable ordinals in the 1930s, and Kleene's system $\mathcal{O}$ comes from that work. Proof theory took up notation systems with Gerhard Gentzen. His consistency proof for Peano arithmetic appeared in *Mathematische Annalen* in 1936, after an earlier version was withdrawn when Paul Bernays objected to a method in it. A second version of 1938 assigned ordinals to sequent derivations, and in 1943 Gentzen proved that Peano arithmetic proves transfinite induction up to each ordinal below $\varepsilon_0$ but not up to $\varepsilon_0$. Wilhelm Ackermann gave a consistency proof by the epsilon substitution method in 1940.

Kurt Schütte used the $\omega$-rule in 1951 to give a new proof of Gentzen's results, with the heights of infinite derivations in place of Gentzen's reduction procedure. Hilbert had considered a rule of this kind in 1931, and William Tait later gave the one-sided calculus that most accounts now use. Solomon Feferman and Schütte, working independently in 1964 and 1965, identified $\Gamma_0$ as the limit of predicative analysis, using Veblen's hierarchy and autonomous progressions of ramified systems.

The impredicative part of the subject began with Gaisi Takeuti. In 1953 he stated his conjecture on cut elimination for his generalized logic calculus. Tait proved the second-order case in 1966 by a semantic method based on Schütte's work, and Moto-o Takahashi in 1967 and Dag Prawitz in 1968 proved the higher-order case independently. Takeuti's consistency proof for $\Pi^1_1$-comprehension, in the *Annals of Mathematics* in 1967, used ordinal diagrams as its measure of complexity.

Heinz Bachmann defined the first collapsing functions in 1950, and William Howard studied the ordinal of the theory of one inductive definition in 1972. Georg Kreisel proposed formal theories of inductive definitions in the early 1960s, and Feferman introduced the iterated theories $\mathrm{ID}_\nu$ in 1970. Wilfried Buchholz, with the $\Omega_{\mu+1}$-rule, and Wolfram Pohlers, with local predicativity, completed the analysis of these theories, and *Iterated inductive definitions and subsystems of analysis* (Lecture Notes in Mathematics 897, 1981), by Buchholz, Feferman, Pohlers and Sieg, collects the results. Buchholz's functions $\psi_\nu$ of 1986 gave a short standard notation for the same ordinals, and his paper *A simplified version of local predicativity* of 1992 introduced operator-controlled derivations. Michael Rathjen analysed $\mathrm{KPM}$ with a Mahlo cardinal in 1991 and gave the well-ordering proof in 1994.

## Further reading

- W. Pohlers, *Proof Theory: The First Step into Impredicativity*. A textbook that goes from Gentzen's analysis of arithmetic to the theories of inductive definitions, with the $\omega$-rule and collapsing functions.
- K. Schütte, *Proof Theory*. The classical account of infinitary derivations, the Veblen functions and the ordinal $\Gamma_0$.
- G. Takeuti, *Proof Theory*. Gentzen's consistency proof in detail, and Takeuti's own ordinal diagrams.
- W. Buchholz, S. Feferman, W. Pohlers and W. Sieg, *Iterated Inductive Definitions and Subsystems of Analysis: Recent Proof-Theoretical Studies*. The source for the theories $\mathrm{ID}_\nu$ and the methods that analyse them.
- M. Rathjen, "The art of ordinal analysis". A survey of the programme, its notation systems and its results up to strong impredicative theories.
- H. Schwichtenberg and S. S. Wainer, *Proofs and Computations*. Ordinal analysis together with the provably total functions and fast-growing hierarchies.
