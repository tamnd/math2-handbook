## History

Modern modal logic began with C. I. Lewis, who objected that material implication makes a false statement imply everything. In *A Survey of Symbolic Logic* (1918) he proposed strict implication, $\square(p \to q)$, and in *Symbolic Logic* (1932), written with C. H. Langford, he set out the systems $\mathsf{S1}$ to $\mathsf{S5}$. These were axiomatized with strict implication as primitive, and they were given no semantics. In a short note of 1933, Gödel took $\square$ as primitive and axiomatized $\mathsf{S4}$ as propositional logic with $\square p \to p$, the distribution axiom, $\square p \to \square\square p$ and the rule of necessitation, the form used today. In the same note he translated intuitionistic logic into it, reading $\square$ as "it is provable that".

The first semantic methods were algebraic. McKinsey proved in 1941 that $\mathsf{S2}$ and $\mathsf{S4}$ are decidable, using finite algebras, and McKinsey and Tarski proved in 1948 that Gödel's translation is faithful. Jónsson and Tarski's work on Boolean algebras with operators (1951–52) already contained a representation that amounts to the canonical frame, but it was not read as a semantics for modal logic. Carnap's *Meaning and Necessity* (1947) read necessity as truth in all state descriptions, an early form of the universal frames of $\mathsf{S5}$. In *An Essay in Modal Logic* (1951), von Wright studied the system now called $\mathsf{T}$ and began deontic logic. Prior's *Time and Modality* (1957) began tense logic.

Relational semantics was found in the late 1950s by several authors, among them Kanger, Hintikka and Kripke. Kripke proved the completeness of quantified $\mathsf{S5}$ in 1959, and in 1963 he gave the semantics of frames with an accessibility relation for the normal propositional logics; his semantics for intuitionistic logic followed in 1965. Hintikka's *Knowledge and Belief* (1962) gave epistemic logic its possible-worlds form. The canonical model method was developed during the 1960s, and notes by Lemmon and Scott from that time were published in 1977. Filtration, in its relational form, comes from Lemmon in 1966 and from Segerberg, whose *An Essay in Classical Modal Logic* (1971) made the method systematic. Harrop's criterion for decidability dates from 1958.

Scott and Montague introduced neighbourhood semantics independently in 1970, and Chellas's *Modal Logic: An Introduction* (1980) made it standard. Van Benthem's dissertation of 1976 founded correspondence theory and the study of modal logic as a fragment of first-order logic. Ladner proved in 1977 that satisfiability for the logics between $\mathsf{K}$ and $\mathsf{S4}$ is PSPACE-hard, and that it is NP-complete for $\mathsf{S5}$. Halpern and Moses extended these bounds to logics of knowledge and belief with several agents in 1992.

## Further reading

- Patrick Blackburn, Maarten de Rijke and Yde Venema, *Modal Logic* (Cambridge University Press, 2001). The standard graduate text, with full proofs of canonicity, filtration, the standard translation and the complexity results.
- Brian F. Chellas, *Modal Logic: An Introduction* (Cambridge University Press, 1980). A careful first course that treats normal logics and neighbourhood semantics side by side.
- G. E. Hughes and M. J. Cresswell, *A New Introduction to Modal Logic* (Routledge, 1996). An unhurried account of the standard systems, their axioms and completeness proofs, with many worked countermodels.
- Alexander Chagrov and Michael Zakharyaschev, *Modal Logic* (Oxford University Press, 1997). The reference for the lattice of modal logics, the finite model property, incompleteness and decidability.
- The Open Logic Project, *Boxes and Diamonds: An Open Introduction to Modal Logic*. A free textbook that covers frames, the standard systems, canonical models and filtrations at the level of this chapter.
