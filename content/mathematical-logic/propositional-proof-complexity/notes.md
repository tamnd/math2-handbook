## History

Resolution came first as a method of proof search. Martin Davis and Hilary Putnam worked with clauses in 1960, and John Alan Robinson made resolution the basis of automated theorem proving in 1965. G. S. Tseitin looked at the lengths of such proofs before the field had a name: in a paper published in 1968 he proved lower bounds for regular resolution, using the parity formulas on graphs that now carry his name, and he introduced the extension rule, which lets a proof name a formula by a new variable.

Stephen Cook and Robert Reckhow gave the subject its frame in "The relative efficiency of propositional proof systems", in the *Journal of Symbolic Logic* in 1979. They defined a proof system as a polynomial-time function onto the tautologies, defined p-simulation, and proved that a polynomially bounded proof system exists exactly when NP equals coNP. Reckhow's thesis at Toronto, of 1976, showed that all Frege systems are p-equivalent. The 1979 paper also gave extended Frege proofs of polynomial size for the pigeonhole formulas and asked whether Frege systems have them. Cook had linked extended Frege with his feasible arithmetic PV in 1975.

The first exponential lower bound for general resolution was Armin Haken's, in "The intractability of resolution" of 1985, for the pigeonhole formulas. Alasdair Urquhart proved exponential bounds for the Tseitin formulas on expanders in "Hard examples for resolution" in 1987. In the same year Samuel Buss answered the question of Cook and Reckhow with polynomial-size Frege proofs of the pigeonhole principle, and William Cook, Collette Coullard and György Turán showed that cutting planes refutes the same formulas in polynomial size. Miklós Ajtai proved in 1988 that bounded-depth Frege has no polynomial-size proofs of them, with methods from nonstandard models of arithmetic, and Krajíček, Pudlák and Woods, and independently Pitassi, Beame and Impagliazzo, made the bound exponential in the early 1990s with switching lemmas.

The 1990s brought the methods that are used most today. Beame, Impagliazzo, Krajíček, Pitassi and Pudlák introduced the Nullstellensatz system, and Clegg, Edmonds and Impagliazzo introduced the polynomial calculus in 1996; Razborov proved the degree bound for the pigeonhole principle in 1998. Jan Krajíček introduced feasible interpolation in a paper published in 1997, and Pavel Pudlák used its monotone form with real circuits to prove an exponential lower bound for cutting planes in the same year. Eli Ben-Sasson and Avi Wigderson's "Short proofs are narrow: resolution made simple" (1999) reduced most resolution lower bounds to bounds on width.

The question of finding proofs grew alongside. Krajíček and Pudlák showed in 1998 that extended Frege has no feasible interpolation unless RSA is insecure, which also rules out its automatizability under the same assumption. Alekhnovich and Razborov showed that resolution is not automatizable unless FPT = W[P], and Albert Atserias and Moritz Müller proved in 2019 that it is not automatizable unless P = NP. Lower bounds for Frege systems remain open, as they have been since 1979.

## Further reading

- Jan Krajíček, *Proof Complexity* (2019). The most complete account of the field, from resolution and the algebraic systems to Frege systems, interpolation and the links with bounded arithmetic.
- Jan Krajíček, *Bounded Arithmetic, Propositional Logic, and Complexity Theory* (1995). The standard text on the correspondence between theories of arithmetic and proof systems, with the classical lower bounds.
- Stephen Cook and Phuong Nguyen, *Logical Foundations of Proof Complexity* (2010). Develops theories of bounded arithmetic and their propositional translations in detail, with extended Frege and its relatives.
- Alasdair Urquhart, "The complexity of propositional proofs", *Bulletin of Symbolic Logic* (1995). A short survey of the systems and lower bounds up to the mid-1990s, written for logicians.
- Paul Beame and Toniann Pitassi, "Propositional proof complexity: past, present, and future" (1998). A survey of resolution, bounded-depth Frege, cutting planes and the algebraic systems, with the main proof techniques.
