## History

Arthur Prior founded tense logic in *Time and Modality* (1957), with the operators "it will always be" and "it has always been", and he studied which axioms fit which pictures of time. Hans Kamp proved in his UCLA thesis *Tense Logic and the Theory of Linear Order* (1968) that since and until express every first-order property of a point over complete linear orders. Amir Pnueli brought temporal logic to computer science in "The temporal logic of programs" (1977), where linear time describes the runs of programs that never stop. Edmund Clarke and Allen Emerson introduced computation tree logic and model checking in 1981, and Jean-Pierre Queille and Joseph Sifakis found model checking independently in 1982; Emerson and Joseph Halpern defined CTL* in 1983. Pnueli received the Turing Award in 1996, and Clarke, Emerson and Sifakis shared it in 2007. James Allen classified the thirteen relations between intervals in 1983, and Halpern and Yoav Shoham defined their interval logic in 1986.

Jaakko Hintikka's *Knowledge and Belief* (1962) read knowledge and belief as boxes and argued from the semantics of possible worlds. David Lewis defined common knowledge in *Convention* (1969), and Robert Aumann gave it a formal treatment for game theory in "Agreeing to disagree" (1976). Fagin, Halpern, Moses and Vardi made epistemic logic a tool for distributed computing, with the analysis of the coordinated attack problem. Jan Plaza introduced public announcement logic in 1989, and Alexandru Baltag, Lawrence Moss and Sławomir Solecki generalized announcements to action models in 1998.

Ernst Mally wrote a first logic of obligation in *Grundgesetze des Sollens* (1926), but the modern subject began with Georg Henrik von Wright's paper "Deontic logic" in *Mind* (1951). Alf Ross published his paradox in 1941 and Roderick Chisholm his paradox of contrary-to-duty obligations in 1963. Frank Ramsey's footnote of 1929 on evaluating conditionals by adding the antecedent to one's beliefs led to Robert Stalnaker's semantics of 1968 and to David Lewis's *Counterfactuals* (1973). Nuel Belnap and Michael Perloff started the logic of seeing to it in 1988.

Gödel suggested in a lecture of 1938 that provability might be analysed through explicit proofs, and Sergei Artemov built the logic of proofs on this idea in the 1990s. Carlos Alchourrón, Peter Gärdenfors and David Makinson published their postulates for belief revision in 1985, Adam Grove gave the sphere semantics in 1988, and Gärdenfors proved his triviality theorem in the *Philosophical Review* in 1986. Frederic Fitch published the knowability argument in "A logical analysis of some value concepts" in the *Journal of Symbolic Logic* (1963) and credited it to an anonymous referee, later identified as Alonzo Church. Inquisitive logic was axiomatized by Ivano Ciardelli and Floris Roelofsen in 2011, and $\mathcal{ALC}$ was introduced by Manfred Schmidt-Schauß and Gert Smolka in 1991.

## Further reading

- Christel Baier and Joost-Pieter Katoen, *Principles of Model Checking* (2008). LTL, CTL and their model checking algorithms, with automata and many worked systems.
- Ronald Fagin, Joseph Y. Halpern, Yoram Moses and Moshe Y. Vardi, *Reasoning About Knowledge* (1995). The standard account of epistemic logic, common knowledge and the muddy children, with applications to distributed systems.
- Hans van Ditmarsch, Wiebe van der Hoek and Barteld Kooi, *Dynamic Epistemic Logic* (2007). Public announcements, action models and their reduction axioms, from first definitions to completeness proofs.
- David Lewis, *Counterfactuals* (1973). The sphere semantics and the logic $\mathsf{VC}$, argued in plain prose.
- Sergei Artemov and Melvin Fitting, *Justification Logic: Reasoning with Reasons* (2019). The logic of proofs, its models and the realization theorem.
- Peter Gärdenfors, *Knowledge in Flux* (1988). The AGM postulates, epistemic entrenchment and the triviality result for the Ramsey test.
