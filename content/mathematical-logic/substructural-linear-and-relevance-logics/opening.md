In the sequent calculus for intuitionistic logic, $p \Rightarrow p \wedge p$ is derivable: copy the hypothesis $p$, then prove each half of the conjunction from one copy. So is $p, q \Rightarrow p$: add a hypothesis $q$ that the proof never uses. And $p, q \Rightarrow r$ is derivable exactly when $q, p \Rightarrow r$ is. These three steps do not touch any connective. They are the *structural rules*: contraction merges two copies of a hypothesis, weakening adds a hypothesis, and exchange swaps two neighbouring hypotheses. A *substructural logic* is what remains when some of them are dropped.

Each rule can fail for a reason. In a grammar, the order of words matters. Give "the" the type $np/n$, "dog" the type $n$ and "sleeps" the type $np \backslash s$. Then the list of types for "the dog sleeps" derives the sentence type $s$ in Lambek's calculus, and the list for "dog the sleeps" does not, because the calculus has no exchange. In a logic of resources, a hypothesis is used up. One coin gives one coffee, but it does not give two coffees, and a coin that the proof does not spend cannot be ignored. That is the reading of linear logic, which has no contraction and no weakening and brings both back, under control, for formulas marked $!A$. In a logic of relevance, $p \rightarrow (q \rightarrow p)$ is rejected, because $q$ plays no part in getting $p$. That is the motive for the relevance logic $\mathbf{R}$, which has contraction but no weakening.

The family has a common base and a common algebra. The full Lambek calculus has no structural rule, and its models are the residuated lattices, ordered monoids with two residuals of the product. In these models each structural rule is one equation: exchange is $xy = yx$, weakening is $x \le 1$, and contraction is $x \le x^2$. Adding all three gives back intuitionistic logic. Dropping rules changes more than the set of theorems. Provability in the multiplicative fragment of linear logic is NP-complete, it is undecidable in full linear logic and in $\mathbf{R}$, and it becomes decidable again when weakening is added to linear logic.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in six groups, read in order.

1. **The structural rules.** [Substructural logic](/ZZKTJY5J4T/) names the family and lists its members by the rules they keep. [Weakening](/VHRXX0T5XT/), [contraction](/V0MZVC2XA9/) and [exchange](/YAFD18WYJ8/) are each given as a sequent rule, a Hilbert axiom and an equation, with sequents that each logic proves or refutes.
2. **The Lambek calculi.** The [full Lambek calculus](/V5XVVN16GS/) $\mathsf{FL}$ and its extensions $\mathsf{FL}_e$, $\mathsf{FL}_w$, $\mathsf{FL}_c$ and $\mathsf{FL}_{ew}$, and the [Lambek calculus](/5FAC9KX4H0/) $\mathsf{L}$ of categorial grammar.
3. **Linear logic and its models.** [Linear logic](/2P2184B6HE/) with its multiplicative, additive and exponential connectives. [Proof nets](/HN6WN1VE39/) and the Danos–Regnier criterion, [phase semantics](/64MHHQQ6G6/) in commutative monoids, and [coherence spaces](/WSGZ5HGK1X/) with stable functions.
4. **Complexity of linear logic.** The [decidability of linear logic](/BNRWJB2R8C/) fragment by fragment, and the [light linear logics](/E7JVJSSWBT/) ELL, LLL and SLL, whose proofs compute the elementary or the polynomial-time functions.
5. **Relevance logic.** [Relevance logic](/KXTCZ2FY5M/) and the variable sharing principle, [Routley–Meyer semantics](/H4Z8J0KMAY/) with its ternary relation and star, and [Urquhart's undecidability theorem](/X7F69S7QPX/).
6. **More substructural logics.** [Affine logic](/K67C2N2QAH/), the [bunched logic](/5JKHNK7AR5/) of separation logic, [abelian logic](/W3CPQAFQJ3/), and [ludics](/S4PWX2MFM2/).

## A first reading

For a first reading, take these entries in order: [substructural logic](/ZZKTJY5J4T/), [weakening](/VHRXX0T5XT/), [contraction](/V0MZVC2XA9/), [exchange](/YAFD18WYJ8/), [full Lambek calculus](/V5XVVN16GS/), [Lambek calculus](/5FAC9KX4H0/), [linear logic](/2P2184B6HE/), [proof net](/HN6WN1VE39/), [phase semantics](/64MHHQQ6G6/), [relevance logic](/KXTCZ2FY5M/), [Routley–Meyer semantics](/H4Z8J0KMAY/) and [affine logic](/K67C2N2QAH/). The attached examples matter more than usual here. Most of them take one small sequent and show, by a cut-free search or by a small model, that a given logic does or does not prove it, and the exercises ask for variations of these arguments.

Coherence spaces, the two complexity entries, Urquhart's theorem, bunched logic, abelian logic and ludics can wait for a second pass. The complexity entries assume some complexity theory, and ludics assumes linear logic and the idea of focused proof search.

## Before you start

You need the [sequent calculus](/W72CA3R9MP/), in particular [Gentzen's calculi LK and LJ](/PZX9J3VEMT/) with their structural rules, and the [cut-elimination theorem](/AYRZ98Y0NW/); the chapter on [structural proof theory](/R10RMQCJQX/) has all three. Many arguments compare a substructural logic with [intuitionistic logic](/Q11N8X99FK/), so read the chapter on [intuitionistic and intermediate logics](/38EQ20GR7M/) first, including [Heyting algebras](/NG943XNQWH/) and [Kripke semantics](/NGHWZ9Y7ZV/), which Routley–Meyer semantics extends. For the algebra you need [monoids](/0N6R902FG1/) and [residuated lattices](/E337A6WVH3/). The [paradoxes of material implication](/3T7ZJFC9QF/) explain the motive of relevance logic. For the complexity entries, the classes [NP](/SBDQH0TJ5X/) and [PSPACE](/XNRQHTG7QM/) are enough.
