## History

Formal logic begins with Aristotle. His *Prior Analytics* sets out the syllogism, classifies the arguments by figure, and proves the valid moods of the second and third figures by reducing them to the four perfect moods of the first. The fourth figure was added later; it is often credited to Galen, but the attribution is doubtful. The Stoics, above all Chrysippus, built a separate logic of whole propositions, with five indemonstrable argument forms that include modus ponens and modus tollens. The syllogistic, with the square of opposition and the medieval names of the moods, stayed at the centre of logic teaching until the nineteenth century.

The modern subject starts with algebra. In 1847 George Boole published *The Mathematical Analysis of Logic* and Augustus De Morgan published *Formal Logic*, and Boole's *An Investigation of the Laws of Thought* followed in 1854. Gottlob Frege's *Begriffsschrift* of 1879 gave the first formal system in the modern sense, with quantifiers, axioms and a rule of inference, and with proofs that could be checked step by step. Charles Sanders Peirce developed quantifiers independently in the 1880s, and in the late 1890s he designed the existential graphs, a logic of diagrams.

Hilbert turned consistency into a mathematical question. His *Grundlagen der Geometrie* of 1899 showed that Euclidean geometry is consistent if the arithmetic of the reals is, following the pattern of Beltrami's 1868 model of hyperbolic geometry inside Euclidean geometry. In the 1920s he and his collaborators, among them Paul Bernays and Wilhelm Ackermann, set out the program of proving the consistency of mathematics by finitary means. Emil Post's dissertation of 1921 proved that classical propositional logic is consistent and complete in the strong sense that now bears his name. Gödel proved the completeness of first-order logic in 1929 and published it in 1930, so that consistency and having a model became the same thing, and in 1931 his second incompleteness theorem showed that no consistent theory of this kind proves its own consistency. Gentzen's consistency proof for arithmetic of 1936, by induction up to the ordinal $\varepsilon_0$, showed what can still be done.

The metalanguage became explicit with Tarski's work on truth in formalized languages of 1933, and relative consistency became a main tool of set theory: Gödel used the constructible universe in 1938 to show that the axiom of choice and the generalized continuum hypothesis are consistent with ZF, and Paul Cohen proved in 1963 that their negations are too. The abstract study of logics came later. Per Lindström characterized first-order logic in 1969 by compactness and the downward Löwenheim–Skolem property, and Joseph Goguen and Rod Burstall published the theory of institutions in 1992. Tarski proposed permutation invariance as the mark of logical notions in a lecture of 1966, published in 1986, and Gila Sher extended the idea in *The Bounds of Logic* (1991).

## Further reading

- Herbert B. Enderton, *A Mathematical Introduction to Logic*, second edition (2001). A clear first course that defines formal systems, soundness and consistency with care and proves the main theorems.
- Elliott Mendelson, *Introduction to Mathematical Logic*, sixth edition (2015). Its Hilbert-style systems and detailed derivations match the formal side of this chapter.
- Joseph R. Shoenfield, *Mathematical Logic* (1967). The standard source for conservative extensions and extensions by definitions, with a precise proof of eliminability.
- Jean van Heijenoort (editor), *From Frege to Gödel: A Source Book in Mathematical Logic, 1879–1931* (1967). The founding papers in English, from the *Begriffsschrift* to the incompleteness theorems, each with an introduction.
- William Kneale and Martha Kneale, *The Development of Logic* (1962). A history of logic from Aristotle and the Stoics to the twentieth century.
