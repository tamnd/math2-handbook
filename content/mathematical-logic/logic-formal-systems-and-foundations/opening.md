A proof in a textbook is written for a reader who fills small gaps. A proof in a formal system has no gaps: it is a finite list of formulas, each an axiom or the result of a rule applied to earlier lines, and a short program can check it. In the [Hilbert system](/1MGMQX43B8/) of propositional logic, the formula $p \to p$ takes five lines. That seems a poor trade, but it makes proofs into mathematical objects. Once proofs are objects, we can ask questions about all of them at once: can this theory prove $0 = 1$? Does adding a new symbol let us prove anything new about the old ones? Can a theory prove its own consistency?

This chapter sets up the words in which such questions are asked. It starts with the oldest formal logic, Aristotle's syllogistic, and then gives the modern notions: [formal system](/439M8B4DY2/), [consequence relation](/J78Y1M4GND/), [consistency](/VM8TQ70CVJ/), [soundness](/4A84VHDTHV/), [conservative extension](/N238J43XNE/) and [relative consistency](/X3G0RG2GPZ/). The main theorems about these notions, such as completeness and incompleteness, come in later chapters; here they appear as the reasons the notions matter.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in seven groups, read in order.

1. **What logic studies.** [Mathematical logic](/DYJ0KGTPMR/) and its four branches, the [foundations of mathematics](/EFFRJFW67Y/), and the [history of the subject](/WP3602P9EH/) from Boole and Frege to Gödel, Tarski and Gentzen.
2. **Aristotle's syllogistic.** The four sentence forms A, E, I, O, the [syllogism](/MZBNX2T6Q6/) itself, the [square of opposition](/VYNBSVZ2TH/), and the [figures](/S4BTTGFR6J/) and [moods](/1TD6HRM5NG/). Of the 256 forms, 24 are valid in the traditional reading and 15 in the modern one; the difference is existential import.
3. **Formal systems and their rules.** A [formal system](/439M8B4DY2/) has an alphabet, formation rules, axioms and rules of inference, each of them decidable. The [object language and the metalanguage](/D9G8M42GKC/) must be kept apart, and a [rule of inference](/QPD3X471Q1/) can be derivable or only admissible.
4. **Consequence in the abstract.** A [consequence relation](/J78Y1M4GND/) is a relation $\Gamma \vdash \varphi$ with reflexivity, monotonicity and cut. [Abstract logical systems](/RQXV7PM2WT/) compare whole logics, through institutions and through [Lindström's theorem](/FYZ3CJGZYV/).
5. **Consistency, completeness and soundness.** [Consistency](/VM8TQ70CVJ/), [Post consistency and Post completeness](/WK2P0E82B4/), and [soundness](/4A84VHDTHV/). Classical propositional logic is Post complete but its empty theory is not negation complete, and these are different senses of "complete".
6. **Comparing and extending theories.** [Conservative extensions](/N238J43XNE/), [extensions by definitions](/GCK0NN60E4/), [relative consistency proofs](/X3G0RG2GPZ/) and [Hilbert's program](/P1RW6JWX73/), which asked for a finitary proof that mathematics is consistent.
7. **Other views of logic.** Peirce's [existential graphs](/D3ZS6WHPM1/), a diagrammatic logic with the same theorems as propositional and first-order logic, and the question of which notions are [logical constants](/2QQ7Q5SKP8/).

## A first reading

For a first reading, take these nine entries in order: [mathematical logic](/DYJ0KGTPMR/), [Aristotelian syllogistic](/MZBNX2T6Q6/), [formal system](/439M8B4DY2/), [object language and metalanguage](/D9G8M42GKC/), [rule of inference](/QPD3X471Q1/), [consistency](/VM8TQ70CVJ/), [soundness](/4A84VHDTHV/), [conservative extension](/N238J43XNE/) and [relative consistency proof](/X3G0RG2GPZ/). They give the vocabulary that the rest of the volume uses without comment. The figures and moods of the syllogism, abstract logical systems, existential graphs and logicality can wait; they repay a second reading once the later chapters have shown completeness and incompleteness at work. [Hilbert's program](/P1RW6JWX73/) reads best after the chapter on the incompleteness theorems, though its first sections need nothing beyond this chapter.

## Before you start

No earlier chapter of this volume is needed. The area [reasoning and proof](/SGSVYFDSYK/) helps, in particular [the language of mathematical statements](/WMY13MXY3W/) and [proof, theorems and mathematical method](/N4P3E53HM0/). Some entries use the connectives and quantifiers of the next chapter, [propositions, connectives and quantifiers](/PM1AYWABA7/); if $\to$, $\forall$ or a truth table is new to you, read the first groups of that chapter first. A few examples mention [Peano arithmetic](/HACXBCM7SS/) and [Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory](/0HE5WDQ9FJ/); you need only their names and what they are about, since later chapters treat them in full. Simple facts about sets, functions and induction are used freely.
