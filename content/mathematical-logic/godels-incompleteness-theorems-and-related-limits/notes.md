## History

Gödel announced the first incompleteness theorem at a conference in Königsberg in September 1930, and published both theorems in 1931 in "Über formal unentscheidbare Sätze der Principia Mathematica und verwandter Systeme I". The paper introduced the numbering of formulas by prime powers, the primitive recursive functions in nearly their modern form, and the $\beta$-function. Gödel proved the first theorem for $\omega$-consistent systems and only sketched the second, promising a second part that never appeared. He also noticed, by 1930, that arithmetical truth cannot be defined in arithmetic, and said so in a letter to von Neumann in 1931. Tarski published the undefinability theorem in 1933.

Rosser removed the hypothesis of $\omega$-consistency in 1936, in "Extensions of some theorems of Gödel and Church". The first complete proof of the second theorem appeared in the second volume of Hilbert and Bernays's *Grundlagen der Mathematik* (1939), which isolated the properties of the provability predicate that the proof needs. Carnap stated the diagonal lemma in general form in 1934; Gödel had used a special case. In 1952 Henkin asked whether a sentence that says of itself that it is provable is provable, and Löb answered in 1955, in "Solution of a problem of Leon Henkin", with the theorem that bears his name and the derivability conditions in their present form.

Turing's thesis "Systems of logic based on ordinals" (1939) asked whether adding consistency statements along ordinal notations could overcome incompleteness. Feferman took up the question in "Transfinite recursive progressions of axiomatic theories" (1962), proving completeness for the progression based on uniform reflection, and with Spector he showed in the same year how badly such progressions depend on the choice of notations. Feferman's "Arithmetization of metamathematics in a general setting" (1960) showed that the second theorem depends on how the axioms are described. In 1976 Solovay proved that the modal logic GL describes exactly what $\mathsf{PA}$ proves about its own provability predicate.

Gödel's 1936 note "Über die Länge von Beweisen" stated the speed-up theorem without proof; Parikh and others later gave proofs and sharper forms. Kreisel and Lévy studied reflection principles systematically in 1968. The search for natural independent statements led to the theorem of Paris and Harrington in 1977 and to the result of Kirby and Paris in 1982 that Goodstein's theorem is unprovable in $\mathsf{PA}$. Willard's self-verifying theories, published in 2001 and 2002, showed how weak a theory must be to prove its own consistency in a restricted sense.

## Further reading

- Peter Smith, *An Introduction to Gödel's Theorems*, 2nd edition (2013). A complete and careful textbook account of both theorems, Rosser's theorem, Löb's theorem and Tarski's theorem, starting from very little.
- Torkel Franzén, *Gödel's Theorem: An Incomplete Guide to Its Use and Abuse* (2005). Explains what the theorems say and corrects the many misreadings of them outside logic.
- Craig Smoryński, "The incompleteness theorems", in Jon Barwise (ed.), *Handbook of Mathematical Logic* (1977). A survey of the theorems, the derivability conditions and their refinements at a research level.
- George Boolos, *The Logic of Provability* (1993). The modal logic of the provability predicate, from the derivability conditions and Löb's theorem to Solovay's completeness theorem.
- Petr Hájek and Pavel Pudlák, *Metamathematics of First-Order Arithmetic* (1993). The reference work for the arithmetization of syntax, partial truth predicates, reflection and the length of proofs in fragments of arithmetic.
