## History

Reuben Goodstein proved in 1944, in "On the restricted ordinal theorem" in the Journal of Symbolic Logic, that his sequences always reach $0$. His proof used ordinals below $\varepsilon_0$, and nobody then asked whether this was necessary. The question became pressing after Gödel, because his unprovable sentences were about provability, and it was natural to hope that the theorems of ordinary number theory were all within reach of $\mathsf{PA}$.

The first natural counterexample came in 1977. Jeff Paris and Leo Harrington published "A mathematical incompleteness in Peano arithmetic" in the Handbook of Mathematical Logic, edited by Jon Barwise. Their method grew out of work by Paris and Laurie Kirby on cuts in non-standard models and on indicators. In 1981 Jussi Ketonen and Robert Solovay gave a direct combinatorial account of the growth of the Paris-Harrington function in "Rapidly growing Ramsey functions". In 1982 Kirby and Paris published "Accessible independence results for Peano arithmetic" in the Bulletin of the London Mathematical Society. It introduced the hydra game and proved that Goodstein's theorem is not provable in $\mathsf{PA}$. In 1983 E. A. Cichon gave a short proof of the Goodstein result through the Hardy hierarchy. Akihiro Kanamori and Kenneth McAloon added the principle on regressive colourings in 1987, in the Annals of Pure and Applied Logic.

The hierarchies behind these proofs are older. Stanley Wainer introduced the Hardy hierarchy in 1972; the name comes from G. H. Hardy, whose paper of 1904 contains the idea of the definition. The fast-growing hierarchy that calibrates the provably total functions is also called the Schwichtenberg-Wainer hierarchy, after Helmut Schwichtenberg and Wainer. Georg Kreisel had shown that the provably total functions of $\mathsf{PA}$ are those defined by recursion below $\varepsilon_0$. Charles Parsons and Grigori Mints identified the provably total functions of $\mathrm{I}\Sigma_1$ with the primitive recursive functions. Jean-Yves Girard in 1981, and Cichon and Wainer in 1983, studied the slow-growing hierarchy and when it catches up with the fast-growing one. Andreas Weiermann later showed how strongly it depends on the fundamental sequences.

Statements beyond $\mathsf{PA}$ followed. Joseph Kruskal proved his tree theorem in 1960, and in the early 1980s Harvey Friedman showed that a finite form of it is not provable in $\mathsf{ATR}_0$. Neil Robertson and Paul Seymour proved the graph minor theorem in the series Graph Minors, published from 1983 to 2004. In 1987 Friedman, Robertson and Seymour published "The metamathematics of the graph minor theorem", with the finite form and its unprovability in $\Pi^1_1\text{-}\mathsf{CA}_0$. In the same year Wilfried Buchholz published "An independence result for $(\Pi^1_1\text{-}\mathsf{CA}) + \mathsf{BI}$", with the labelled hydras.

Lev Beklemishev found the worm principle in work on Japaridze's polymodal provability logic $\mathsf{GLP}$. "The worm principle" appeared as a Utrecht preprint in 2003 and was published in Logic Colloquium '02, in the Lecture Notes in Logic, in 2006.

## Further reading

- Richard Kaye, *Models of Peano Arithmetic*. A textbook on non-standard models of arithmetic, with cuts, indicators and the Paris-Harrington theorem.
- Petr Hájek and Pavel Pudlák, *Metamathematics of First-Order Arithmetic*. The reference for fragments of arithmetic, provably total functions and the combinatorial independence results.
- Helmut Schwichtenberg and Stanley S. Wainer, *Proofs and Computations*. Proof theory with the fast-growing and Hardy hierarchies, and the classification of provably total functions.
- Stephen G. Simpson, *Subsystems of Second Order Arithmetic*. The systems $\mathsf{ATR}_0$ and $\Pi^1_1\text{-}\mathsf{CA}_0$ that the tree and graph statements escape.
- Ronald L. Graham, Bruce L. Rothschild and Joel H. Spencer, *Ramsey Theory*. The finite and infinite Ramsey theorems behind the Ramsey-type statements of the chapter.
