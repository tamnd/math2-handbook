## History

Aristotle analyzed sentences into a subject and a predicate and studied the forms "every $A$ is $B$" and "some $A$ is $B$". In Book IV of the *Metaphysics* he defends the law of noncontradiction as the firmest of all principles, and in *On Interpretation* he discusses whether "there will be a sea battle tomorrow" already has a truth value, the problem that later led Łukasiewicz to a third value. The Stoics built a logic of whole propositions. Philo of Megara gave the truth condition of the material conditional, and Chrysippus listed five indemonstrable argument forms, among them modus ponens, modus tollens and disjunctive syllogism.

The algebra of logic came in the nineteenth century. George Boole published *The Mathematical Analysis of Logic* in 1847 and *An Investigation of the Laws of Thought* in 1854, and Augustus De Morgan's *Formal Logic* of 1847 stated the laws that carry his name. Boole's sum applied only to disjoint classes; William Stanley Jevons argued in 1864 for the inclusive "or" that mathematics uses today. In 1895 Lewis Carroll's dialogue "What the Tortoise Said to Achilles" showed that a rule of inference cannot be replaced by one more premise.

Quantifiers arrived with Gottlob Frege's *Begriffsschrift* of 1879, which wrote generality with a bound variable and a scope and expressed "some" as "not all not". Charles Sanders Peirce and his student Oscar Howard Mitchell introduced quantifiers independently in the 1880s. Giuseppe Peano wrote arithmetic in symbols in 1889, Bertrand Russell analyzed "the" as existence plus uniqueness in 1905, and Whitehead and Russell's *Principia Mathematica* (1910 to 1913) carried large parts of mathematics into symbolic form.

The truth table method and the study of connectives took shape around 1920. Peirce had noticed about 1880 that a single connective suffices, Henry Sheffer published the stroke in 1913, and Jean Nicod gave a single axiom for propositional logic with it in 1917. In 1921 Wittgenstein's *Tractatus* and Emil Post's paper both used truth tables, and Wittgenstein introduced the word "tautology" in its modern sense. Jan Łukasiewicz described a three-valued logic in 1920. On the other side, Brouwer rejected the general law of excluded middle, and Arend Heyting formalized intuitionistic logic in 1930; the tables in this chapter of which laws survive come from that work.

## Further reading

- Daniel J. Velleman, *How to Prove It: A Structured Approach*, third edition (2019). The first chapters cover connectives, truth tables and quantifiers with many exercises, and lead straight into proof writing.
- Herbert B. Enderton, *A Mathematical Introduction to Logic*, second edition (2001). Its chapter on sentential logic treats truth tables, tautologies and complete sets of connectives with full proofs.
- Elliott Mendelson, *Introduction to Mathematical Logic*, sixth edition (2015). A good source for normal forms, the adequacy of connectives and the step to a formal propositional calculus.
- Raymond Smullyan, *What Is the Name of This Book?* (1978). The book that made knights and knaves famous, with puzzles that train exactly the biconditional reasoning of the last entry.
