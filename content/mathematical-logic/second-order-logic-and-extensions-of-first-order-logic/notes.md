## History

Quantifiers over concepts were part of logic from the start. Gottlob Frege's *Begriffsschrift* of 1879 quantified over concepts as freely as over objects, and Richard Dedekind's *Was sind und was sollen die Zahlen?* of 1888 proved that his axioms for the natural numbers, with induction over all sets, determine $\N$ up to isomorphism. Frege stated Hume's principle in *Die Grundlagen der Arithmetik* of 1884, quoting Hume's *Treatise*, but in the *Grundgesetze der Arithmetik* (1893 and 1903) he derived arithmetic from Basic Law V, which Russell's letter of 1902 showed to be inconsistent. Charles Parsons observed in 1965 that Hume's principle alone suffices, Crispin Wright carried out the derivation in the early 1980s, and Richard Heck showed in 1993 that Frege's own derivation uses Basic Law V essentially only to prove it. George Boolos gave the principle its name.

The paradoxes led to type theory. Whitehead and Russell's ramified theory was simplified, by a proposal of Leon Chwistek and of Frank Ramsey, to the simple theory of types, and Alonzo Church gave it its modern form with the lambda calculus in 1940. Hermann Weyl's *Das Kontinuum* of 1918 developed analysis from predicative definitions only. In 1950 Leon Henkin introduced general models for the theory of types and proved completeness for them, and in 1955 Jaakko Hintikka reduced the standard semantics of every finite order to second order. Whether full second-order logic is logic at all became a philosophical question: W. V. Quine, in *Philosophy of Logic* (1970), called it "set theory in sheep's clothing", and Boolos answered in 1984, in "To be is to be a value of a variable (or to be some values of some variables)", with the plural reading of monadic second-order variables.

The monadic fragment found its main use in computer science. J. Richard Büchi in 1960, Calvin Elgot in 1961 and Boris Trakhtenbrot independently proved that monadic second-order logic on words defines exactly the regular languages. Büchi proved in 1962 that the monadic theory of the natural numbers with successor is decidable, by automata on infinite words, and Michael Rabin extended this to the infinite binary tree in 1969. Ronald Fagin's theorem of the 1970s identified existential second-order logic on finite structures with the class NP, and Bruno Courcelle's theorem of 1990 gave linear-time algorithms for monadic properties of graphs of bounded treewidth.

The quantifiers themselves were generalized. Andrzej Mostowski defined cardinality quantifiers in 1957, and Per Lindström gave the general definition in 1966 and in 1969 proved his characterization of first-order logic. Henkin introduced branching quantifiers at a symposium in Warsaw in 1959, published in 1961, and Herbert Enderton and Wilbur Walkoe showed in 1970 that they reach exactly existential second-order logic. H. Jerome Keisler proved the completeness theorem for "uncountably many" in 1970. Jon Barwise and Robin Cooper brought generalized quantifiers into linguistics in 1981. Hintikka and Gabriel Sandu introduced independence-friendly logic in 1989, Wilfrid Hodges gave it a compositional team semantics in 1997, and Jouko Väänänen's *Dependence Logic* of 2007 made dependence an atomic formula.

## Further reading

- Herbert B. Enderton, *A Mathematical Introduction to Logic*, second edition (2001). Chapter 4 treats second-order logic in both semantics, with the many-sorted translation.
- Stewart Shapiro, *Foundations without Foundationalism: A Case for Second-Order Logic* (1991). The standard account of the full and Henkin semantics, their metatheory, and the case for second-order logic in the foundations of mathematics.
- Heinz-Dieter Ebbinghaus and Jörg Flum, *Finite Model Theory*, second edition (1999). Monadic second-order logic on words and graphs, Fagin's theorem and the games used to prove that properties are not definable.
- Jouko Väänänen, *Dependence Logic* (2007). Team semantics, dependence atoms and the equivalence with existential second-order logic, written as a textbook.
- Stanley Peters and Dag Westerståhl, *Quantifiers in Language and Logic* (2006). Generalized quantifiers from the logical and the linguistic side, including conservativity and Lindström's theorem.
