## History

Axioms for the natural numbers came from Dedekind, in *Was sind und was sollen die Zahlen?* (1888), and from Peano, in *Arithmetices principia* (1889). Both stated induction for arbitrary sets, so their systems are second-order and determine the natural numbers up to isomorphism. The first-order system now called Peano arithmetic, with one induction axiom for each formula, took shape in the 1920s and 1930s as logicians separated first-order logic from its stronger relatives. Skolem showed in 1934 that no first-order theory true in $\N$ rules out non-standard models.

Skolem had already, in 1923, developed arithmetic with free variables and recursive definitions, without quantifiers, in order to avoid assumptions about the infinite. This system grew into primitive recursive arithmetic, and Tait argued in "Finitism" (1981) that it captures exactly the finitist reasoning of Hilbert's program. Gödel's paper of 1931 on incompleteness introduced the $\beta$-function, to show that definitions by recursion can be expressed with only addition and multiplication. Gentzen proved the consistency of Peano arithmetic in 1936 by induction up to the ordinal $\varepsilon_0$.

The search for the weakest theory to which Gödel's theorems apply led Raphael Robinson to the finitely axiomatized theory $\mathsf{Q}$ in 1950. Tarski, Mostowski and Robinson presented it in *Undecidable Theories* (1953), together with the still weaker theory $\mathsf{R}$. Grzegorczyk proposed in 2005 a theory of concatenation of strings as another base theory, and he and Zdanowski showed that it too is essentially undecidable. The arithmetical hierarchy of formulas and sets was introduced by Kleene in 1943 and independently by Mostowski in 1947. Ryll-Nardzewski proved in 1952 that Peano arithmetic is not finitely axiomatizable.

The study of fragments of Peano arithmetic became a field of its own in the 1970s. Parikh's theorem on bounded induction, of 1971, showed that $\mathrm{I}\Delta_0$ cannot prove that exponentiation is total. Kirby and Paris in 1978 compared induction and collection schemes for each class $\Sigma_n$, and the independence result of Paris and Harrington (1977) gave a combinatorial statement true in $\N$ but unprovable in $\mathsf{PA}$. Kalmár's elementary functions turned out to be exactly the provably total functions of elementary function arithmetic, and the theory became the base theory of choice in the study of how much arithmetic ordinary mathematics needs.

## Further reading

- Richard Kaye, *Models of Peano Arithmetic* (1991). The standard introduction to non-standard models, with careful treatments of $\mathsf{Q}$, the fragments $\mathrm{I}\Sigma_n$ and the non-finite axiomatizability of $\mathsf{PA}$.
- Petr Hájek and Pavel Pudlák, *Metamathematics of First-Order Arithmetic* (1993). The reference work on fragments of arithmetic, from bounded induction to full $\mathsf{PA}$, with complete proofs.
- Peter Smith, *An Introduction to Gödel's Theorems*, 2nd edition (2013). A patient account of $\mathsf{Q}$, $\mathsf{PA}$, the formula classes, $\Sigma_1$-completeness and representability, written as preparation for the incompleteness theorems.
- George S. Boolos, John P. Burgess and Richard C. Jeffrey, *Computability and Logic*, 5th edition (2007). Proves the representability of recursive functions in a weak theory with clear, detailed arithmetic.
- William W. Tait, "Finitism", *The Journal of Philosophy* (1981). The argument that primitive recursive arithmetic is the formal counterpart of finitist mathematics.
