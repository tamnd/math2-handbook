## History

Proof theory began with Hilbert's program in the 1920s, when Hilbert, Ackermann and Bernays looked for finitary consistency proofs. Hilbert proposed the epsilon substitution method, and Ackermann's dissertation of 1924 gave a first consistency argument with it, which turned out to cover only a fragment of arithmetic. Hilbert and Bernays presented the epsilon calculus in the second volume of *Grundlagen der Mathematik* in 1939. After Gödel's incompleteness theorems of 1931 showed that no such proof could be formalized in arithmetic itself, Ackermann proved in 1940, in *Mathematische Annalen*, that the substitution process for arithmetic stops, with an induction up to $\varepsilon_0$.

Gerhard Gentzen's *Untersuchungen über das logische Schließen*, published in 1934 and 1935, introduced natural deduction and the sequent calculi LK and LJ. Its main theorem, the Hauptsatz, is cut elimination, proved with the mix rule; the sharpened Hauptsatz is the midsequent theorem. Gentzen wrote that the introduction rules are, as it were, the definitions of the connectives. He used his methods for a consistency proof of Peano arithmetic in 1936. Jaśkowski gave a form of natural deduction independently. Kleene introduced the calculi G3 in *Introduction to Metamathematics* in 1952, and Tait later gave a proof of cut elimination by reducing the cut rank in a one-sided calculus.

Dag Prawitz proved normalization for natural deduction in *Natural Deduction* of 1965, and later strong normalization. Gentzen had a normalization proof of his own that he never published; von Plato found it in his manuscripts. Prawitz's inversion principle became one of the sources of proof-theoretic semantics. Arthur Prior's tonk appeared in *The Runabout Inference-Ticket* in *Analysis* in 1960, and Nuel Belnap answered in *Tonk, Plonk and Plink* in 1962. Michael Dummett coined the word harmony and developed the theory in *The Logical Basis of Metaphysics* of 1991, and Peter Schroeder-Heister introduced the name proof-theoretic semantics in print in the same year.

The cost of cut elimination was settled by Statman and Orevkov, whose non-elementary lower bounds were published in 1979; Buss later sharpened them. Focusing came from logic programming. Miller, Nadathur, Pfenning and Scedrov introduced uniform proofs in 1991, and Andreoli introduced focusing for linear logic in 1992, in the *Journal of Logic and Computation*. Avron's hypersequents, Belnap's display calculus, Negri's labelled calculi in *Proof analysis in modal logic* and Guglielmi's calculus of structures in *A System of Interaction and Structure* extended cut elimination to many non-classical logics. Girard introduced proof nets in 1987. On the identity of proofs, Thiele found Hilbert's unpublished 24th problem, on the simplicity of proofs, in Hilbert's notebooks in 2000 and described it in the *American Mathematical Monthly* in 2003.

## Further reading

- A. S. Troelstra and H. Schwichtenberg, *Basic Proof Theory*. The standard reference for cut elimination, the G3 calculi, normalization and the complexity bounds.
- S. Negri and J. von Plato, *Structural Proof Theory*. A careful treatment of contraction-free calculi, inversion, and the method of adding rules for axioms.
- G. Takeuti, *Proof Theory*. Gentzen's calculi, the midsequent theorem and consistency proofs for arithmetic, in Gentzen's own style.
- D. Prawitz, *Natural Deduction: A Proof-Theoretical Study*. The original account of normalization and the inversion principle, and still short and readable.
- J.-Y. Girard, Y. Lafont and P. Taylor, *Proofs and Types*. Normalization through the Curry-Howard correspondence, with an introduction to linear logic.
- S. R. Buss (ed.), *Handbook of Proof Theory*. Survey chapters on cut elimination, first-order proof theory and proof complexity.
