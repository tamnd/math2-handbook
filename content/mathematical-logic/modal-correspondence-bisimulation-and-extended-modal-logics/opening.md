A Kripke model is a relational structure, so modal logic can be compared directly with first-order logic. The standard translation turns each modal formula into a first-order formula $\varphi(x)$ with one free variable, by reading $\square\psi$ as $\forall y\,(R x y \to \psi(y))$. Modal logic then becomes a fragment of first-order logic, and the question is which fragment. Johan van Benthem's characterization theorem answers it: a first-order formula is equivalent to the translation of a modal formula exactly when its truth is preserved by [bisimulation](/X27BR4YP2Y/). A bisimulation is a relation between the worlds of two models that matches atomic facts and moves along the accessibility relation in both directions; the theorem says that modal logic is the bisimulation-invariant part of first-order logic. Modal logic shares good properties, such as decidability, with other fragments of first-order logic, including the [two-variable fragment](/S7C3C7DJRF/) and the [guarded fragment](/Y7YS1C3GAE/).

Correspondence theory asks when a modal axiom, read on frames, defines a first-order property of the accessibility relation. Sahlqvist's theorem gives a large syntactic class of formulas that have first-order correspondents and that are canonical, so the logics they axiomatize are complete. Not every formula behaves so well. Löb's axiom defines a property of frames, transitivity together with the absence of infinite ascending chains, that is not first-order. S. K. Thomason and Kit Fine found in 1974 normal modal logics that are complete for no class of frames. The Goldblatt–Thomason theorem says which first-order classes of frames are modally definable.

Many logics extend the basic language. The [modal $\mu$-calculus](/MZ0M2KR9S0/), introduced by Dexter Kozen in 1983, adds least and greatest fixed points, and by a theorem of Janin and Walukiewicz it is the bisimulation-invariant part of [monadic second-order logic](/3WHQFSHR58/). Propositional dynamic logic has one box for each program. Hybrid logic adds names for worlds, and first-order modal logic adds quantifiers. Topological semantics reads $\square$ as the interior operator; McKinsey and Tarski proved in 1944 that $\mathrm{S4}$ is the logic of the real line in this sense.
