## History

Correspondence theory and bisimulation have a common origin. Johan van Benthem's dissertation "Modal correspondence theory", at the University of Amsterdam in 1976, compared modal formulas with first-order ones, used bisimulations under the name p-relations, and proved that modal logic is the bisimulation-invariant fragment of first-order logic. Henrik Sahlqvist's paper of 1975, "Completeness and correspondence in the first and second order semantics for modal logic", gave the class of formulas that are both first-order on frames and canonical. In the same year Robert Goldblatt and S. K. Thomason characterized the modally definable elementary classes in "Axiomatic classes in propositional modal logic", and Goldblatt and van Benthem showed independently that the McKinsey formula is not first-order on frames.

Bisimulation came into computer science separately. Matthew Hennessy and Robin Milner proved their theorem at ICALP in 1980, with a journal version in 1985, and David Park introduced bisimulation for concurrent processes in 1981; Milner made it the central equivalence of his calculus of communicating systems. Eric Rosen proved the van Benthem theorem over finite models in 1997.

The lattice of normal modal logics was studied from the 1960s. Bull proved in 1966 that every normal extension of $\mathsf{S4.3}$ has the finite model property, David Makinson described the two coatoms in 1971, and Kit Fine and Thomason published the first Kripke incomplete logics in 1974. Blok then proved that the degree of incompleteness of a normal modal logic is always $1$ or $2^{\aleph_0}$. On the side of quantifiers, Ruth Barcan published the first systems of quantified modal logic in 1946, with the Barcan formula as an axiom, and proved the necessity of identity in 1947; Saul Kripke gave the semantics with varying domains in 1963.

The extended logics came from several directions. Vaughan Pratt introduced dynamic logic in 1976, Michael Fischer and Richard Ladner defined propositional dynamic logic and proved it decidable in 1977, and Dexter Kozen and Rohit Parikh gave a short completeness proof for Krister Segerberg's axioms in 1981. Kozen gave the modal $\mu$-calculus its modern form in 1983, Igor Walukiewicz proved Kozen's axioms complete, and David Janin and Walukiewicz showed in 1996 that it is the bisimulation-invariant part of monadic second-order logic. Arthur Prior's tense logic of the 1960s, treated in *Past, Present and Future* (1967), is the origin of hybrid logic, which Patrick Blackburn and others developed in the 1990s.

Topology entered early. Tarski gave a topological semantics for intuitionistic logic in 1938, McKinsey and Tarski proved in "The algebra of topology" (1944) that $\mathsf{S4}$ is the logic of the real line, and in 1948 they proved that Gödel's translation of 1933 is faithful. Blok and Leo Esakia proved the isomorphism between intermediate logics and extensions of $\mathsf{Grz}$ independently in the 1970s. Kripke's non-normal worlds for $\mathsf{S2}$ and $\mathsf{S3}$ date from 1965, and Segerberg and Valentin Shehtman studied products such as $\mathsf{S5} \times \mathsf{S5}$ in the 1970s, before Dov Gabbay and Shehtman's general theory of 1998.

## Further reading

- Patrick Blackburn, Maarten de Rijke and Yde Venema, *Modal Logic* (2001). The standard graduate text on bisimulation, the van Benthem theorem, Sahlqvist correspondence, the Goldblatt–Thomason theorem and algebraic duality.
- Alexander Chagrov and Michael Zakharyaschev, *Modal Logic* (1997). The reference for the lattice of logics, incompleteness, canonicity, modal companions and the Blok–Esakia theorem.
- G. E. Hughes and M. J. Cresswell, *A New Introduction to Modal Logic* (1996). A readable account of quantified modal logic with varying domains and the Barcan formulas.
- David Harel, Dexter Kozen and Jerzy Tiuryn, *Dynamic Logic* (2000). Propositional and first-order dynamic logic, with the completeness and complexity of $\mathsf{PDL}$.
- Dov Gabbay, Agi Kurucz, Frank Wolter and Michael Zakharyaschev, *Many-Dimensional Modal Logics: Theory and Applications* (2003). The monograph on fusions and products of modal logics.
