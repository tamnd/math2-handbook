1. The largest bisimulation is $\set{(s, r'), (s, s')}$: the world $s$ and both worlds of $\mathfrak{M}'$ have a successor at every step, while $t$ has none and $r$ has the dead end $t$ as a successor. So $r$ and $r'$ are not bisimilar, and $\lozenge\square\bot$ is true at $r$ and false at $r'$.

3. The classes are $\set{1}$, $\set{2, 3}$ and $\set{4, 5, 6}$: the last three worlds carry $p$ and see only $p$-worlds, and $2$ and $3$ carry no $p$ and see exactly one world, which is in the last class. The contraction is $a \mathrel{R} b \mathrel{R} c \mathrel{R} c$ with $p$ true at $c$ only, and $a$ corresponds to $1$. Both models are finite, so they are image-finite, and modal equivalence coincides with bisimilarity.

6. $R_{a ; b} = \set{(1, 3)}$ and $R_{a^*} = \set{0, 1, 2}^2 \cup \set{(3, 3)}$. Then $R_{a^* ; b} = \set{(0, 3), (1, 3), (2, 3)}$, so $\langle a^* ; b \rangle p$ holds at $\set{0, 1, 2}$. The formula $[a^*] \neg p$ also holds exactly at $\set{0, 1, 2}$; it fails at $3$ because $3 \mathrel{R_{a^*}} 3$.

7. $T_1 = \set{3, 4}$, $T_2 = \set{1, 2, 3, 4}$ and $T_3 = T_4 = \set{0, 1, 2, 3, 4}$. World $4$ enters at the first step because it has no successors, so $\square X$ holds there for every $X$. World $5$ never enters, since its successor $5$ never does: the path $5, 5, 5, \dots$ avoids $p$ forever.

9. $T(\neg p) = \square(\square p \to \bot)$, equivalent to $\square\neg\square p$, and $T(\neg\neg p)$ is equivalent to $\square\lozenge\square p$. So $T(p \vee \neg p)$ is $\square p \vee \square\neg\square p$ and $T(\neg\neg p \to p)$ is $\square(\square\lozenge\square p \to \square p)$. On the chain both are true at $1$ and false at $0$: at $0$ we have $\square\lozenge\square p$ but not $\square p$. By the theorem, $\mathsf{IPC}$ proves neither formula.

10. $\mathrm{Cl}\, \Q = \R$ and $\mathrm{Int}\, \Q = \emptyset$, so the four truth sets are $\R$, $\R$, $\emptyset$ and $\emptyset$. The McKinsey formula is false at every point of $\R$, while every space validates every theorem of $\mathsf{S4}$.

12. If a world $x$ has three distinct successors $a$, $b$, $c$, make $p$ false at $a$, make $p$ true and $q$ false at $b$, and make $p$ and $q$ true and $r$ false at $c$: all three disjuncts fail at $x$. Conversely, if all three disjuncts fail at $x$, the three witnesses are pairwise distinct, since a world with $\neg p$ cannot also satisfy $p \wedge \neg q$ or $p \wedge q \wedge \neg r$, and a world with $p \wedge \neg q$ cannot satisfy $p \wedge q \wedge \neg r$.

13. (a) Not closed under generated subframes: a reflexive point next to an irreflexive one, and the subframe generated by the irreflexive point. (b) Not closed under bounded morphic images: $(\N, S)$ with the successor relation is asymmetric and maps onto the reflexive point. (c) Not closed under disjoint unions: two one-point frames. (d) Definable, by $\lozenge\square\bot$.

16. Let $w$ be normal with $w \models \square(p \to q)$, and let $w \mathrel{R} u$. If $u$ is non-normal, $\square p$ is false at $u$. If $u$ is normal and $u \models \square p$, every successor $z$ of $u$ is a successor of $w$ by transitivity, so $z \models p \to q$ and $z \models q$. For the failure, take $0 \mathrel{R} 1 \mathrel{R} 2$ with all loops, $p$ true everywhere and $q$ true at $0$ and $1$ only.

21. $T(\neg p) \equiv \square\neg\square p$ and $T(\neg\neg p) \equiv \square\lozenge\square p$, so the disjunction says $\lozenge\square p \to \square\lozenge\square p$. If $w$ sees $y$ and $z$ with no common successor, put $p$ true exactly on the successors of $y$: then $y \models \square p$, but a world $u$ with $z \mathrel{R} u$ and $u \models \square p$ would satisfy $u \mathrel{R} u$ and so lie above $y$ and $z$. If the frame is directed, a world $u$ above $y$ and $z$ satisfies $\square p$ whenever $y$ does, by transitivity.

23. The sets decrease, and $W \times W$ is finite. The limit $Z$ satisfies the forth and back conditions with respect to itself, and every bisimulation is contained in every $Z_k$ by induction on $k$. For the last part, induct on $k$; for the direction from equivalence to $Z_{k+1}$, use that each world has finitely many successors and that, up to equivalence, there are only finitely many formulas of depth $k$ in the finitely many variables that matter.

24. If $y$ is such a successor and $x \models \square\lozenge p$, then $y \models \lozenge p$, so $y \mathrel{R} y$ and $p$ holds at $y$; hence $y \models \square p$ and $x \models \lozenge\square p$. Conversely, every successor of $x$ sees a final cluster, a maximal set of mutually related worlds that sees nothing outside itself. If none of these clusters is a single world $y$ with $R(y) \subseteq \set{y}$, each has at least two worlds; make $p$ true at exactly one world of each final cluster.

25. $\mathsf{Ver}$ proves $\square\psi$ for every $\psi$, because $\square\bot \to \square\psi$ follows from $\bot \to \psi$ by necessitation and $\mathrm{K}$. So every formula is $\mathsf{Ver}$-equivalent to a formula without $\square$, obtained by replacing each $\square\psi$ with $\top$, and that formula is valid on $\bullet$ exactly when it is a tautology. For $\mathsf{Triv}$, replace each $\square\psi$ with $\psi$.

26. Let $\mathfrak{M}_n$ be the chain $0 \mathrel{R} 1 \mathrel{R} \cdots \mathrel{R} n + 1$ with $p$ true everywhere, and let $\mathfrak{N}_n$ be the same chain with $p$ false at $n + 1$ only. The roots satisfy the same formulas of modal depth at most $n$, by induction or by exercise 23, while the fixed-point formula is true at the first root and false at the second.

27. In the ultrafilter extension, $u \mathrel{R^{\mathrm{ue}}} v$ holds when $\set{x : x < y \text{ for some } y \in A} \in u$ for every $A \in v$. Every set in a non-principal ultrafilter is infinite, and for infinite $A$ that set is $\N$. So each non-principal ultrafilter is reflexive and is seen by every ultrafilter, while $(\N, <)$ has no reflexive point. The class is first-order, so it is closed under ultraproducts, and the Goldblatt–Thomason theorem would force it to reflect ultrafilter extensions.
