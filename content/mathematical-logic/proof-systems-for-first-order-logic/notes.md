## History

Frege gave the first formal system with quantifiers in his *Begriffsschrift* of 1879, in the axiomatic style that is now called a Hilbert system. Hilbert and Ackermann stated a first-order calculus of this kind in their book *Grundzüge der theoretischen Logik* of 1928 and asked whether it derives every valid formula; Gödel answered yes in his dissertation of 1929. Herbrand proved a deduction theorem in his thesis of 1930. Quine's *Mathematical Logic* of 1940 took modus ponens as the only rule and closed the axioms under generalization, the form that Enderton's textbook uses, so that the rule of generalization becomes the generalization theorem.

Gerhard Gentzen's paper "Investigations into logical deduction", published in 1934 and 1935, introduced both natural deduction and the sequent calculi LK and LJ. Gentzen wanted a calculus close to actual reasoning, and found that its proofs were easier to study in sequent form. He proved the cut-elimination theorem there, and with it the consistency of pure logic and the midsequent theorem. He also showed that natural deduction, the sequent calculi and a Hilbert-style calculus derive the same formulas. Stanisław Jaśkowski published a system of suppositional proofs independently in 1934, with boxes around the parts of a proof that depend on an assumption. Frederic Fitch's textbook *Symbolic Logic* of 1952 drew these boxes as vertical lines, and Kleene's *Introduction to Metamathematics* of the same year introduced the G3 calculi. Dag Prawitz proved the normalization theorem for natural deduction in his monograph *Natural Deduction* of 1965.

Semantic tableaux turn the search for a countermodel into a proof. Evert Willem Beth and Jaakko Hintikka introduced them independently in 1955, and Stig Kanger gave a related method. Hintikka's model sets, the sets that describe a model from the inside, are the Hintikka sets of this chapter. Raymond Smullyan's *First-Order Logic* of 1968 gave tableaux the uniform $\alpha, \beta, \gamma, \delta$ notation and proved Hintikka's lemma in it.

Resolution came from automated reasoning. Martin Davis and Hilary Putnam used a propositional form of the rule in 1960 in a procedure for satisfiability. John Alan Robinson introduced the resolution principle with unification in a paper in the Journal of the ACM in 1965, and it became the standard calculus of first-order provers. George Robinson and Lawrence Wos added paramodulation for equality in 1969, and Leo Bachmair and Harald Ganzinger developed superposition, with its ordering restrictions, in the early 1990s.

## Further reading

- Herbert B. Enderton, *A Mathematical Introduction to Logic*, second edition (2001). Chapter 2 sets out the Hilbert calculus of this chapter, with the generalization and deduction theorems and the equality axioms, at an undergraduate pace.
- Elliott Mendelson, *Introduction to Mathematical Logic*, sixth edition (2015). Chapter 2 uses a Hilbert system with a rule of generalization, so it is the place to see the restricted deduction theorem in full.
- Dirk van Dalen, *Logic and Structure*, fifth edition (2013). A clear treatment of natural deduction for first-order logic, with completeness and normalization.
- Raymond M. Smullyan, *First-Order Logic* (1968). The classic account of analytic tableaux and Hintikka sets, short and exact.
- A. S. Troelstra and H. Schwichtenberg, *Basic Proof Theory*, second edition (2000). Natural deduction, the sequent calculi and Hilbert systems side by side, with cut elimination, normalization and the translations between them.
- Melvin Fitting, *First-Order Logic and Automated Theorem Proving*, second edition (1996). Tableaux and resolution as proof procedures, with unification, Skolemization and implementations.
