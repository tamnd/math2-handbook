In classical logic, $\set{p, \neg p} \vDash q$ for every $q$. A database that records one train as both on time and late therefore entails every statement, including false ones about trains it never mentions. Priest's logic of paradox blocks this with a third value $\mathsf{B}$, "both true and false". Put $v(p) = \mathsf{B}$ and $v(q) = \mathsf{F}$: then $p$ and $\neg p$ are designated and $q$ is not, so $q$ does not follow. The same valuation also refutes disjunctive syllogism, so the change has a price. Classical logic makes other assumptions that can fail in practice too. It proves $\exists x\, (x = t)$ for every term $t$, so it cannot reason about "Pegasus" or about $1/x$ at $x = 0$ without saying that they exist. It is monotonic: from "Tweety is a bird" and "birds fly" it concludes that Tweety flies, and it cannot withdraw that conclusion when it learns that Tweety is a penguin.

The logics of this chapter each change one such assumption and keep the rest of classical logic as far as they can. Most of them are stated with a semantics of the usual kind: matrices with three or four values, Kripke models, models with an inner and an outer domain, or models with a normality order. As in most work on these logics, the metatheory is classical, so the methods are those of the earlier chapters. The point is to see exactly which classical law each logic gives up, and what it gets in return.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in six groups, read in order.

1. **Non-classical logics and explosion.** [Non-classical logic](/3JK7T9D6X1/) sorts logics into deviations and extensions of classical logic. [Explosion](/TTKZGJEEK7/) is the rule from a contradiction to every formula, and the Lewis argument shows which rules force it.
2. **Paraconsistent logics.** [Paraconsistent logic](/C7YWCR87QJ/) is the general idea, and the [logic of paradox](/BXJZG67PX3/) is its simplest many-valued case. [Dialetheism](/3P0FM0AQF3/) is a view about truth, not a logic. [Da Costa's systems](/KN2VBRQB3Q/), [Jaśkowski's discussive logic](/489SFB5NX2/) and [adaptive logic](/X8RW5F4261/) are three other ways to block explosion.
3. **Empty names, gaps, connexive theses and many conclusions.** [Free logic](/Y0CNMRXHVJ/) lets terms fail to denote, and [partial logic](/F5SP7HQFFS/) lets formulas lack a value. [Connexive logic](/NQFYY76BSM/) proves formulas that classical logic does not prove. [Multiple-conclusion logic](/3AJTDAP93H/) puts a set of formulas on the right of $\vdash$, read as a disjunction.
4. **Nonmonotonic reasoning.** [Nonmonotonic logic](/BKDFH4PKRP/) compares default logic, circumscription and autoepistemic logic. [Default logic](/K4AYFFKY2D/) defines extensions as fixed points, and the [preferential consequence relations](/G4QC9MKFAG/) give the general rules and their models.
5. **Combined logics and the logic of language.** [Combining logics](/HP1N0MBTDJ/) treats fusion, products, fibring and the collapse problem. [Montague grammar](/FT512R206H/) gives English sentences an exact semantics in a typed intensional logic.
6. **Logics from physics and computation.** [Quantum logic](/2E9DMB7A6Q/) studies the lattice of closed subspaces of a Hilbert space. [Computability logic](/C7CV0XMSTT/) reads formulas as games that a machine must win by an algorithm.

## A first reading

For a first reading, take these entries in order: [non-classical logic](/3JK7T9D6X1/), [explosion](/TTKZGJEEK7/), [paraconsistent logic](/C7YWCR87QJ/), [logic of paradox](/BXJZG67PX3/), [Da Costa's systems](/KN2VBRQB3Q/), [free logic](/Y0CNMRXHVJ/), [partial logic](/F5SP7HQFFS/), [nonmonotonic logic](/BKDFH4PKRP/), [default logic](/K4AYFFKY2D/) and [preferential consequence relations](/G4QC9MKFAG/). Work through the small examples attached to them: the three-valued valuations, the Tweety and Nixon examples, and the default theory with no extension. They show each logic at work in a few lines.

The other entries can wait. Dialetheism is mainly philosophy. Discussive and adaptive logics are easier once the logic of paradox is familiar. Connexive and multiple-conclusion logic change the shape of consequence in ways that are clearer after the rest of the chapter. The last five entries stand mostly on their own. They link this chapter to modal logic, to linguistics, to physics and to the theory of computation, and you can read them in any order.

## Before you start

You need [the propositional calculus](/JDK63589SA/), with valuations and [logical consequence](/1HK0VYWTV7/), and [first-order logic](/086QKPF0A6/) for the free and partial logics. The chapter on [many-valued and fuzzy logics](/WZP584JQ06/) introduces matrices, designated values, [strong Kleene logic](/SZHA10A9X7/) and [first-degree entailment](/KXTP4P3GHT/). The chapter on [syntax, Kripke semantics and completeness of modal logic](/SKY7AQFG1V/) gives the Kripke models used for discussive logic and for combined logics, and [S5](/VC3A2VWXND/) in particular. [Intuitionistic logic](/Q11N8X99FK/) is the main example of a deviation, and the chapter on [substructural, linear and relevance logics](/98QF5J4W16/) treats a family of paraconsistent logics that this chapter only mentions. For the last two entries, some knowledge of [Hilbert spaces](/HM2HHE3J51/) and of [computable functions](/KZSB5E471K/) helps.
