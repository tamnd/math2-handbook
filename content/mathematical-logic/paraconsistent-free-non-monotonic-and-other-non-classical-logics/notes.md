## History

The Lewis argument for explosion was known in the twelfth century, when William of Soissons found a version of it, and it now carries the name of C. I. Lewis, who published it. Formal logics without explosion came much later. Nicolai Vasil'ev proposed a syllogistic with contradictory statements from about 1910. Stanisław Jaśkowski, a student of Łukasiewicz, published discussive logic in 1948 and the discussive conjunction in 1949. Florencio Asenjo and Newton da Costa developed paraconsistent logics in their dissertations of 1954 and 1963, and Francisco Miró Quesada coined the word "paraconsistent" in 1976. Graham Priest introduced LP in "The Logic of Paradox" (1979), and he and Richard Routley coined "dialetheism" in 1981. Diderik Batens later developed adaptive logics, and logicians in Campinas and São Paulo turned da Costa's ideas into the theory of logics of formal inconsistency.

Stephen Kleene introduced the strong three-valued tables in 1938, for partial recursive functions, and Saul Kripke used them in 1975 for his theory of truth. Logic with an empty domain was studied in the first half of the twentieth century, and Quine named it inclusive logic in 1954. Free logic in the modern sense began with Jaakko Hintikka (1959) and Karel Lambert, who gave it its name. Connexive ideas go back to Aristotle and Boethius, and Hugh MacColl (1878) and E. J. Nelson (1930) anticipated them. The modern subject starts with Richard Angell (1962) and Storrs McCall (1966), who named it. Heinrich Wansing introduced the system $\mathbf{C}$ in 2005. Gentzen's sequent calculus already had several formulas on the right, and D. J. Shoesmith and T. J. Smiley developed multiple-conclusion logic as a subject in their 1978 book.

Nonmonotonic logic grew out of artificial intelligence. In the late 1970s John McCarthy, Drew McDermott and Jon Doyle, and Raymond Reiter proposed formalisms for reasoning by default. A special issue of *Artificial Intelligence* in 1980 contained Reiter's "A logic for default reasoning" and McCarthy's circumscription. Robert Moore introduced autoepistemic logic in 1985, and Yoav Shoham proposed the selection of preferred models in 1987. Sarit Kraus, Daniel Lehmann and Menachem Magidor set out system $\mathbf{P}$ and proved its representation theorem in 1990. Lehmann and Magidor defined rational closure in 1992, and Judea Pearl's system Z of 1990 turned out to be equivalent to it.

Products of modal logics go back to Krister Segerberg (1973) and Valentin Šehtman (1978), and Richmond Thomason introduced fusions in 1984. Dov Gabbay introduced fibring in 1996 and found the collapse problem the same year. Richard Montague turned to natural language around 1966 and set out his approach in "Universal Grammar" and "English as a Formal Language" (both 1970) and in "The Proper Treatment of Quantification in Ordinary English" (1973). Barbara Partee and others then joined it to linguistics.

Quantum logic began with the logical calculus of projections in John von Neumann's book of 1932 and with the paper of Garrett Birkhoff and von Neumann in 1936. Andrew Gleason proved his theorem on measures in 1957, Constantin Piron his representation theorem in 1964, and Simon Kochen and Ernst Specker their theorem in 1967. Hilary Putnam asked "Is Logic Empirical?" in 1968. Giorgi Japaridze introduced computability logic in 2003, in "Introduction to computability logic", and later developed cirquent calculus for its proof theory.

## Further reading

- Graham Priest, *An Introduction to Non-Classical Logic: From If to Is*, second edition (2008). A textbook treatment of many-valued, paraconsistent, free, relevant and intuitionistic logics, with tableaux for each.
- Walter Carnielli and Marcelo E. Coniglio, *Paraconsistent Logic: Consistency, Contradiction and Negation* (2016). The full theory of da Costa's systems and the logics of formal inconsistency.
- D. J. Shoesmith and T. J. Smiley, *Multiple-Conclusion Logic* (1978). The book that set up multiple-conclusion consequence and its proof theory.
- David Makinson, *Bridges from Classical to Nonmonotonic Logic* (2005). A short path from classical consequence to default, preferential and other nonmonotonic relations.
- Irene Heim and Angelika Kratzer, *Semantics in Generative Grammar* (1998). The textbook of type-driven semantics that grew out of Montague grammar.
