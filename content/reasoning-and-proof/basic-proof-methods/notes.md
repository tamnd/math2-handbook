## History

The methods of this chapter are older than their names. Most propositions in Euclid's Elements are proved by chains of steps from the postulates and earlier results, and many of them are direct proofs; others are indirect, assuming the opposite and reaching an absurdity. Euclid's proofs speak of one figure ("let $ABC$ be a triangle"), and the reader understands that the argument applies to every triangle, which is the idea of the arbitrary element. Pappus of Alexandria, in Book 7 of his Collection, described analysis, which assumes what is sought and works back to something known, and synthesis, which runs the analysis in reverse to give the proof. This is the forward-backward method in its oldest form.

The logic behind the methods was made exact in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. The equivalence of an implication with its contrapositive is a law of the classical propositional logic that Frege and Russell formalized. Bertrand Russell's theory of descriptions of 1905 analysed "the $x$ such that $P(x)$" as a claim of existence and uniqueness. Gerhard Gentzen's natural deduction of 1934 stated each method as a rule: introduction of an implication by a temporary assumption, introduction of a universal statement from an arbitrary variable that occurs in no open assumption, and elimination of a disjunction by cases.

Constructive and non-constructive existence proofs became a matter of dispute. In 1888 David Hilbert proved that the invariants of binary forms are generated by finitely many of them, and his paper of 1890 gave the basis theorem in general, without a method to find the generators. Paul Gordan, who had computed such generators by hand, is reported to have called the proof "theology". Luitzen Egbertus Jan Brouwer's intuitionism rejected the law of excluded middle for infinite collections, and with it existence proofs by contradiction; Arend Heyting made intuitionistic logic formal in 1930. Errett Bishop's Foundations of Constructive Analysis (1967) showed how much of analysis survives with constructive proofs only.

Counterexamples have settled long-standing conjectures. The Pólya conjecture of 1919 was disproved by Haselgrove in 1958, and in 1980 Tanaka found its smallest counterexample, $906150257$. In 1985 Andrew Odlyzko and Herman te Riele disproved the Mertens conjecture by a proof that a counterexample exists without exhibiting one. Imre Lakatos's Proofs and Refutations (1976) describes how counterexamples lead mathematicians to revise both proofs and definitions. Proofs of impossibility have their own history: Ruffini (1799) and Abel (1824) on the quintic, Wantzel (1837) on trisecting the angle and doubling the cube, Lindemann (1882) on squaring the circle, Turing (1936) on the halting problem and Arrow (1951) on voting rules.

Teaching the methods as a list is more recent. George Pólya's How to Solve It (1945) treated working backwards as a heuristic. Daniel Solow's How to Read and Do Proofs (1982) gave the forward-backward method its name, and transition-to-proof books such as Velleman's How to Prove It arrange their chapters by the logical form of the goal, as this chapter does. Proof assistants now check the methods mechanically; John Harrison's paper "Without loss of generality" (2009) studied the formal content of the wlog step.

## Further reading

- Daniel J. Velleman, *How to Prove It: A Structured Approach* (3rd edition, Cambridge University Press, 2019). Chapter 3 treats each method of this chapter by the form of the goal, with many exercises; it is the closest match to the entries.
- George Pólya, *How to Solve It* (Princeton University Press, 1945). The classic account of heuristics for finding proofs, including working backwards.
- Daniel Solow, *How to Read and Do Proofs*. Short and systematic, organized around the forward-backward method and the key questions to ask of a goal.
- Richard Hammack, *Book of Proof*. A free introductory text with separate chapters on direct proof, contrapositive, contradiction, existence and disproof.
- Imre Lakatos, *Proofs and Refutations: The Logic of Mathematical Discovery* (Cambridge University Press, 1976). A dialogue on Euler's polyhedron formula that shows how counterexamples reshape theorems and definitions.
- Bernard R. Gelbaum and John M. H. Olmsted, *Counterexamples in Analysis* (1964). A collection of counterexamples to plausible statements in real analysis, useful once you know some calculus.
