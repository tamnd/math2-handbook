## History

The methods of this chapter are much older than their names. Most propositions of Euclid's *Elements* are proved by chains of steps from the postulates and from earlier propositions, and many are direct proofs; others are indirect, assuming the opposite and reaching an absurdity. Euclid argues about one figure, "let $ABC$ be a triangle", and the reader understands that the argument applies to every triangle, which is the idea of the arbitrary element. Pappus of Alexandria, in Book 7 of his *Collection*, described analysis, which assumes what is sought and works back to something known, and synthesis, which reverses the analysis to give the proof; this is the forward-backward method in its oldest form.

The logic behind the methods was made exact around 1900 and after. The equivalence of an implication with its contrapositive is a law of the classical logic that Frege and Russell formalized, and Russell's theory of descriptions of 1905 analysed "the $x$ such that $P(x)$" as a claim of existence and uniqueness. In the 1930s Gerhard Gentzen's natural deduction stated each method as a rule of inference, with the side condition on open assumptions that gives "arbitrary" its formal meaning.

Existence proofs became a matter of dispute. Paul Gordan proved in 1868, by explicit computation, that the invariants of binary forms are generated by finitely many of them. In 1890 David Hilbert published the basis theorem and proved finite generation for forms in any number of variables without a method to find the generators, and Gordan is reported to have called the proof theology. L. E. J. Brouwer's intuitionism, from the first decade of the twentieth century, rejected the law of excluded middle for infinite collections and with it existence proofs by contradiction; Arend Heyting made intuitionistic logic formal in 1930. Errett Bishop's *Foundations of Constructive Analysis* (1967) showed how much of analysis survives with constructive proofs only.

Counterexamples have settled old conjectures. Euler found the factor $641$ of $2^{32} + 1$ in 1732, against Fermat's belief that all numbers $2^{2^n} + 1$ are prime. Lander and Parkin refuted Euler's sum of powers conjecture in 1966. The Pólya conjecture of 1919 was disproved by Haselgrove in 1958, and Tanaka found its smallest counterexample, $906150257$, in 1980. In 1985 Andrew Odlyzko and Herman te Riele disproved the Mertens conjecture by showing that a counterexample exists without exhibiting one. Proofs of impossibility have their own line: Ruffini (1799) and Abel (1824) on the general quintic, Wantzel (1837) on trisecting the angle and doubling the cube, Lindemann (1882) on squaring the circle, Turing (1936) on the halting problem and Arrow (1951) on voting rules.

Teaching the methods as a list is recent. George Pólya's *How to Solve It* (1945) treated working backwards as a heuristic, and Daniel Solow's *How to Read and Do Proofs* (1982) gave the forward-backward method its name. John Harrison's paper "Without loss of generality" (2009) studied what the wlog step means formally, and supported it in the HOL Light theorem prover.

## Further reading

- Daniel J. Velleman, *How to Prove It: A Structured Approach* (third edition, 2019). It organizes proofs by the logical form of the goal, as the table in the summary does, and has many exercises at the level of this chapter.
- Richard Hammack, *Book of Proof*. A clear introductory text with separate chapters on direct proof, contrapositive, contradiction, existence and disproof.
- Daniel Solow, *How to Read and Do Proofs* (1982). A short systematic course built around the forward-backward method and the questions to ask of a goal.
- George Pólya, *How to Solve It* (1945). The classic account of heuristics for finding proofs, including working backwards and the auxiliary problem.
- Imre Lakatos, *Proofs and Refutations: The Logic of Mathematical Discovery* (1976). A dialogue on Euler's polyhedron formula that shows how counterexamples lead to better proofs and better definitions.
