A proof does its work only when another person can read and check it. Compare two answers to "show that the sum of two even integers is even". The first is a line of algebra:

$$2a + 2b = 2(a + b).$$

The second says: "Let $m$ and $n$ be even integers. By the definition of even, $m = 2a$ and $n = 2b$ for some integers $a$ and $b$. Then $m + n = 2a + 2b = 2(a + b)$, and $a + b$ is an integer, so $m + n$ is even." The algebra is the same. Only the second answer says what $a$ and $b$ are, which definition is used, and what the last line shows, so only the second can be checked by someone who did not write it.

The chapter is about that difference, from both sides. A writer turns an argument found in scratch work into a text for a stated reader, with each letter introduced, each step given a reason and the conclusion stated. A reader works through such a text line by line, supplies the omitted steps and tries to break the argument. Both skills can be taught, and both decide whether a proof is accepted: in a course, a correct idea written as a bare column of formulas loses marks, and in research, a referee cannot accept a paper whose main step is missing.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in four groups, read in order.

1. **From argument to text.** The steps of [writing a proof](/8JQHJK94RZ/), the difference between [scratch work and the final proof](/E97XXFW9MV/), the moves that [the logical form of a statement](/872K5XB580/) dictates, and [unpacking definitions](/41JZ39GE3F/), which is where most short proofs start.
2. **Words and formats.** [The language of proofs](/GKMRQCA8CW/): what "let", "fix", "suppose" and "it suffices to show" commit the writer to. Then two formats besides the paragraph: the [two-column proof](/P8PCGFASP8/) of school geometry and Lamport's [structured proofs](/H2W8D7BMSW/).
3. **Detail and style.** The [level of detail](/VYGBV5D8Y4/) that a reader can fill in, and the conventions of [mathematical writing style](/D5YPYTQ88G/).
4. **The reader's side.** [Reading proofs](/40GCSM2G56/) actively by self-explanation, [checking a proof](/4M98ZZNK0E/) as a referee does, and the [structure of a mathematical paper](/XZHACQ74C0/).

## A first reading

Read [writing proofs](/8JQHJK94RZ/), [scratch work and the final proof](/E97XXFW9MV/), [proof strategies by logical form](/872K5XB580/), [unpacking definitions](/41JZ39GE3F/), [the language of proofs](/GKMRQCA8CW/), [level of detail](/VYGBV5D8Y4/), [reading proofs](/40GCSM2G56/) and [checking a proof](/4M98ZZNK0E/). Together they cover what a student needs to write homework proofs and to read a textbook.

The two-column and structured formats can be skimmed at first; they matter for readers who teach geometry or who write long proofs that must be checked in detail. The style entry and the structure of a paper are best read when you begin to write longer texts.

## Before you start

The chapter assumes that you know what a [mathematical proof](/0B94TKFQJX/) is and have seen the [basic proof methods](/958EF46EYJ/), such as [direct proof](/0NTF6DMVAP/), [contradiction](/0J0XBKG64S/) and [proof by cases](/0WCP0PVET6/). The entry on logical form uses [quantifiers](/EGTZPA6QH3/) and [conditional statements](/FA6YZGR2SS/) from the [language of mathematical statements](/WMY13MXY3W/). The entries on words and style build on the chapter on [notation, conventions and jargon](/Z2WNQ1DNTX/), in particular on what words such as [trivial and obvious](/Y0HXCJRB9B/) signal in a proof. The examples use only school algebra, divisibility of integers, and basic facts about sets and functions.
