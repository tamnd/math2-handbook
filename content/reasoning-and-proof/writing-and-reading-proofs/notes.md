## History

The written form of a proof goes back to Euclid's *Elements*, where each proposition is stated, set out with named points, proved step by step from earlier results, and closed by restating what was shown. Proclus, in his commentary on Book I, named these parts, and the closing formula survives in Latin as Q.E.D. Pappus described the two directions of work that still separate scratch work from the final text: analysis, which assumes what is sought and works back to known facts, and synthesis, which runs the same steps forwards as a proof.

The link between the logical form of a statement and the moves of a proof was made exact by Gentzen's natural deduction (1934–35), in which each connective and quantifier has rules for introducing and for using it. Textbooks for students turned these rules into advice. Daniel Solow's *How to Read and Do Proofs* taught a forward-backward method, and Daniel Velleman's *How to Prove It* (first edition 1994) organized a whole course around givens and goals. Proof assistants such as Lean now display the same givens and goal on the screen and change them one tactic at a time.

Advice on writing for human readers has its own literature. Paul Halmos's essay "How to Write Mathematics" (1970) was reprinted in an American Mathematical Society booklet of 1973 together with essays by Norman Steenrod, Menahem Schiffer and Jean Dieudonné. The notes of Donald Knuth's Stanford course were published as *Mathematical Writing* (1989) by Knuth, Tracy Larrabee and Paul Roberts, and Nicholas Higham's *Handbook of Writing for the Mathematical Sciences* reached a second edition in 1998. At the other extreme from informal prose stands Whitehead and Russell's *Principia Mathematica* (1910–1913), whose fully formal proofs show how much an ordinary proof leaves out. In school geometry in the United States the two-column format spread in the early twentieth century, a history traced by Patricio Herbst in 2002. Leslie Lamport proposed structured, hierarchically numbered proofs in "How to Write a Proof" (1995) and returned to them in "How to Write a 21st Century Proof" (2012).

Reading and checking proofs became an object of study later. Halmos, in his memoir *I Want to Be a Mathematician* (1985), urged readers to fight a text rather than read it passively. Research in mathematics education has measured what readers understand: Juan Pablo Mejía-Ramos and coauthors proposed a model for assessing proof comprehension in 2012, and Mark Hodds, Lara Alcock and Matthew Inglis showed in 2014 that a short training in self-explanation improves it. The need for checking is shown by cases such as Alfred Kempe's proof of the four colour theorem, published in 1879 and accepted until Percy Heawood found the error in 1890.

## Further reading

- Daniel J. Velleman, *How to Prove It: A Structured Approach*, 3rd edition (2019). Teaches proof writing through givens, goals and the logical form of statements.
- Paul R. Halmos, "How to Write Mathematics" (1970). A short essay on planning, writing and revising mathematical text, still the most quoted advice on the subject.
- Donald E. Knuth, Tracy Larrabee and Paul M. Roberts, *Mathematical Writing* (1989). Course notes full of worked examples of bad sentences and their repairs.
- Nicholas J. Higham, *Handbook of Writing for the Mathematical Sciences*, 2nd edition (1998). A practical reference on style, notation, papers and the publication process.
- Lara Alcock, *How to Study as a Mathematics Major* (2013). Explains to students how to read definitions, theorems and proofs, including self-explanation.
