## History

The form of the written proof goes back to Euclid's *Elements*, written around 300 BC. Each proposition is stated, then proved, and the proof ends by repeating what was to be shown; the Latin "quod erat demonstrandum", shortened to Q.E.D., translates that Greek closing phrase. Pappus of Alexandria described two methods of the Greek geometers: analysis, which starts from what is sought and works backward, and synthesis, which turns the analysis around into a proof. The pair is the ancestor of the modern distinction between scratch work and the final proof.

In the twentieth century the structure of proofs became an object of study and of teaching. Gerhard Gentzen introduced natural deduction in 1934 and 1935, with an introduction rule and an elimination rule for each connective and quantifier; the strategies by logical form are the informal version of these rules. In American schools, the two-column proof became the custom of geometry classes early in the century, and a printed example from 1913 is often reproduced; Patricio Herbst traced that history in 2002. Whitehead and Russell's *Principia Mathematica* (1910 to 1913) showed, by contrast, how long a proof becomes when no step is left out.

Advice on writing mathematics grew into a small literature. Paul Halmos wrote the essay "How to Write Mathematics" in 1970, and in 1973 the American Mathematical Society reprinted it in a booklet together with essays by Norman Steenrod, Menahem Schiffer and Jean Dieudonné. Donald Knuth, Tracy Larrabee and Paul Roberts published *Mathematical Writing* in 1989, from a course at Stanford. Leslie Lamport proposed structured proofs in "How to Write a Proof" in the *American Mathematical Monthly* in 1995, and returned to them in "How to Write a 21st Century Proof" in 2012.

Teaching students to find, read and check proofs has its own line. George Pólya's *How to Solve It* (1945) advised going back to definitions and made "looking back" the last stage of solving a problem. Daniel Velleman's *How to Prove It* (1994) built a course around the givens and the goal at each stage of a proof, and Halmos's memoir *I Want to Be a Mathematician* (1985) urged readers to question each hypothesis of a theorem. Research on proof comprehension followed; in 2014 Mark Hodds, Lara Alcock and Matthew Inglis reported that a short training in self-explanation improved undergraduates' understanding of proofs. The need for checking is old: Alfred Kempe's 1879 proof of the four colour theorem stood until Percy Heawood found an error in 1890.

## Further reading

- Daniel J. Velleman, *How to Prove It: A Structured Approach*. The standard course on proof strategies by logical form, with scratch work and final proofs kept apart.
- Daniel Solow, *How to Read and Do Proofs: An Introduction to Mathematical Thought Processes*. The forward-backward method, with many exercises in reading short proofs.
- Norman E. Steenrod, Paul R. Halmos, Menahem M. Schiffer and Jean A. Dieudonné, *How to Write Mathematics*. Four short essays, Halmos's the best known, on writing for a reader.
- Donald E. Knuth, Tracy Larrabee and Paul M. Roberts, *Mathematical Writing*. Notes from a course, with many examples of sentences, symbols and displays before and after revision.
- Nicholas J. Higham, *Handbook of Writing for the Mathematical Sciences*. A practical guide to papers, theses and talks, including the structure of a paper.
- Franco Vivaldi, *Mathematical Writing*. A textbook for undergraduates on writing definitions, statements and proofs, with exercises.
