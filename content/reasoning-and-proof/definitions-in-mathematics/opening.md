Try to add fractions by adding tops and bottoms: put $\frac{a}{b} \oplus \frac{c}{d} = \frac{a + c}{b + d}$. Then $\frac12 \oplus \frac13 = \frac25$. But $\frac12$ and $\frac24$ are the same rational number, and $\frac24 \oplus \frac13 = \frac37$, which is not $\frac25$. The rule does not define an operation on rational numbers at all, because its result depends on how the input is written. The usual sum $\frac{ad + bc}{bd}$ does not have this defect, and checking that is part of defining it. A definition looks like a mere agreement about words, and it is one. But many definitions make hidden claims, that something exists, that it is unique, or that it does not depend on a choice, and each such claim needs a proof.

The chapter is about definitions as working tools. It shows what a definition is, the kinds of definition and the proof that each kind owes, what it means for two definitions to be equivalent, when "the" is allowed, and what "unique up to" means. It ends with the terms that are never defined and with the reasons behind the definitions in use: why $1$ is not prime, why a field needs $0 \neq 1$, and why $0! = 1$.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in three groups, read in order.

1. **What a definition is and what it must prove.** A [mathematical definition](/8ZCZ3HW91Z/) has a definiendum and a definiens, and in a definition "if" means "if and only if". [Kinds of definitions](/B60DBNF77S/) lists explicit, recursive, inductive, by abstraction, axiomatic, implicit, by universal property and by construction, each with its obligation. [Well-defined](/HNGZEBKVY2/) gives the test for a rule on classes: $x \sim x'$ must imply $g(x) = g(x')$.
2. **One object, several descriptions.** [Equivalent definitions](/NZ42PS2P1A/) are proved to agree by a chain of implications; in the ring $\Z[\sqrt{-5}]$ two definitions of a prime that agree in $\Z$ come apart. [Definite descriptions](/5P955TVSS2/) need existence and uniqueness, and [up to](/CBC72TX3NQ/) says when uniqueness holds only for an equivalence relation.
3. **Where definitions start and how they change.** A [primitive notion](/QMQEJKFEYZ/) is undefined and fixed by axioms. [Choosing good definitions](/S1R1VD31BS/) shows how theorems and counterexamples shape definitions.

## A first reading

For a first reading, take these six entries in order: [mathematical definition](/8ZCZ3HW91Z/), [kinds of definitions](/B60DBNF77S/), [well-defined](/HNGZEBKVY2/), [equivalent definitions](/NZ42PS2P1A/), [definite descriptions](/5P955TVSS2/) and [up to](/CBC72TX3NQ/). The entry on well-definedness matters most for later work, since every construction with residues, cosets or classes of pairs needs it. Primitive notions and the choice of definitions can wait until you meet axiomatic geometry or a definition that surprises you.

## Before you start

You need [the language of mathematical statements](/WMY13MXY3W/), especially quantifiers and the biconditional, and you need to know what a [proof](/0B94TKFQJX/) is, since each hidden claim of a definition needs one. The uniqueness and well-definedness proofs use direct proof, contradiction and contrapositive from [proof methods](/G9JFJGY4TQ/). For definitions by abstraction you need an [equivalence relation](/0G8VEZ3WTK/), its [equivalence classes](/0W95W33CMP/) and the [quotient set](/0011MTQMR6/). A few examples use groups and residues modulo $n$; they say so where they start.
