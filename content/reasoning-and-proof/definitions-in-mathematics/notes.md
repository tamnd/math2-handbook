## History

Book I of [Euclid's Elements](/83WHDAV68J/) opens with 23 definitions, 5 postulates and 5 common notions. Some of the definitions, such as "a point is that which has no part", are descriptions that no proof uses; others, such as the definition of a circle, do real work. Euclid's lemma, that a prime which divides a product divides one of the factors, is Proposition 30 of Book VII. Many early Greek writers did not count $1$ as a number, so its primality did not arise; from the seventeenth century some mathematicians listed $1$ as the first prime, and many in the nineteenth century still did.

The nineteenth century made the obligations of definitions explicit. Carl Friedrich Gauss introduced congruence and the sign $\equiv$ in *Disquisitiones Arithmeticae* (1801), and his rules for adding and multiplying congruences amount to the statement that the operations on residues are well defined. In the 1840s Ernst Kummer found that unique factorization fails in some rings of cyclotomic integers, which led to the separation of irreducible and prime elements. Gottlob Frege, in *Die Grundlagen der Arithmetik* (1884), discussed definition by abstraction, with the direction of a line defined from parallelism. Richard Dedekind, in *Was sind und was sollen die Zahlen?* (1888), proved that definitions by recursion on the natural numbers are legitimate.

Geometry led the move to undefined terms. Moritz Pasch, in 1882, built geometry on undefined terms and required that proofs not depend on their intuitive meaning. Giuseppe Peano in 1889 and David Hilbert in *Grundlagen der Geometrie* (1899) gave axioms with explicit primitive notions for arithmetic and geometry. Hilbert's remark that points, lines and planes could be replaced by "tables, chairs and beer mugs", as long as the axioms hold, puts the idea in one sentence.

The twentieth century added new kinds of definition and new ways to judge them. Bertrand Russell analysed "the $F$ is $G$" as "exactly one thing is $F$, and it is $G$" in "On Denoting" (1905), and Whitehead and Russell's *Principia Mathematica* (1910 to 1913) wrote descriptions with an inverted iota. Samuel Eilenberg and Saunders Mac Lane founded category theory in 1945, and with it "unique up to unique isomorphism" became the standard uniqueness of universal constructions. Imre Lakatos published *Proofs and Refutations* as four articles in 1963 and 1964 and as a book in 1976; it follows the counterexamples to Euler's polyhedron formula, stated in the 1750s, and the changes to the definition of a polyhedron that they forced.

## Further reading

- Daniel J. Velleman, *How to Prove It: A Structured Approach*, third edition (2019). Treats definitions, equivalence relations, well-defined functions on classes and recursion at the level of a first proof course.
- Imre Lakatos, *Proofs and Refutations: The Logic of Mathematical Discovery* (1976). The classic account of how counterexamples reshape definitions, told through Euler's formula.
- David Hilbert, *Grundlagen der Geometrie* (1899), in English as *Foundations of Geometry*. The model of a theory built on primitive notions and axioms.
- Gottlob Frege, *Die Grundlagen der Arithmetik* (1884), in English as *The Foundations of Arithmetic*. A readable discussion of what a definition of number must achieve, including definition by abstraction.
- Bertrand Russell, "On Denoting", *Mind* (1905). The short paper behind the analysis of definite descriptions.
