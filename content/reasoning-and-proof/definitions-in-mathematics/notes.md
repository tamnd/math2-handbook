## History

Book I of [Euclid's Elements](/83WHDAV68J/) opens with 23 definitions, 5 postulates and 5 common notions. Some of the definitions, such as "a point is that which has no part", are descriptions that no proof ever uses. Others, such as the definition of a circle, do real work in the proofs. Euclid's lemma, that a prime dividing a product divides one of the factors, is Proposition 30 of Book VII. Many Greek writers did not count $1$ as a number at all, so the question of whether it is prime did not arise. From the seventeenth century some mathematicians listed $1$ as the first prime, and many in the nineteenth century still did. The modern convention won because it lets unique factorization be stated without exceptions.

The nineteenth century made explicit what a definition obliges you to prove. Carl Friedrich Gauss introduced congruence and the sign $\equiv$ in *Disquisitiones Arithmeticae* (1801). His rules for adding and multiplying congruences amount to the statement that the operations on residues are well defined. In the 1840s Ernst Kummer found that unique factorization fails in some rings of cyclotomic integers, and this led to separating irreducible elements from prime ones. Gottlob Frege, in *Die Grundlagen der Arithmetik* (1884), discussed definition by abstraction, defining the direction of a line from the relation of parallelism. Richard Dedekind, in *Was sind und was sollen die Zahlen?* (1888), proved that definitions by recursion on the natural numbers are legitimate.

Geometry led the move to undefined terms. In 1882 Moritz Pasch built geometry on undefined terms and required that proofs not depend on what the terms mean intuitively. Giuseppe Peano in 1889, for arithmetic, and David Hilbert in *Grundlagen der Geometrie* (1899), for geometry, gave axioms with explicitly named primitive notions. Hilbert put the idea in one sentence: points, lines and planes could be replaced by "tables, chairs and beer mugs", as long as the axioms hold.

The twentieth century added new kinds of definition and new ways to judge them. In "On Denoting" (1905) Bertrand Russell analysed "the $F$ is $G$" as "exactly one thing is $F$, and it is $G$". Whitehead and Russell's *Principia Mathematica* (1910 to 1913) wrote such descriptions with an inverted iota. Samuel Eilenberg and Saunders Mac Lane founded category theory in 1945. With it, "unique up to unique isomorphism" became the standard form of uniqueness for objects defined by a universal property. Imre Lakatos published *Proofs and Refutations* as four articles in 1963 and 1964, and as a book in 1976. It follows the counterexamples to Euler's polyhedron formula, first stated in the 1750s, and the changes to the definition of a polyhedron that they forced. Two cubes joined at a vertex are one such counterexample.

## Further reading

- Daniel J. Velleman, *How to Prove It: A Structured Approach*. Treats definitions, equivalence relations, functions on classes and recursion at the level of a first proof course, with many exercises.
- Kevin Houston, *How to Think Like a Mathematician*. A first-year guide to reading definitions and using them in proofs.
- Imre Lakatos, *Proofs and Refutations: The Logic of Mathematical Discovery*. The classic account of how counterexamples reshape definitions, told through Euler's formula.
- David Hilbert, *Foundations of Geometry*. The model of a theory built on primitive notions and axioms.
- Gottlob Frege, *The Foundations of Arithmetic*. A readable discussion of what a definition of number must achieve, including definition by abstraction.
- Bertrand Russell, "On Denoting", *Mind* (1905). The short paper behind the analysis of definite descriptions.
