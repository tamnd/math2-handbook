## History

The oldest account of a method of discovery in mathematics is that of Pappus of Alexandria, in the fourth century CE. In Book 7 of his *Collection* he described analysis, which takes what is sought as granted and passes back to something admitted, and synthesis, which runs the same chain forwards. He separated theoretical analysis, which seeks a proof, from problematical analysis, which seeks a construction. In the seventeenth century René Descartes, in the *Discourse on the Method* (1637), gave rules for dividing a difficulty into parts and proceeding from the simple to the complex, and his geometry, published with the *Discourse*, turned problems about figures into algebra. Leibniz planned a general art of discovery.

George Pólya revived the subject for mathematics. *How to Solve It* (1945) grew out of his teaching at ETH Zurich and at Stanford. It has a short part on the four phases of a solution, a dialogue between a teacher and a class, and a "short dictionary of heuristic" with entries on analogy, specialization, generalization, auxiliary problems and elements, working backwards, the inventor's paradox and more. A second edition appeared in 1957. Pólya went on with *Mathematics and Plausible Reasoning* (two volumes, 1954) and *Mathematical Discovery* (two volumes, 1962 and 1965).

Imre Lakatos (1922 to 1974), who had translated *How to Solve It* into Hungarian, wrote his doctoral thesis of 1961 on the logic of mathematical discovery. A version of its first chapter appeared as "Proofs and Refutations" in four parts in the *British Journal for the Philosophy of Science* in 1963 and 1964. The book of the same name, edited by John Worrall and Elie Zahar, appeared in 1976, after his death. It is a classroom dialogue about Euler's polyhedron formula, and it draws on Pólya and on Karl Popper.

The psychology of invention has its own line. Henri Poincaré lectured in Paris in 1908 on mathematical invention, and the lecture became a chapter of *Science and Method*; his story of the idea that came to him as he stepped onto an omnibus at Coutances is the best known report of its kind. The psychologist Édouard Toulouse published a study of Poincaré's habits of work in 1910. Graham Wallas named the four stages of preparation, incubation, illumination and verification in *The Art of Thought* (1926). Jacques Hadamard collected reports from mathematicians and scientists, among them a letter from Einstein, in *An Essay on the Psychology of Invention in the Mathematical Field* (1945). Ut Na Sio and Thomas Ormerod's meta-analysis of 2009 found that incubation helps in laboratory problems, with effects that depend on the task.

Research on teaching problem solving tested how much the named heuristics help. Alan Schoenfeld's *Mathematical Problem Solving* (1985) found that knowing the names of heuristics is not enough: a solver also needs knowledge, control of the solving process and suitable beliefs about mathematics. John Mason, Leone Burton and Kaye Stacey put specializing and generalizing at the centre of *Thinking Mathematically* (1982), and Stephen Brown and Marion Walter turned systematic variation into a method of posing problems in *The Art of Problem Posing*.

## Further reading

- George Pólya, *How to Solve It: A New Aspect of Mathematical Method*, Princeton University Press (1945; second edition 1957). The source of most of the chapter; read the dictionary of heuristic slowly, one entry at a time.
- George Pólya, *Mathematics and Plausible Reasoning*, two volumes, Princeton University Press (1954). Many worked cases of analogy, specialization and generalization, most of them from Euler.
- George Pólya, *Mathematical Discovery: On Understanding, Learning, and Teaching Problem Solving*, two volumes (1962 and 1965). Patterns of solution with many exercises, aimed at teachers.
- Imre Lakatos, *Proofs and Refutations: The Logic of Mathematical Discovery*, edited by John Worrall and Elie Zahar, Cambridge University Press (1976). The dialogue on Euler's formula; the footnotes give the real history.
- Jacques Hadamard, *An Essay on the Psychology of Invention in the Mathematical Field*, Princeton University Press (1945). The classic inquiry into how ideas come, built on Poincaré's account.
- Alan H. Schoenfeld, *Mathematical Problem Solving*, Academic Press (1985). What experts and students actually do with heuristics, and why lists of heuristics are not enough.
