## History

The oldest account of a method of discovery is in Book 7 of the *Collection* of Pappus of Alexandria, who worked in the fourth century CE. Pappus separates analysis, which assumes what is sought and works back to something known, from synthesis, which runs the steps forwards as a proof. In the seventeenth century Descartes and Leibniz both planned a general art of discovery. Descartes's *Discourse on the Method* (1637) gives as its second rule the division of each difficulty into as many parts as possible, and his *La Géométrie* of the same year showed what a good notation for unknowns can do. Euler's work of the 1730s gives a famous case of analogy: he found the sum of the reciprocal squares by treating $\sin x$ like a polynomial with known roots, and he gave other arguments for it later.

George Pólya made the subject a part of teaching. *How to Solve It* (1945, second edition 1957) gives the four phases of solving a problem, a list of questions for each, and a dictionary of heuristics, with entries on working backwards and on Pappus. *Mathematics and Plausible Reasoning* (1954) studies induction and analogy as the means by which conjectures are found, and *Mathematical Discovery* (1962 and 1965) returns to problem solving for teachers. Mason, Burton and Stacey, in *Thinking Mathematically* (1982), use three phases called entry, attack and review. Alan Schoenfeld's *Mathematical Problem Solving* (1985) studied students and experts at work and found that knowing the names of heuristics is not enough: a solver also needs control, the decision when to drop a plan, and suitable beliefs about mathematics.

Imre Lakatos (1922 to 1974), who had translated *How to Solve It* into Hungarian, turned heuristics into a philosophy of mathematical practice. His doctoral thesis of 1961, *Essays in the Logic of Mathematical Discovery*, became a series of articles in the *British Journal for the Philosophy of Science* in 1963 and 1964, and then the book *Proofs and Refutations*, edited by John Worrall and Elie Zahar and published in 1976, after his death. Its dialogue follows Cauchy's proof of Euler's formula for polyhedra through its counterexamples, in analogy with Karl Popper's account of conjectures and refutations in science, and argues that hypotheses such as "the surface is a topological sphere" and uniform convergence are generated by proofs.

The psychology of invention has its own line. In *Science et méthode* (1908) Henri Poincaré told how, after a period of work on Fuchsian functions, the key idea came to him as he stepped onto an omnibus at Coutances. Graham Wallas, drawing on such reports by Poincaré and Hermann von Helmholtz, named four stages in *The Art of Thought* (1926): preparation, incubation, illumination and verification. Jacques Hadamard's *The Psychology of Invention in the Mathematical Field* (1945), reissued in 1996 as *The Mathematician's Mind*, collected answers from mathematicians and scientists, among them a letter from Einstein. The posing of problems was studied too: David Hilbert's list of $23$ problems of 1900 is the best-known case of posing as research, and Brown and Walter made systematic variation into a method in *The Art of Problem Posing* (1983).

## Further reading

- George Pólya, *How to Solve It: A New Aspect of Mathematical Method*. The source of the four phases and of most of the heuristics in this chapter, with many short worked problems.
- George Pólya, *Mathematics and Plausible Reasoning* (two volumes: *Induction and Analogy in Mathematics* and *Patterns of Plausible Inference*). How conjectures are found and made credible, with Euler's discoveries as a running example.
- Imre Lakatos, *Proofs and Refutations: The Logic of Mathematical Discovery*. The dialogue on Euler's formula, and the best source for monster-barring and lemma incorporation.
- Jacques Hadamard, *The Psychology of Invention in the Mathematical Field*. A short book on how mathematicians report their discoveries, including Poincaré's account.
- John Mason, Leone Burton and Kaye Stacey, *Thinking Mathematically*. Problems for working alone, with advice on getting started and on what to do when stuck.
- Arthur Engel, *Problem-Solving Strategies*. A large collection of competition problems arranged by method, for practising the heuristics on harder material.
