A finished proof shows that a theorem is true. It seldom shows how anyone found the theorem, or the proof. This chapter is about that earlier stage: the rules of thumb, called heuristics, by which mathematicians find answers and arguments. "Try a special case", "work backwards from the goal", "solve a more general problem", "draw a figure": each of these often leads to an idea, and none of them is sure to.

Two small cases show the pattern. To find $1^3 + 2^3 + \cdots + n^3$, compute the first values $1, 9, 36, 100$. They are the squares of $1, 3, 6, 10$, the triangular numbers, and this suggests the formula $\left(\frac{n(n+1)}{2}\right)^2$; an induction then proves it. To find the last two digits of $3^{100}$, split the problem: work modulo $4$ and modulo $25$ separately, and put the two answers together. In both cases a heuristic finds the answer and a proof makes it certain. The chapter keeps that division throughout. Each entry on a single heuristic solves a real problem with it, and then shows a problem on which the same heuristic misleads.

Most of the heuristics come from George Pólya's *How to Solve It* (1945). The later entries widen the view: Imre Lakatos on how counterexamples reshape a theorem, the uses and the risks of intuition, what Poincaré and Hadamard reported about sudden ideas, and the art of posing new problems.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in five groups, read in order.

1. **What a heuristic is.** [Heuristics in mathematics](/6KCEWEP4WA/) separates a heuristic from an algorithm, which always ends with a correct answer, and from a proof. [Pólya's four steps](/DYD57G2Y5Z/) give a plan for a whole solution: understand the problem, devise a plan, carry it out, look back.
2. **Changing the size of the problem.** [Analogy](/7KDT0R1HF2/) moves to a similar problem, [specialization](/FTBJX4G51S/) to fewer cases, [generalization](/8NJ0VN9Z97/) to more. The [inventor's paradox](/FVRKDXBJF6/) says that the more ambitious problem can be the easier one.
3. **Starting from the goal.** [Working backwards](/3QTB9S1THS/) asks what would come just before the goal, as in the analysis and synthesis of Pappus. An [auxiliary problem](/DYSJB93V11/) is a related problem solved first because it helps.
4. **Adding, splitting and changing.** [Auxiliary elements](/8TKKRAXKFN/) such as a line or a function, [decomposing and recombining](/KEZPDNM2NH/), [variation of the problem](/KT5SASGTQ5/), and the advice to [draw a figure and introduce notation](/6CVYB413ER/).
5. **Discovery beyond single rules.** Lakatos's [proofs and refutations](/79P9CTRN0V/), [mathematical intuition](/4BT08RE4CR/), the [psychology of mathematical invention](/XMGGTNSTYC/) and [problem posing](/Y896GWYF8A/).

## A first reading

For a first reading, take these nine entries in order: [heuristics in mathematics](/6KCEWEP4WA/), [Pólya's four steps](/DYD57G2Y5Z/), [specialization](/FTBJX4G51S/), [generalization](/8NJ0VN9Z97/), [the inventor's paradox](/FVRKDXBJF6/), [working backwards](/3QTB9S1THS/), [auxiliary problem](/DYSJB93V11/), [auxiliary elements](/8TKKRAXKFN/) and [proofs and refutations](/79P9CTRN0V/). With each one, read at least one example where the heuristic works and the one where it misleads; the pair is the point of the entry. The entries on intuition, invention and problem posing describe rather than teach, and can wait.

The heuristics are not learned by reading about them. Take a problem from another chapter that you could not solve, and go through the questions of the four steps with it. Most of the exercises at the end of this chapter name the heuristic to try.

## Before you start

You need to know what a [mathematical proof](/0B94TKFQJX/) is, and how a [conjecture](/WMEMMHZ3BH/) and a [counterexample](/0ED72CTBNP/) differ from a theorem; the chapter on [proof, theorems and mathematical method](/N4P3E53HM0/) covers these, together with [deductive, inductive and abductive reasoning](/5V35SGEN1T/). The later entries use the [forward-backward method](/V8ZB838JY5/) and proofs by [mathematical induction](/0ASJVEFA9J/) from [basic proof methods](/958EF46EYJ/). The examples use school algebra and geometry, some first-year calculus and a little elementary number theory, such as congruences.
