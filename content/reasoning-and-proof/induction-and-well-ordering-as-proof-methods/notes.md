## History

Arguments from a smallest case are older than induction in its modern form. Proposition 31 of Book VII of Euclid's *Elements* proves that every composite number is measured by some prime. If the divisors kept being composite, there would be an endless decreasing sequence of numbers, and Euclid notes that this is impossible among numbers. In the seventeenth century Pierre de Fermat made this into a method, which he called infinite descent. He used it to prove that the area of a right triangle with integer sides cannot be a square.

Induction from $n$ to $n + 1$ appears in Francesco Maurolico's *Arithmeticorum libri duo* (1575), where he proves that the sum of the first $n$ odd numbers is $n^2$. Blaise Pascal wrote his *Traité du triangle arithmétique* in 1654, and it was published in 1665. In it he states the two parts of the method, a first case and a passage from each case to the next, and uses them to prove properties of the arithmetic triangle. Augustus De Morgan gave the method the name "mathematical induction" in the nineteenth century. Richard Dedekind, in *Was sind und was sollen die Zahlen?* (1888), derived induction and definition by recursion from his theory of chains. Giuseppe Peano made induction one of his axioms for the natural numbers in 1889.

Augustin-Louis Cauchy gave the forward-backward proof of the AM-GM inequality in his *Cours d'analyse* (1821). Wilhelm Ackermann published his function in 1928, and its proofs of growth are standard examples of double induction. Rod Burstall's paper "Proving properties of programs by structural induction" (1969) introduced the term into computer science. Solomon Golomb's 1954 article on polyominoes in the *American Mathematical Monthly* contains the tromino tiling of a $2^n \times 2^n$ board with one square missing. A version of the false proof that all horses have the same colour appears as an exercise in George Pólya's *Induction and Analogy in Mathematics* (1954).

## Further reading

- Daniel J. Velleman, *How to Prove It: A Structured Approach*. Its chapter on induction covers ordinary and strong induction, recursion and the well-ordering principle, with careful attention to how a proof is written.
- Richard Hammack, *Book of Proof*. A free first course in proof with a full chapter of induction exercises at several levels.
- Arthur Engel, *Problem-Solving Strategies*. Its chapters on induction and on the extremal principle collect competition problems that use these methods in strong form.
- George Pólya, *Induction and Analogy in Mathematics*. Shows how a guess is found from small cases and then proved by induction, and why the guess alone is not enough.
- Kevin Houston, *How to Think Like a Mathematician*. Short chapters on induction and on common errors for a reader new to proofs.
