## History

Arguments by induction appeared long before the method had a name. Euclid's Elements, Book VII, Proposition 31, proves that every composite number has a prime divisor by an argument of descent: a composite number has a smaller divisor, and the divisors cannot decrease for ever. Implicit uses of induction go back to al-Karaji around the year 1000 and to Gersonides. Francesco Maurolico used the method in 1575 to prove that the sum of the first $n$ odd numbers is $n^2$, and Blaise Pascal gave it an explicit form in his Traité du triangle arithmétique (1665).

Fermat developed the descent argument in the seventeenth century, named it descente infinie, and used it on Diophantine problems, among them his theorem on right triangles. This is the well-ordering principle used long before anyone stated it. Descent stayed a tool of number theory: in the twentieth century the Mordell-Weil theorem used a descent argument for the rational points of an elliptic curve, and Weil extended it to abelian varieties. In 1821 Augustin-Louis Cauchy, in his Cours d'analyse, proved the inequality of arithmetic and geometric means by forward-backward induction, first for $2^k$ numbers and then stepping down from $n$ numbers to $n - 1$.

The formal treatment of induction came in the nineteenth century, with Boole, De Morgan, Peirce, Dedekind and Peano. Dedekind, in Was sind und was sollen die Zahlen? (1888), defined the natural numbers as the intersection of all sets that contain the first number and are closed under the successor, which is an inductive definition in the modern sense, and he proved that definitions by recursion on the natural numbers are legitimate. Peano's axioms made induction one of the axioms of arithmetic.

The twentieth century extended induction to two variables and to structures other than numbers. Wilhelm Ackermann published his function in 1928, and nested induction is the standard way to prove that its recursion gives a value for every pair; the two-variable form now in most books is due to Rózsa Péter and Raphael Robinson. Péter studied recursion on structures more general than the natural numbers in 1959, and Rod Burstall's paper "Proving properties of programs by structural induction" (The Computer Journal, 1969) brought structural induction into computer science. Solomon Golomb's 1954 proof that a deficient $2^n \times 2^n$ board can be tiled by L-trominoes became the standard picture proof by induction.

The false proofs have a history of their own. George Pólya, in Induction and Analogy in Mathematics (1954), gave the argument that any $n$ numbers are equal as an exercise on the misuse of induction, and Joel Cohen recast it in 1961, in a satirical article, as the proof that all horses are the same colour.

## Further reading

- Daniel J. Velleman, *How to Prove It: A Structured Approach* (3rd edition, Cambridge University Press, 2019). Chapter 6 covers ordinary and strong induction, recursion and closures with many exercises, at the level of this chapter.
- George Pólya, *Induction and Analogy in Mathematics* (Princeton University Press, 1954). The first volume of Mathematics and Plausible Reasoning; it shows how induction proofs are found, not only how they are checked.
- Arthur Engel, *Problem-Solving Strategies* (Springer, 1998). Its chapters on induction and on the extremal principle give many competition problems solved by the methods of this chapter.
- Eric Lehman, F. Thomson Leighton and Albert R. Meyer, *Mathematics for Computer Science*. Free lecture notes with a careful treatment of induction, the well-ordering principle and structural induction over recursive data types.
- Richard Dedekind, *Was sind und was sollen die Zahlen?* (1888), translated as "The nature and meaning of numbers" in Essays on the Theory of Numbers. The original source for inductive definition and the justification of recursion.
