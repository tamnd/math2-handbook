## History

The patterns of this chapter are older than the names now used for them. Archimedes found the area of a segment of a parabola by showing that the area can be neither larger nor smaller than his value, each case refuted by a contradiction; this two-sided method of exhaustion is the ancestor of equality by two inequalities and of the modern epsilon proofs. Euclid's *Elements*, Book VII, Propositions 1 and 2, find the greatest common measure of two numbers by repeated subtraction, the oldest well-known algorithm that also serves as a proof. Leonardo of Pisa (Fibonacci) described the greedy method for Egyptian fractions in his *Liber Abaci* of 1202. Algebraic identities appear, stated in words, long before algebraic symbols, as in the *Arithmetica* of Diophantus, and Gerolamo Cardano's *Ars Magna* (1545) reduced the general cubic to a depressed one by a translation, a reduction to a special case.

The language of sets, functions and relations came much later. Gauss's *Disquisitiones Arithmeticae* (1801) treated congruence of integers, one of the first equivalence relations studied for its own sake, and Frege's *Foundations of Arithmetic* (1884) defined the direction of a line from the relation of parallelism, an early definition by abstraction. Ernst Zermelo listed the axiom of extensionality, on which double inclusion rests, as the first axiom of his 1908 axiom system for set theory. The words injection, surjection and bijection come from the Bourbaki group; many English textbooks still say "one-to-one" and "onto".

The estimates of analysis took their present form in the nineteenth century. Bernard Bolzano in 1817 and Augustin-Louis Cauchy in 1821 described limits and continuity in terms of quantities that become small, and Karl Weierstrass and his students made the epsilon-delta definition standard. Cauchy also claimed in 1821 that a convergent series of continuous functions has a continuous sum; Niels Henrik Abel pointed to counterexamples among Fourier series in 1826, and the corrected theorem, for uniform convergence, is the model epsilon over three argument. Weierstrass proved in 1885 that polynomials are dense in the continuous functions on a closed bounded interval, which supplies the dense class for many density arguments, and Michel Plancherel proved in 1910 that the Fourier transform preserves the $L^2$ norm, which lets the transform be extended from a dense subspace to all of $L^2(\R)$.

In the twentieth century some patterns were named and taught as methods. George Pólya's *How to Solve It* (1945) listed symmetry and specialization among its heuristics. Thomas Hakon Grönwall published his inequality in 1919, and it became a standard step in continuity arguments for differential equations. The existence proof by algorithm gained new examples, such as the stable matchings that David Gale and Lloyd Shapley built in 1962, and Robert Floyd in 1967 and Tony Hoare in 1969 made invariants and variants the standard tools for proving programs correct.

## Further reading

- Daniel J. Velleman, *How to Prove It: A Structured Approach*. Proof strategies organized by the logical form of the goal, with many exercises on sets, functions and relations.
- Richard Hammack, *Book of Proof*. A first course in proofs, with chapters on proving set equalities, properties of functions and equivalence relations.
- Stephen Abbott, *Understanding Analysis*. A careful first course in real analysis, with the epsilon-delta, uniform convergence and density arguments of the middle of this chapter.
- George Pólya, *How to Solve It*. A short guide to heuristics, including symmetry, specialization and working backwards.
- Donald E. Knuth, *The Art of Computer Programming, Volume 1: Fundamental Algorithms*. Opens with Euclid's algorithm and proves algorithms correct with invariants.
