## History

Several of the patterns are as old as Greek geometry. Euclid's *Elements* proves that two magnitudes are equal by showing that neither is greater than the other, and the method of exhaustion of Eudoxus and Archimedes is an equality by two inequalities: an area is shown to be neither larger nor smaller than a given value, each case leading to a contradiction. The same books contain the oldest proof by algorithm, the procedure for the greatest common divisor in Book VII, propositions 1 and 2, whose correctness rests on the fact that the common divisors of the pair do not change from step to step. Fibonacci's *Liber Abaci* (1202) gives the greedy method for writing a fraction as a sum of distinct unit fractions.

Symmetry and reduction belong to the algebra of the following centuries. Al-Khwarizmi solves quadratic equations by completing the square, a change of variable that removes the linear term, and Cardano's *Ars Magna* (1545) does the same for the cubic, reducing it to the depressed form by the substitution $x = y - a/3$. Gauss introduced congruence and its notation in the *Disquisitiones Arithmeticae* (1801), and Frege's *Grundlagen der Arithmetik* (1884) defined numbers through classes of equinumerous concepts, an early use of equivalence classes as objects. Zermelo stated the axiom of extensionality, which makes double inclusion a proof of equality, in his axioms for set theory of 1908. The words injection, surjection and bijection come from Bourbaki.

The analysis patterns grew out of the effort to make the calculus rigorous. Bolzano in 1817 and Cauchy in his *Cours d'analyse* (1821) defined continuity by inequalities, and Weierstrass gave the definition the $\varepsilon$-$\delta$ form now in use. Cauchy also claimed that a convergent series of continuous functions has a continuous sum; Abel gave a counterexample in 1826, a Fourier series with a jump, and the repair was the notion of uniform convergence, with the $\varepsilon/3$ argument as its proof. The density argument became routine with the approximation theorem of Weierstrass (1885) and the extension of the Fourier transform to $L^2$ by Plancherel (1910).

The global patterns came later. Grönwall's inequality (1919) is the prototype of the a priori bounds that bootstrapping improves, and continuity arguments of this kind run through the theory of nonlinear differential equations, as in Yau's continuity method for the Calabi conjecture. Open-closed arguments appear wherever connectedness is used: in the identity theorem for analytic functions and in uniqueness proofs for differential equations. In computer science, Floyd (1967) and Hoare (1969) made the pairing of an invariant with a decreasing measure into a method for proving programs correct, and the Gale–Shapley algorithm (1962) is a well-known existence proof by procedure. Pólya's *How to Solve It* (1945) collected many of these patterns, under names such as "specialization" and "symmetry", as heuristics for students.

## Further reading

- Daniel J. Velleman, *How to Prove It: A Structured Approach*, 3rd edition (2019). The standard introduction to proofs organized by the logical form of the goal, with many exercises on sets, functions and relations.
- George Pólya, *How to Solve It* (1945). The short dictionary of heuristic in its third part treats symmetry, specialization, generalization and working backwards.
- Walter Rudin, *Principles of Mathematical Analysis*, 3rd edition (1976). A compact course in which the epsilon-delta, $\varepsilon/3$ and density patterns appear in their standard form.
- Terence Tao, *Nonlinear Dispersive Equations: Local and Global Analysis* (2006). Its first chapter states the abstract bootstrap principle and uses it throughout.
