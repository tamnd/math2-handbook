Some proofs do not follow from the definitions of the objects. They come from a count, a quantity that does not change, or a choice of the largest object. Take any five points in a square of side $2$. Cut the square into four squares of side $1$; two of the points lie in the same small square, so their distance is at most $\sqrt 2$. Nothing about the positions of the points was used, only that five objects went into four boxes. Or take a chessboard with two opposite corners removed. Each domino covers one white and one black square, the two removed corners have the same colour, and so the $62$ remaining squares cannot be covered by $31$ dominoes. The colouring is an invariant of every tiling, and it rules out all of them at once.

These methods come from combinatorics, but they are used in every area of mathematics. The pigeonhole principle gives rational approximations in number theory. Double counting proves identities and the handshaking lemma. Monovariants prove that algorithms stop. Cantor's diagonal argument proves that the real numbers are uncountable, and Erdős's count of colourings gives a lower bound for Ramsey numbers without exhibiting any colouring. Each entry of the chapter states one method, shows it on worked examples, and gives a non-example where the method does not apply.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in five groups.

1. **The pigeonhole principle.** The [pigeonhole principle](/0HGVCWQGY3/), its [generalized form](/0BY7GN3449/) with $\ceil{m/n}$ objects in some box, the [infinite form](/0FS3RHF6KE/), and four [applications](/5EAFG80VX6/).
2. **Counting in two ways.** [Double counting](/0F8YBHF2W3/) of a set of pairs, the [bijection principle](/0GV2573FP4/), and [combinatorial proofs](/SS07J9ZAQ7/) of identities.
3. **Invariants and monovariants.** An [invariant](/8NQ190278Y/) shows that a target cannot be reached, and a [monovariant](/TP3GC5J08D/) shows that a process stops. [Parity arguments](/H9JT1XNDKQ/) and [colouring proofs](/90Q4PK1CST/) are the two most common kinds.
4. **Extremal and averaging arguments.** The [extremal principle](/MD3MRG90W4/) takes a largest or smallest object, and an [averaging argument](/HXP9VMXV8S/) finds an element at least as large as the mean.
5. **Existence by counting, probability and diagonals.** [Counting arguments](/1KVSS0KRZA/) and [probabilistic proofs](/6YY8W7HG5M/) show that an object exists without building it, and [diagonalization](/V2H30SA654/) builds an object missing from a list.

## A first reading

For a first reading, take these entries in order: the [pigeonhole principle](/0HGVCWQGY3/), the [generalized pigeonhole principle](/0BY7GN3449/), its [applications](/5EAFG80VX6/), [double counting](/0F8YBHF2W3/), [combinatorial proof](/SS07J9ZAQ7/), [invariants](/8NQ190278Y/), [monovariants](/TP3GC5J08D/), [colouring proofs](/90Q4PK1CST/), the [extremal principle](/MD3MRG90W4/) and [diagonalization](/V2H30SA654/). Read the non-examples with care: each shows a quantity or a count that looks useful and decides nothing.

On a first pass you can skip the [infinite pigeonhole principle](/0FS3RHF6KE/) and the [bijection principle](/0GV2573FP4/), whose content is mostly in the entries around them, and the entry on [probabilistic proofs](/6YY8W7HG5M/), which assumes some analysis and probability.

## Before you start

You need the methods of [basic proof methods](/958EF46EYJ/), above all [proof by contradiction](/0J0XBKG64S/), which most entries here use.
