## History

The pigeonhole principle was used long before it had a name. Jean Leurechon's *Selectæ Propositiones* (1622) argues that two people must have the same number of hairs on their heads. Peter Gustav Lejeune Dirichlet used it in number theory in the nineteenth century, among other places for his theorem on approximating real numbers by rationals, and German writers called it the *Schubfachprinzip*, the drawer principle. Paul Erdős and George Szekeres proved their theorem on monotone subsequences in 1935.

Leonhard Euler's 1736 paper on the bridges of Königsberg is an early parity argument: a walk that crosses each bridge once must enter and leave each land mass, and all four land masses meet an odd number of bridges. The fact that the degrees of a graph add up to twice the number of edges, the first example of double counting, is in the same paper. The 15 puzzle became a craze around 1880, and in 1879 William Johnson and William Story proved in the *American Journal of Mathematics* that exactly half of the arrangements can be reached. The mutilated chessboard appears in Max Black's *Critical Thinking* (1946), and Martin Gardner made it widely known; Solomon Golomb developed colouring arguments for polyominoes from 1954 on.

James Joseph Sylvester posed the problem behind the Sylvester-Gallai theorem in 1893, and Tibor Gallai published a proof in 1944. The short extremal proof is due to Leroy Kelly. Georg Cantor proved in 1874 that the algebraic numbers are countable and the real numbers are not, which gave a new proof that transcendental numbers exist; he introduced the diagonal argument in 1891. Erdős proved his lower bound on Ramsey numbers by counting colourings in 1947, and Claude Shannon proved in 1949 that most Boolean functions need large circuits. Sergei Bernstein gave his probabilistic proof of the Weierstrass approximation theorem in 1912. The diagonal method reached logic and computation with Kurt Gödel's incompleteness theorems of 1931 and Alan Turing's proof of 1936 that the halting problem is undecidable.

## Further reading

- Arthur Engel, *Problem-Solving Strategies*. Its opening chapters on invariants, colouring, the extremal principle and the box principle give hundreds of graded problems with solutions.
- George Pólya, *How to Solve It*. The classic account of how to look for an unknown, an auxiliary problem and a special case, which is how the right boxes or the right invariant are found.
- Arthur T. Benjamin and Jennifer J. Quinn, *Proofs That Really Count: The Art of Combinatorial Proof*. Proves hundreds of identities by naming the set that both sides count.
- Martin Aigner and Günter M. Ziegler, *Proofs from THE BOOK*. Contains short proofs by pigeonhole, double counting and probabilistic arguments, among them Sylvester-Gallai and the lower bound for Ramsey numbers.
- Noga Alon and Joel H. Spencer, *The Probabilistic Method*. The standard text on proving existence by counting and probability, for a reader who wants to go further.
