## History

Several of these methods are as old as proof itself. The Euclidean algorithm, in Book VII of Euclid's Elements, stops because the remainders decrease, which is a monovariant argument, and Fermat's infinite descent in the seventeenth century is another. Jean Leurechon stated a form of the pigeonhole principle in 1622: two people have the same number of hairs on their heads. Dirichlet used the principle in number theory in the nineteenth century, and it is still called Dirichlet's box principle, or in German the Schubfachprinzip, the drawer principle. Leonhard Euler's paper of 1736 on the bridges of Königsberg used a parity argument to show that the walk does not exist, and proved that the degrees of a graph add up to twice the number of edges, one of the oldest double counting arguments.

Combinatorial proof grew alongside the algebra of generating functions. Euler proved in 1748 that the partitions of a number into odd parts and into distinct parts are equally many, using generating functions, and James Glaisher gave a bijective proof in 1883. Heinz Prüfer's bijective proof of Cayley's formula for labelled trees followed in 1918. Invariants entered recreational mathematics with the 15 puzzle: in 1879 William Woolsey Johnson and William Edward Story used a parity invariant to show that half of its starting positions cannot be solved. The mutilated chessboard was posed by the philosopher Max Black in Critical Thinking (1946); Solomon Golomb discussed it in 1954, Martin Gardner wrote about it in his Mathematical Games column in 1957, and Ralph Gomory proved that removing any two cells of different colours leaves a region that dominoes can tile.

The extremal principle is behind many twentieth-century results. Sylvester posed the problem of ordinary lines in 1893, Tibor Gallai published a proof in 1944, and Leroy Milton Kelly found the proof by least distance. Paul Erdős and George Szekeres proved their theorem on monotone subsequences in 1935. Oskar Perron's paradox, "the largest positive integer is $1$", is the standard warning that an extremal argument needs the extremal object to exist.

Cantor proved in 1874 that the algebraic numbers are countable and the real numbers are not, which gave a new proof that transcendental numbers exist. His diagonal argument of 1891 showed that the binary sequences are uncountable and that a set is smaller than its power set. Gödel's incompleteness theorems of 1931, Turing's proof of the undecidability of the halting problem in 1936, and the time hierarchy theorem of Juris Hartmanis and Richard Stearns of 1965 all use diagonal constructions.

Counting and probability became methods of existence proof with Erdős. In 1947 he proved a lower bound on Ramsey numbers by counting colourings, and in 1965 he proved that every set of $n$ nonzero integers has a sum-free subset with more than $n/3$ elements, by averaging. Claude Shannon proved in 1949 that most Boolean functions need large circuits. Probabilistic proofs of theorems outside probability are older: Sergei Bernstein gave a probabilistic proof of the Weierstrass approximation theorem in 1912, and Émile Borel proved in 1909 that almost every real number is normal.

## Further reading

- Martin Aigner and Günter M. Ziegler, *Proofs from THE BOOK* (Springer). Short, complete proofs, with a chapter on the pigeonhole principle and double counting and many proofs by the extremal and probabilistic methods.
- Arthur Engel, *Problem-Solving Strategies* (Springer, 1998). Chapters on invariants, colouring, the extremal principle and the box principle, each with a large set of solved competition problems.
- Arthur T. Benjamin and Jennifer J. Quinn, *Proofs That Really Count: The Art of Combinatorial Proof* (Mathematical Association of America, 2003). Hundreds of identities proved by counting, the best place to practise combinatorial proof.
- Noga Alon and Joel H. Spencer, *The Probabilistic Method* (Wiley). The standard text on probabilistic existence proofs, from Erdős's Ramsey bound onwards; it assumes basic probability.
- Mitchel T. Keller and William T. Trotter, *Applied Combinatorics*. A free textbook with clear chapters on the pigeonhole principle, combinatorial proof and basic Ramsey theory.
