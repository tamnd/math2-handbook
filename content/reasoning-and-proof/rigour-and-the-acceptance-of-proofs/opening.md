Euclid's first proposition constructs an equilateral triangle on a given segment by drawing two circles and taking a point where they meet. For two thousand years this was a model of proof. Yet nothing in Euclid's axioms says the circles meet: in the plane of points with rational coordinates the two circles on the segment from $(0, 0)$ to $(1, 0)$ have no common point. The proof was rigorous for its readers and is not rigorous for ours. Standards of rigour change, and they depend on who is reading.

The question of what makes a proof acceptable became pressing in the twentieth century. The four colour theorem was proved in 1976 with a computer check of more than a thousand configurations that no person has done by hand. The classification of finite simple groups runs to many thousands of pages by about a hundred authors. A claimed proof of the abc conjecture was released in 2012 and is still disputed. This chapter is about rigour, about the kinds of argument that count as proofs, and about how mathematicians come to accept them.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in four groups, read in order.

1. **What rigour is.** [Mathematical rigour](/E1PE9R8DBB/) is relative to a reader and a stock of accepted facts. [Formal and informal proofs](/8VZ77R4R8S/) separates derivations in a formal system from the proofs of daily practice, and the [history of proof and rigour](/20F7K9RVT6/) runs from Greek geometry through the rigour of analysis to the computer.
2. **Pictures and computers.** A [proof without words](/V6ESFEFKJC/) is a picture that makes an identity visible. A [computer-assisted proof](/MC18C7BMFT/) hands a finite list of checks to a program, as for the four colour theorem and the Kepler conjecture.
3. **Length and acceptance.** [Long proofs and surveyability](/RM0S65BHWH/) treats proofs that no single reader checks, and [acceptance of proofs](/MJ3EHQJJHX/) treats refereeing, consensus, errors and contested claims.
4. **What proofs are for.** [Many proofs of one theorem](/KNQDDN6PT2/) says why known theorems are proved again, [the Jaffe–Quinn and Thurston debate](/K3M3TEX7SJ/) weighs speculation against proof, and [proofs from the Book](/A4RJYP79A7/) describes Erdős's ideal.

## A first reading

Read [mathematical rigour](/E1PE9R8DBB/), [formal and informal proofs](/8VZ77R4R8S/), [proof without words](/V6ESFEFKJC/), [computer-assisted proof](/MC18C7BMFT/), [acceptance of proofs](/MJ3EHQJJHX/) and [proofs from the Book](/A4RJYP79A7/). Together they show what a proof must do and the main ways in which practice departs from the textbook picture. The history and the debate can follow, and they read best after the others.

## Before you start

You need to know what a [mathematical proof](/0B94TKFQJX/) is and what it is for, from [proof, theorems and mathematical method](/N4P3E53HM0/), and the [basic proof methods](/958EF46EYJ/): direct proof, contradiction, contrapositive and induction. A few examples use the [intermediate value theorem](/00KAFBR32E/) and [uniform convergence](/08VZ57PT26/) from first-year analysis. The entries on formal proof mention [axioms](/GD2JBXJV5Z/), [proof assistants](/YM6PRDE88V/) and the [incompleteness theorems](/X9WYNYE29C/), but they do not need their details.
