## History

Deductive proof begins in Greek geometry, with results attributed to Thales and Hippocrates of Chios, and Eudoxus' method of exhaustion gave a rigorous treatment of areas and volumes. Euclid's *Elements*, around 300 BC, set the model of definitions, postulates and propositions that lasted two thousand years. Other traditions proved too: Liu Hui's commentary on the *Nine Chapters* (263 CE) justifies its procedures, al-Karaji around 1000 argued in the style of induction, and Jyeṣṭhadeva's *Yuktibhāṣā*, around 1530, gives proofs of the Kerala series for $\pi$.

The calculus of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries ran ahead of its foundations, and Berkeley's *The Analyst* (1734) mocked its vanishing quantities. Bolzano proved the intermediate value theorem in 1817, and Cauchy's *Cours d'analyse* (1821) defined limits and continuity, though it also stated that a convergent sum of continuous functions is continuous, which Abel contradicted in 1826. Weierstrass's continuous nowhere differentiable function and Dedekind's construction of the real numbers both appeared in 1872. Hilbert's *Foundations of Geometry* (1899) supplied the axioms of order and continuity that Euclid had used without stating. Frege's *Begriffsschrift* (1879), *Principia Mathematica* (1910–1913) and Gödel's incompleteness theorems (1931) made proof itself a mathematical object, and from the late 1930s Bourbaki wrote mathematics in a uniform axiomatic style.

Computers changed the question. Appel and Haken announced the four colour theorem in 1976, and Tymoczko argued in 1979 that its proof was not surveyable and brought experiment into mathematics. In the same year De Millo, Lipton and Perlis described proof as a social process. Gonthier and Werner checked the four colour proof in Coq in 2005. Hales announced the proof of the Kepler conjecture in 1998, and the Flyspeck project completed its formal verification in 2014. The Boolean Pythagorean triples problem was solved by a SAT solver in 2016, with a proof certificate of about $200$ terabytes. Long human proofs raised the same issue: the Feit–Thompson theorem (1963) ran to $255$ pages, and the classification of finite simple groups, announced as complete in 1983, needed the quasithin case of Aschbacher and Smith, published in 2004.

Acceptance has its own history of errors and repairs. Wiles announced Fermat's last theorem in June 1993; a gap was found that autumn, and the repair with Taylor was published in 1995. Perelman's preprints of 2002 and 2003 were checked by several groups by 2006. Mochizuki's preprints of 2012 were questioned by Scholze and Stix in 2018 and published in 2021, without agreement among experts. In 1993 Jaffe and Quinn proposed labelling speculative work "theoretical mathematics", and Thurston's reply "On proof and progress in mathematics" (1994) appeared with fifteen other responses. Erdős had spoken of "The Book" of perfect proofs for many years when Aigner and Ziegler published *Proofs from THE BOOK* in 1998, two years after his death.

## Further reading

- Imre Lakatos, *Proofs and Refutations* (Cambridge University Press, 1976). A dialogue on how proofs and definitions change under counterexamples.
- William P. Thurston, "On proof and progress in mathematics", *Bulletin of the American Mathematical Society* 30 (1994). The essay that answered Jaffe and Quinn, on proof as a way of building understanding.
- Thomas Tymoczko, "The four-color problem and its philosophical significance", *Journal of Philosophy* 76 (1979). The source of the debate on surveyability.
- Martin Aigner and Günter M. Ziegler, *Proofs from THE BOOK* (Springer, first edition 1998). A collection of proofs in Erdős's sense, from number theory to graph theory.
- Roger B. Nelsen, *Proofs without Words* (Mathematical Association of America). Several volumes of pictures, each with a short reference to the identity it shows.
- Donald MacKenzie, *Mechanizing Proof: Computing, Risk, and Trust* (MIT Press, 2001). A history of computer proof and of the arguments about whether to trust it.
