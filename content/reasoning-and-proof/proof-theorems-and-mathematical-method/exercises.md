1. ◦ Say whether each argument is deductive, enumeratively inductive or abductive, in the sense of [deductive, inductive and abductive reasoning](/5V35SGEN1T/). (a) Every multiple of $6$ is a multiple of $3$, and $84 = 6 \cdot 14$, so $84$ is a multiple of $3$. (b) The numbers $11^2 = 121$, $101^2 = 10201$ and $1001^2 = 1002001$ are palindromes, so $(10^k + 1)^2$ is a palindrome for every $k \ge 1$. (c) The sums $\frac{1}{1 \cdot 2}$, $\frac{1}{1 \cdot 2} + \frac{1}{2 \cdot 3}$ and $\frac{1}{1 \cdot 2} + \frac{1}{2 \cdot 3} + \frac{1}{3 \cdot 4}$ equal $\frac12$, $\frac23$, $\frac34$; this would be explained if $\frac{1}{k(k+1)} = \frac1k - \frac{1}{k+1}$, so that the sum telescopes, and so that is probably the reason. Then decide whether each conclusion is true.

2. ◦ Write out the hypotheses and the conclusion of each statement, in the sense of [hypotheses and conclusion](/W48XN2MEM9/). (a) If $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and $f(a) < 0 < f(b)$, then $f(c) = 0$ for some $c$ in $(a, b)$. (b) Every prime $p > 2$ is odd. (c) The diagonals of a rhombus are perpendicular.

3. ◦ A student argues: "If $x > 3$, then $x^2 > 9$. Here $x^2 > 9$, so $x > 3$." Name the error, and give a real number $x$ with $x^2 > 9$ and $x \le 3$.

4. ◦ Compute $n^2 + n + 17$ for small $n \ge 0$ and find the smallest $n$ for which the value is not prime. Factor that value, and explain why that $n$ must fail without testing any value for primality.

5. ◦ Give the present status (theorem, refuted, or open) of each statement, with the name of whoever settled it where it is settled: (a) Goldbach's conjecture; (b) Fermat's Last Theorem; (c) Fermat's claim that every number $2^{2^n} + 1$ is prime; (d) Euler's sum of powers conjecture; (e) the Poincaré conjecture; (f) the four colour theorem; (g) the twin prime conjecture.

6. ◦ A book proves the [fundamental theorem of arithmetic](/084HH1NDCB/) with the help of [Euclid's lemma](/051VYKA1EX/), and right after it states: "if a prime $p$ divides $a^2$, then $p$ divides $a$". Which label, in the sense of [theorems, lemmas, corollaries and propositions](/KK5F0F03ZW/), fits Euclid's lemma, and which fits the last statement? Is the last statement less certain than the theorem?

7. ◦ Define $f$ on $[0, 2]$ by $f(x) = -1$ for $x < 1$ and $f(x) = 1$ for $x \ge 1$. Check that $f(0) < 0 < f(2)$ and that $f$ has no zero. Which hypothesis of the intermediate value theorem in Exercise 2(a) fails? Does $f$ contradict the theorem?

8. ◦ Count the vertices $V$, edges $E$ and faces $F$ of a regular octahedron, a triangular prism, a square pyramid and a pentagonal prism, and compute $V - E + F$ for each.

9. ◦ Show that the statement "if $n \mid ab$, then $n \mid a$ or $n \mid b$" is false for $n = 6$. For which integers $n \ge 2$ is it true for all integers $a$ and $b$?

10. • Change the four-point geometry of the [axiomatic method](/GD2JBXJV5Z/) so that its third axiom says "on each line there lie exactly three points", keeping "there are exactly four points" and "for each two distinct points there is exactly one line on which both lie". Prove that the new system is inconsistent: it has no model.

11. • Take the points $1, 2, \dots, 7$ and the seven lines $\set{1,2,4}$, $\set{2,3,5}$, $\set{3,4,6}$, $\set{4,5,7}$, $\set{5,6,1}$, $\set{6,7,2}$, $\set{7,1,3}$. Check that any two distinct points lie on exactly one line, that any two distinct lines share exactly one point, and find the number of lines through each point. What does this show about the axioms "there are exactly seven points", "two distinct points lie on exactly one common line", "each line has exactly three points"?

12. • An [equivalence relation](/0G8VEZ3WTK/) is reflexive, symmetric and transitive. For each of the three properties, give a relation on a set of at most three elements that has the other two properties but not this one. Then find the error in this argument: "Symmetry and transitivity imply reflexivity. Given $a$, take $b$ with $a \mathrel{R} b$; by symmetry $b \mathrel{R} a$, and by transitivity $a \mathrel{R} a$."

13. • The mean value theorem says: if $f$ is continuous on $[a, b]$ and differentiable on $(a, b)$, then $f'(c) = \frac{f(b) - f(a)}{b - a}$ for some $c$ in $(a, b)$. For each function, show that the conclusion fails and name the hypothesis that fails. (a) $f(x) = x$ for $0 \le x < 1$ and $f(1) = 0$, on $[0, 1]$. (b) $f(x) = \abs{x - 1}$ on $[0, 3]$.

14. • (a) Let $p$ be a prime. Use Euclid's lemma to prove that $a^2 \equiv b^2 \pmod p$ implies $a \equiv b$ or $a \equiv -b \pmod p$. (b) Show that the same statement is false modulo $8$ and modulo $15$. (c) Find the smallest $n \ge 2$ for which it is false modulo $n$.

15. • Let $\gcd(a, 15) = 1$. Euler's theorem gives $a^8 \equiv 1 \pmod{15}$. Prove the stronger $a^4 \equiv 1 \pmod{15}$ by applying Fermat's little theorem modulo $3$ and modulo $5$. Then show that $4$ cannot be replaced by $2$.

16. • In the groups $\Z/4 \times \Z/2$ and $\Z/2 \times \Z/2 \times \Z/2$, under componentwise addition, count the elements of order $2$. Explain why the number of elements of order $2$ is an invariant under [group isomorphism](/08MG7H8JW5/), and conclude that the two groups are not isomorphic although both have order $8$.

17. • Put $n$ points on a circle so that no three chords meet at one interior point, and draw every chord. For $n = 1, 2, 3, 4, 5$ the disc is cut into $1, 2, 4, 8, 16$ regions, which suggests $2^{n-1}$ regions. The true number is $\binom{n}{4} + \binom{n}{2} + 1$. Compute it for $n = 6$ and $n = 7$. What does the result say about the conjecture, and what kind of reasoning produced it?

18. • Prove the lemma "for every integer $n$, $n^2 \equiv 0$ or $1 \pmod 4$". Deduce the corollary "no integer $m \equiv 3 \pmod 4$ is a sum of two squares of integers", and apply it to $m = 2023$. Is the converse of the corollary true, that is, is every positive $m \not\equiv 3 \pmod 4$ a sum of two squares? If not, give the smallest counterexample.

19. • Prove $1 + 2 + 4 + \dots + 2^{n-1} = 2^n - 1$ for every $n \ge 1$ in two ways: by [mathematical induction](/0ASJVEFA9J/), and by computing $2S - S$ for the sum $S$. Which proof finds the answer by itself, and which needs the answer given in advance? Use that proof to find a closed form for $1 + 3 + 9 + \dots + 3^{n-1}$.

20. • Remove two opposite corner squares from an $8 \times 8$ chessboard. Prove that the remaining $62$ squares cannot be covered by $31$ dominoes, each covering two squares that share a side. Why does the same argument say nothing when the two removed squares have different colours?

21. • For each structure, decide whether it is a [group](/G5S1M9/); if not, name every group axiom that fails. (a) $(\Z, -)$. (b) $(\N, +)$ with $\N = \set{0, 1, 2, \dots}$. (c) $(\R, \cdot)$. (d) $(\R \setminus \set{0}, \cdot)$. (e) $(\set{1, -1}, \cdot)$. Which of them are non-examples that fail exactly one axiom?

22. • For real numbers, $ab = 0$ implies $a = 0$ or $b = 0$: if $a \neq 0$, multiply by $a^{-1}$ to get $b = 0$. Find $2 \times 2$ matrices $A \neq 0$ and $B \neq 0$ with $AB = 0$, and name the step of the proof that fails for matrices.

23. ★ A geometry has finitely many points, $v \ge 3$ of them, and lines, with two axioms: any two distinct points lie on exactly one common line, and every line has exactly three points. Prove that each point lies on exactly $(v - 1)/2$ lines and that there are exactly $v(v - 1)/6$ lines. Deduce that $v \equiv 1$ or $3 \pmod 6$. Which of $v = 6, 7, \dots, 13$ pass this test?

24. ★ In the geometry of Exercise 23 with $v = 7$, prove from the axioms alone, without using the model of Exercise 11, that any two distinct lines share exactly one point.

25. ★ Prove that no polynomial $f$ with integer coefficients and positive degree has $f(n)$ prime for every integer $n \ge 0$.

26. ★ Prove that a $10 \times 10$ board cannot be covered by $25$ tiles of size $1 \times 4$, each tile covering four squares in a row or in a column. The area does not rule it out, since $100 = 25 \cdot 4$, so you need an [invariant](/8NQ190278Y/).

27. ★ Compare $3^n$ and $n^3$ for $n = 1, \dots, 6$ and state a conjecture for all $n \ge 1$. Then prove it, using induction for $n \ge 4$.
