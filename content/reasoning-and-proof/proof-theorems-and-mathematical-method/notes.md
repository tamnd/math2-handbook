## History

Euclid's *Elements*, written around 300 BC, set the pattern that this chapter describes. It opens with definitions, postulates and common notions, and derives every proposition from them and from propositions proved earlier. For two thousand years it was the model of a deductive science. Later readers found steps that the postulates do not support; the very first proposition, the construction of an equilateral triangle, takes a point where two circles meet, and no postulate says that such a point exists.

The nineteenth century changed what axioms were taken to be. Nikolai Lobachevsky (1829) and János Bolyai (1832) developed, independently, a geometry in which Euclid's parallel postulate fails, and Eugenio Beltrami gave a model of it inside Euclidean geometry in 1868. That model showed that the parallel postulate is independent of the other axioms: it is the classic use of a model to prove independence. Giuseppe Peano stated axioms for the natural numbers in 1889, and David Hilbert's *Grundlagen der Geometrie* (1899) gave a full list of axioms for Euclidean geometry, filled the gaps in Euclid, and proved consistency and independence results by building models. In 1931 Kurt Gödel showed that every consistent, effectively axiomatized theory that contains enough arithmetic leaves some statements undecided, so independence is a possible end for a conjecture.

Many of the conjectures in this chapter have a long history. Fermat believed that all the numbers $2^{2^n} + 1$ are prime, and Euler found the factor $641$ of $2^{32} + 1$ in 1732. Christian Goldbach wrote his conjecture in a letter to Euler in 1742, and it is still open. Fermat's Last Theorem, written in a margin by Fermat in the seventeenth century, was proved by Andrew Wiles in 1994; the proof was published in 1995. Euler's conjecture on sums of powers fell in 1966 to a single counterexample found by Leon Lander and Thomas Parkin with a computer. The four colour theorem was proved by Kenneth Appel and Wolfgang Haken in 1976 with a large computer check, and Grigori Perelman proved the Poincaré conjecture in work posted in 2002 and 2003.

The study of how mathematics is made, as opposed to how it is written, owes much to George Pólya, whose *How to Solve It* (1945) and *Mathematics and Plausible Reasoning* (1954) described the heuristics of discovery and the role of guessing. Imre Lakatos wrote *Proofs and Refutations* as a series of articles in the 1960s, using the history of Euler's polyhedron formula to show how proofs, counterexamples and definitions develop together; the book appeared in 1976, after his death. Michael de Villiers listed the functions of proof in 1990, and Timothy Gowers contrasted problem solvers and theory builders in his essay "The two cultures of mathematics" (2000).

## Further reading

- Daniel J. Velleman, *How to Prove It: A Structured Approach*. A first course in reading and writing proofs, with the logic needed and many exercises.
- George Pólya, *How to Solve It*. A short guide to the heuristics of solving problems, with many worked examples.
- George Pólya, *Mathematics and Plausible Reasoning* (two volumes). How conjectures are found by induction and analogy, and how they are tested.
- Imre Lakatos, *Proofs and Refutations: The Logic of Mathematical Discovery*. A dialogue on Euler's formula $V - E + F = 2$ that shows how counterexamples reshape definitions and theorems.
- Philip J. Davis and Reuben Hersh, *The Mathematical Experience*. Essays on what mathematicians do, from proof and rigour to the role of examples and computers.
- David Hilbert, *Foundations of Geometry*. The classic modern axiom system for plane and solid geometry, with its models and independence proofs.
