## History

Greek mathematics made proof the standard. Aristotle studied deductive arguments, in the form of syllogisms, in the fourth century BC. Around 300 BC [Euclid's Elements](/83WHDAV68J/) set out geometry and number theory as definitions, postulates and propositions, each proposition with a proof, and it ended with the construction of the five regular solids and the argument that there are no others. For about two thousand years the *Elements* was the model of a deductive science. Proclus, in his commentary on Euclid, named the parts of a proposition, from the general statement of the hypotheses and the conclusion to the proof.

The nineteenth century rebuilt the foundations. Attempts to prove the [parallel postulate](/2PC3XY0PYZ/) from the other postulates failed, and [non-Euclidean geometry](/SPCYMFN5V2/) showed that it is independent of them. Gottlob Frege published the *Begriffsschrift* in 1879, a formal language in which each step of a proof follows a stated rule. Giuseppe Peano gave his axioms for the natural numbers in 1889, and David Hilbert published *Grundlagen der Geometrie* in 1899, with a complete axiom system for Euclidean geometry and proofs of independence by models. In 1931 Kurt Gödel's [incompleteness theorems](/X9WYNYE29C/) showed the limits of the method. The same period made mathematics more abstract: Arthur Cayley defined a group by its operation in 1854, Felix Klein's Erlangen program of 1872 described each geometry by the invariants of a group, Peano gave an axiomatic definition of a vector space in 1888, Maurice Fréchet introduced metric spaces in 1906 and Felix Hausdorff defined topological spaces in 1914.

Writers also studied how mathematics is made, not only how it is justified. Henri Poincaré described one of his own discoveries in *Science et méthode* (1908), and Jacques Hadamard collected such accounts in *The Psychology of Invention in the Mathematical Field* (1945). George Pólya wrote *How to Solve It* (1945) and *Mathematics and Plausible Reasoning* (1954) on guessing, specializing and generalizing. Imre Lakatos built *Proofs and Refutations* on the history of [Euler's polyhedron formula](/09HT3P35YP/); his articles appeared in the 1960s and the book in 1976, after his death in 1974. Michael de Villiers listed the functions of proof in "The role and function of proof in mathematics" (*Pythagoras*, 1990), and Timothy Gowers described the two cultures of problem solvers and theory builders in an essay of 2000.

Conjectures have marked the progress of the subject. Fermat stated his last theorem around 1637, and Goldbach wrote his conjecture in a letter to Euler in 1742. At the Paris congress of 1900 Hilbert discussed ten open problems, and the published list has twenty-three. The [four colour theorem](/0M406JC8J1/) was proved by Kenneth Appel and Wolfgang Haken in 1976 with an essential computer check, Andrew Wiles proved Fermat's Last Theorem in 1994, with a step completed jointly with Richard Taylor, and Thomas Hales announced a proof of the Kepler conjecture in 1998. Other conjectures fell: Leon Lander and Thomas Parkin refuted Euler's sum of powers conjecture in 1966, and Andrew Odlyzko and Herman te Riele the Mertens conjecture in 1985. The classification of finite simple groups was completed in 2004. Today [proof assistants](/YM6PRDE88V/) such as Lean, Coq and Isabelle check formal proofs of large theorems.

## Further reading

- Daniel J. Velleman, *How to Prove It: A Structured Approach*, third edition (2019). A first course in reading and writing proofs, with the logic of statements and many exercises.
- George Pólya, *How to Solve It* (1945). A short classic on how to find a proof: understand the problem, make a plan, carry it out, look back.
- George Pólya, *Mathematics and Plausible Reasoning* (1954), in two volumes. Shows induction and analogy at work before a proof exists.
- Imre Lakatos, *Proofs and Refutations: The Logic of Mathematical Discovery* (1976). A dialogue on Euler's formula that shows how counterexamples reshape conjectures, proofs and definitions.
- Jacques Hadamard, *The Psychology of Invention in the Mathematical Field* (1945). Accounts by mathematicians of how their discoveries came.
- Timothy Gowers, "The two cultures of mathematics", in *Mathematics: Frontiers and Perspectives* (2000). A short essay on problem solving and theory building as two styles of research.
