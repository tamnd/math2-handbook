The polynomial $n^2 + n + 41$ takes prime values at $n = 0, 1, 2, \ldots, 39$: forty cases in a row. At $n = 40$ its value is $40 \cdot 41 + 41 = 41^2 = 1681$, which is not prime. Forty confirmations did not make the statement "$n^2 + n + 41$ is prime for every $n$" true, and no larger number of them could have. A short argument settles a different claim for all cases at once: if $a = 2k$ and $b = 2m$ are even, then $a + b = 2(k + m)$ is even. The second is a proof. The first is evidence. Mathematics accepts a result only when it has a proof, and the chapter is about that standard and the work that surrounds it.

Proof is the last step of the work, not all of it. A statement is first guessed from examples, as Goldbach guessed from small even numbers that each is a sum of two primes. While it is unproved it is a conjecture. Once proved, it gets a label, such as theorem, lemma or corollary, and a place in a theory that starts from axioms. Along the way, mathematicians test claims on special and extreme cases, generalize what they find, and sort the objects they study into classifications. The entries follow that cycle.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in five groups, read in order.

1. **What a proof is.** The area page [reasoning and proof](/SGSVYFDSYK/) maps the volume. [Mathematical proof](/0B94TKFQJX/) defines a proof as a finite chain of justified steps, and [deductive, inductive and abductive reasoning](/5V35SGEN1T/) explains why only deduction proves. [Mathematical induction](/0ASJVEFA9J/), despite its name, is a deduction.
2. **How results are stated.** [Theorems, lemmas, corollaries and propositions](/KK5F0F03ZW/) are all proved statements; the label signals the role. [Hypotheses and conclusion](/W48XN2MEM9/) shows how to read a statement and why each hypothesis is there.
3. **Axioms and conjectures.** The [axiomatic method](/GD2JBXJV5Z/) builds a theory from primitive terms and axioms, with Euclid and Hilbert as models. A [conjecture](/WMEMMHZ3BH/) is believed but not proved; it can become a theorem or be refuted.
4. **Why mathematicians prove, and how they work.** The [functions of proof](/ENY0GKRJS8/) are verification, explanation, communication, discovery and systematization. The [methodology of mathematics](/3FJ3XMV9KD/) covers how concepts are formed and results are tested.
5. **Examples, generalization and classification.** [Examples in mathematical practice](/F3NNR77WKP/), [abstraction and generalization](/976RZZCT1H/), [classification and invariants](/9HQ3TADPA8/), and the contrast between [problem solvers and theory builders](/Z29HM45R29/).

## A first reading

For a first reading, take these eight entries in order: [mathematical proof](/0B94TKFQJX/), [deductive, inductive and abductive reasoning](/5V35SGEN1T/), [theorems, lemmas, corollaries and propositions](/KK5F0F03ZW/), [hypotheses and conclusion](/W48XN2MEM9/), [axiomatic method](/GD2JBXJV5Z/), [conjecture](/WMEMMHZ3BH/), [examples in mathematical practice](/F3NNR77WKP/) and [classification and invariants](/9HQ3TADPA8/). They give the vocabulary that the rest of the volume uses. The functions of proof, the methodology of mathematics and the two cultures of problem solving and theory building describe how mathematicians work rather than what they prove; read them when you have written some proofs of your own. Skim the area page now and return to it when you start a later chapter.

## Before you start

No earlier chapter is needed: this is the start of the volume, and most examples use school algebra and geometry. A few examples in the entries on hypotheses, abstraction and classification use first-year notions such as [continuity](/0X3WGX95FE/) and [groups](/G5S1M9/); you can skip those examples on a first pass. The chapter on [the language of mathematical statements](/WMY13MXY3W/) explains "if", "for all" and "there exists" in detail, and it helps to read it alongside this one. For the formal side of proof, with proofs as objects that a machine can check, see [mathematical logic](/DYJ0KGTPMR/).
