## History

Collections of false proofs are as old as Greek geometry. Later Greek writers attributed to Euclid a book called the *Pseudaria*, a collection of false proofs in geometry, and the book is lost. Aristotle classified errors of argument in the *Sophistical Refutations*, and in that work and the *Prior Analytics* he described the error of asking for the original point, assuming what is to be proved. The Latin *petitio principii* renders his phrase, and the English "begging the question" comes from a sixteenth-century translation of the Latin.

Some of the most instructive errors come from strong mathematicians. Adrien-Marie Legendre gave several proofs of the parallel postulate in the editions of his *Éléments de géométrie*, first published in 1794, and each used an assumption equivalent to the postulate. In his *Cours d'analyse* of 1821 Augustin-Louis Cauchy stated that a convergent series of continuous functions has a continuous sum, and in 1826 Niels Henrik Abel pointed to a Fourier series whose sum jumps; read with modern definitions, Cauchy's argument shifts a quantifier, and the correct theorem needs uniform convergence. In 1847 Gabriel Lamé announced a proof of Fermat's Last Theorem that assumed unique factorization in rings of cyclotomic integers. Joseph Liouville questioned the assumption at once, and Ernst Kummer, who knew that it can fail, repaired the idea with his ideal numbers.

Two later cases show how long an error can survive. Alfred Kempe published a proof of the four colour theorem in 1879; in 1890 Percy Heawood found a map on which one step fails, and used Kempe's method to prove the five colour theorem instead. Peter Guthrie Tait's proof of 1880 was refuted by Julius Petersen in 1891, and the theorem was proved by Kenneth Appel and Wolfgang Haken in 1976. Andrew Wiles announced a proof of Fermat's Last Theorem in June 1993; a gap was found during refereeing, and Wiles closed it in September 1994 with an argument that uses joint work with Richard Taylor. The two papers appeared in 1995.

The study of fallacies for teaching also has a history. A version of the false proof that every triangle is isosceles was known to Lewis Carroll and was published in 1899. E. A. Maxwell collected many fallacies in *Fallacies in Mathematics* (1959) and used the word "howler" for a wrong method that gives a right answer. Warnings against proof by example became sharper as computers found large counterexamples: George Pólya's conjecture of 1919 on the parity of the number of prime factors was disproved by Haselgrove in 1958, and its smallest counterexample, $906{,}150{,}257$, was found by Tanaka in 1980; Andrew Odlyzko and Herman te Riele disproved the Mertens conjecture in 1985. On proof by intimidation, Gian-Carlo Rota wrote in *Indiscrete Thoughts* (1997) that Mark Kac coined the phrase after lectures by William Feller.

## Further reading

- E. A. Maxwell, *Fallacies in Mathematics*. A short classic collection of false proofs in algebra, geometry and calculus, sorted by the kind of error, with the errors explained.
- Bryan H. Bunch, *Mathematical Fallacies and Paradoxes*. A readable survey of fallacies and paradoxes, from arithmetic to the infinite, at the level of a first course.
- Bernard R. Gelbaum and John M. H. Olmsted, *Counterexamples in Analysis*. Counterexamples that show why each hypothesis of the standard theorems of real analysis is needed, useful for the series, integrals and quantifier shifts of this chapter.
- Daniel J. Velleman, *How to Prove It: A Structured Approach*. The logic of conditionals and quantifiers, with many exercises that train the reader to spot invalid steps.
- Robin Wilson, *Four Colors Suffice: How the Map Problem Was Solved*. The story of the four colour theorem, including Kempe's proof and Heawood's discovery of its gap.
