## History

The study of false proofs is as old as the study of proofs. Proclus reports that Euclid wrote a book of false arguments in geometry, the *Pseudaria*, to train students to detect errors; the book is lost. Aristotle described the logical errors that still carry Latin names, among them assuming the point to be proved, *petitio principii*. Many of the early errors in geometry concerned the parallel postulate: for two thousand years, proofs of it from the other axioms each assumed, without noticing, a statement equivalent to it. Legendre published such attempts in the editions of his *Éléments de géométrie*, first published in 1794.

The nineteenth century supplied the classic cases in analysis. Cauchy stated in 1821 that a convergent series of continuous functions has a continuous sum, and Abel pointed in 1826 to a trigonometric series that seems to contradict it; the gap between pointwise and uniform convergence is a quantifier shift. Riemann's rearrangement theorem showed that a conditionally convergent series can be rearranged to have any sum. In the same century the false proof that every triangle is isosceles circulated; Lewis Carroll knew it, and it was published in 1899. Euclid's own axioms did not settle the betweenness questions on which it turns, and Hilbert's axioms of order, including Pasch's axiom, supplied what was missing.

Errors also reached print in serious work. Kempe's proof of the four colour theorem (1879) stood until Heawood found its error in 1890, and Tait's proof of 1880 stood until Petersen showed in 1891 that it was wrong. Lamé announced a proof of Fermat's Last Theorem in 1847 that assumed unique factorisation in rings of cyclotomic integers; Liouville questioned the assumption at once, and Kummer had shown that it fails for some exponents. Dulac's 1923 proof that a planar polynomial vector field has finitely many limit cycles was found in the early 1980s by Ilyashenko to have a gap, closed by Ilyashenko and by Écalle in 1991 and 1992. Wiles announced a proof of Fermat's Last Theorem in June 1993; Nick Katz found a gap in August, and Wiles closed it in September 1994 using joint work with Richard Taylor.

The danger of generalizing from examples was made vivid by conjectures that held far beyond the range of hand computation. Pólya's conjecture of 1919 on the parity of the number of prime factors was disproved by Haselgrove in 1958, an explicit counterexample was found by Lehman in 1960, and Tanaka found the smallest one, $906{,}150{,}257$, in 1980. The Mertens conjecture was disproved by Odlyzko and te Riele in 1985. Collections of fallacies for teaching followed, notably E. A. Maxwell's *Fallacies in Mathematics* (1959), which used the word "howler" for a wrong method with a right answer. The informal names for rhetorical substitutes for proof have their own anecdotes: Gian-Carlo Rota, in *Indiscrete Thoughts* (1997), credits Mark Kac with the phrase "proof by intimidation", and the memoir of Nathaniel Bowditch (1839) records how many hours each "thus it plainly appears" in Laplace's *Mécanique céleste* cost its translator.

## Further reading

- E. A. Maxwell, *Fallacies in Mathematics* (1959). The classic collection of algebraic, geometric and analytic fallacies, each with its explanation.
- Bryan Bunch, *Mathematical Fallacies and Paradoxes* (1982). A wider survey that sets fallacies beside the paradoxes of logic and set theory.
- Imre Lakatos, *Proofs and Refutations: The Logic of Mathematical Discovery* (1976). A dialogue on Euler's polyhedron formula that shows how counterexamples refine both theorems and proofs.
- Robin Wilson, *Four Colors Suffice: How the Map Problem Was Solved* (2002). The history of the four colour theorem, including the errors of Kempe and Tait.
- Daniel J. Velleman, *How to Prove It: A Structured Approach*, 3rd edition (2019). Its treatment of quantifiers and of the logical form of proofs explains why the errors of logic in this chapter are errors.
