Every rule of algebra comes with a range of validity, and most fallacies use a rule just outside it. The chain

$$1 = \sqrt{1} = \sqrt{(-1)(-1)} = \sqrt{-1}\,\sqrt{-1} = i \cdot i = -1$$

looks like a list of routine steps. The false one is the third: $\sqrt{ab} = \sqrt a \, \sqrt b$ holds for $a, b \ge 0$ and fails here. In the same way, solving $\sqrt{x + 2} = x$ by squaring gives $x^2 - x - 2 = 0$, with roots $2$ and $-1$, and only $2$ satisfies the original equation, because squaring is not a reversible step. Errors of logic work the same way. The polynomial $n^2 + n + 41$ is prime for $n = 0, 1, \dots, 39$, and still the claim "$n^2 + n + 41$ is prime for every $n$" is false, because $40^2 + 40 + 41 = 41^2$.

The chapter collects the standard errors: what they look like, the step that fails, and the correct rule nearby. Finding the failing step in a fallacy is a good test of whether a rule is understood, since the reader must know not only the rule but its hypothesis. The same skill is used to check real proofs, where the errors are not planted and some have survived for years in print.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in five groups, read in order.

1. **What a fallacy is.** The entry on [mathematical fallacies](/S2BW0FDHV8/) defines the term, separates a fallacy from a paradox and from a mere slip, and introduces the howler, a wrong method that gives a right answer.
2. **Rules used outside their range.** [Division by zero](/V7601AG5G3/), [roots and powers](/75ZEQ21AG2/) of negative and complex numbers, and [series and integrals](/KDXKVT5K1E/) handled as if they were finite sums.
3. **Figures that mislead.** [Geometric fallacies](/5CJ4KD4D96/), such as the proof that every triangle is isosceles and the missing square puzzle.
4. **Errors of logic.** [Circular reasoning](/JPHE53PABT/), [affirming the consequent and denying the antecedent](/J7KFCFQRBT/), [proof by example](/ZX6ERT3SW7/), the [quantifier shift](/CRW82NARQV/) and [non-reversible steps](/641ZP4RA5S/).
5. **Errors in practice.** [Famous incorrect proofs](/1CV4HTA6C8/), from Kempe's proof of the four colour theorem to the gap in the first version of Wiles's proof, and [proof by intimidation and handwaving](/D8A1K052XV/).

## A first reading

Read [mathematical fallacies](/S2BW0FDHV8/), [division by zero](/V7601AG5G3/), [roots and powers](/75ZEQ21AG2/), [affirming the consequent](/J7KFCFQRBT/), [proof by example](/ZX6ERT3SW7/), the [quantifier shift](/CRW82NARQV/), [non-reversible steps](/641ZP4RA5S/) and [famous incorrect proofs](/1CV4HTA6C8/). These are the errors that appear most often in student work.

The entry on series and integrals needs a first course in calculus and is best read after one; its examples of rearranged series and iterated limits connect with real analysis. The geometric fallacies, circular reasoning and the entry on proof by intimidation can be read in any order.

## Before you start

The chapter assumes the [basic proof methods](/958EF46EYJ/) and the [language of mathematical statements](/WMY13MXY3W/), in particular [conditional statements](/FA6YZGR2SS/) and the [order of quantifiers](/0FYQMKFE77/). Errors in proofs by induction, such as the argument that [all horses are the same colour](/AJ2FDJJGTH/), are treated with induction in their own entry on [errors in induction proofs](/V52HDTMPV5/). The algebraic examples use complex numbers at the level of $e^{i\theta} = \cos\theta + i \sin\theta$, and the analytic ones use convergent series and improper integrals.
