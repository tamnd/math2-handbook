Mathematicians believe many statements long before they can prove them, and they believe them for reasons. Euler was sure that $\sum 1/n^2 = \pi^2/6$ before he had a proof he trusted, because the value agreed with his numerical sums to many digits and because his argument by analogy with polynomials also gave correct results in cases he could check. In 1995 a program searching for integer relations among constants found a new formula for $\pi$, the BBP formula, and a proof followed. Most number theorists believe the twin prime conjecture because a probabilistic model of the primes predicts how many twin primes there are below $x$, and the count agrees with the data.

Evidence of this kind can also fail. The polynomial $n^2 + n + 41$ is prime for $n = 0, 1, \ldots, 39$ and not for $n = 40$. The inequality $\pi(x) < \operatorname{li}(x)$ holds for every $x$ that has ever been computed, and yet it is false for some $x$ below $1.4 \cdot 10^{316}$. This chapter is about how mathematicians collect evidence that is not a proof, how they weigh it, and how they are misled by it.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in four groups, read in order.

1. **Evidence that is not proof.** [Plausible reasoning](/8DHD3H1CJ9/) gives Pólya's patterns: "$A$ implies $B$, and $B$ is true" makes $A$ more credible, never certain. [Experimental mathematics](/BPEPTN0TT9/) computes to high precision and identifies exact values with tools such as PSLQ.
2. **When evidence misleads.** [Numerical evidence for conjectures](/JFD3QWCX8Z/) asks how far a check must go, with the cases of Pólya, Mertens and Skewes. The [strong law of small numbers](/8AFXPXCE3N/) explains why early cases agree by coincidence, and [patterns that eventually fail](/A2KSJSQSF6/) collects four examples.
3. **From heuristic to conjecture.** [Heuristic arguments](/VCQQNJ3SEZ/) predict facts from random models, dimension counts and scaling. [Forming conjectures](/R2T42N5XGK/) turns data into a precise, testable statement.
4. **Machines and physics as sources.** [Machine-assisted discovery](/D1YQW43N3K/), from Graffiti to search systems of the 2020s, and [physical arguments](/THJV0NFPDQ/), from Archimedes' lever to soap films.

## A first reading

Read [plausible reasoning](/8DHD3H1CJ9/), [the strong law of small numbers](/8AFXPXCE3N/), [patterns that eventually fail](/A2KSJSQSF6/), [forming conjectures](/R2T42N5XGK/), [numerical evidence for conjectures](/JFD3QWCX8Z/) and [experimental mathematics](/BPEPTN0TT9/), in that order. These six use only school algebra, first-year calculus and elementary number theory. Then read [heuristic arguments](/VCQQNJ3SEZ/), which needs the prime number theorem, and the entries on machines and physical arguments, which can wait.

## Before you start

You need the notions of [mathematical proof](/0B94TKFQJX/), [conjecture](/WMEMMHZ3BH/) and [counterexample](/0ED72CTBNP/), and the difference between [deductive, inductive and abductive reasoning](/5V35SGEN1T/), all from the chapter on [proof, theorems and mathematical method](/N4P3E53HM0/). Some entries use the [prime number theorem](/0EHBY5Y84N/), power series and a little linear algebra. The chapter on [heuristics of discovery](/X1NSTVGDNQ/) is a companion: it treats the strategies that find solutions, and this chapter treats the evidence that supports a guess. The chapter on [rigour and the acceptance of proofs](/1BTHSHST4M/) treats computer proofs as proofs; here computation is only evidence.
