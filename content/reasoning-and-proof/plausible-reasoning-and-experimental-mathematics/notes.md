## History

Guessing from cases is as old as number theory. In a letter to Mersenne of 1640 Fermat stated, without proof, which
primes are sums of two squares, and he believed that every number $2^{2^n} + 1$ is prime; the first five
are. In 1732 Euler found $2^{32} + 1 = 641 \cdot 6700417$. Euler was also the great practitioner of reasoning by
analogy: he found $\sum 1/n^2 = \pi^2/6$ by treating $\sin x / x$ like a polynomial with known roots, and then
checked the guess numerically and through further consequences before a full proof was available.

George Pólya made this kind of reasoning an object of study. His *How to Solve It* (1945) already discussed
induction, analogy and heuristic reasoning, and *Mathematics and Plausible Reasoning* (1954) gave, in two volumes,
*Induction and Analogy in Mathematics* and *Patterns of Plausible Inference*, with Euler's work as the main case.
The same century produced the famous warnings. Littlewood proved in 1914 that $\pi(x) - \operatorname{li}(x)$
changes sign infinitely often, against all the evidence of the tables, and Skewes bounded the first change in 1933
(assuming the Riemann hypothesis) and in 1955 (without it). Pólya's own conjecture of 1919 on the Liouville
function was disproved by Haselgrove in 1958, and Tanaka found the least counterexample, $906150257$, in 1980.
Mertens's conjecture of 1897 was disproved by Odlyzko and te Riele in 1985 without any explicit counterexample.
Richard Guy collected the small-number traps in "The strong law of small numbers" in the *American Mathematical
Monthly* in 1988, and added "The second strong law of small numbers" in *Mathematics Magazine* in 1990.

Heuristic models came with analytic number theory. Hardy and Littlewood published conjectured asymptotic formulas
for prime pairs and Goldbach representations in 1923, and Cramér introduced his random model of the primes in
1936. Erdős's lower bound for Ramsey numbers of 1947 showed that a random object could also give a proof, not only
a guess. Maier's theorem of 1985 was the first proof that the Cramér model fails somewhere.

Computers turned the experimental side into a discipline. Neil Sloane began collecting integer sequences in 1964,
and the collection went online in 1996 as the OEIS. The LLL algorithm dates from 1982, and Ferguson and Bailey
described PSLQ in the early 1990s; in 1995 Simon Plouffe found the BBP formula for $\pi$ by an integer relation
search, and Bailey, Peter Borwein and Plouffe published it in 1997. The journal *Experimental Mathematics* was
founded in 1992. Programs that propose conjectures started with Fajtlowicz's Graffiti in the mid-1980s, and
search systems such as FunSearch (December 2023) and AlphaEvolve (May 2025) produced new constructions that were
then checked independently.

Physical arguments are older than all of this. Archimedes described his mechanical method, which balances slices
of solids on a lever, in a letter to Eratosthenes; the text was lost until Heiberg identified it in 1906 in the
Archimedes Palimpsest. Archimedes himself said that the method finds results but does not prove them. In the
nineteenth century Riemann used Dirichlet's principle, the physical idea that an energy has a minimum; Weierstrass
showed that the existence of the minimum needs a proof, and Hilbert gave the principle a rigorous form around 1900.

## Further reading

- George Pólya, *Mathematics and Plausible Reasoning*, two volumes: the source of the patterns of plausible
  inference, with many worked cases from Euler.
- George Pólya, *How to Solve It*: a short, practical guide to heuristic reasoning in solving problems.
- Jonathan Borwein and David Bailey, *Mathematics by Experiment: Plausible Reasoning in the 21st Century*: the
  experimental method with integer relation algorithms, high-precision computation and many case studies.
- Richard K. Guy, "The strong law of small numbers", *American Mathematical Monthly*, 1988: a collection of
  patterns that hold and patterns that fail, posed as puzzles.
- Mark Levi, *The Mathematical Mechanic: Using Physical Reasoning to Solve Problems*: physical arguments for
  results in geometry and calculus, each followed by the mathematics behind it.
