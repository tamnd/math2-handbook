## History

Euler used numerical evidence openly. In the 1730s he computed $\sum 1/n^2$ to many decimals, guessed $\pi^2/6$, and justified it by treating $\sin x / x$ as a polynomial with infinitely many roots, an argument he trusted because it also gave Leibniz's series for $\pi/4$. Gauss studied tables of primes as a boy, around 1792 or 1793, and conjectured that their density near $x$ is $1/\ln x$; the prime number theorem was proved in 1896. Fermat's claim of 1640 that the numbers $2^{2^n} + 1$ are all prime, and Euler's factor $641$ of 1732, became the standard warning. In 1914 Littlewood showed that $\pi(x) - \operatorname{li}(x)$ changes sign infinitely often, against all the evidence of the tables. Skewes gave the first bound for a sign change in 1933, assuming the Riemann hypothesis, and without it in 1955.

George Pólya made plausible reasoning a subject in *Mathematics and Plausible Reasoning* (1954), whose two volumes are *Induction and Analogy in Mathematics* and *Patterns of Plausible Inference*. He described the patterns as a logic of credibility, separate from proof, and he took many of his examples from Euler. His own conjecture of 1919 on the function $L(n)$ was disproved by Haselgrove in 1958, and the Mertens conjecture of 1897 was disproved by Odlyzko and te Riele in 1985. Richard Guy's "strong law of small numbers" was quoted by Martin Gardner in 1980 and published in the *American Mathematical Monthly* in 1988, where it won a Lester R. Ford Award.

Heuristic models of the primes go back to Hardy and Littlewood, whose 1923 conjectures on prime pairs and Goldbach partitions came from the circle method with a correction for small primes. Cramér's random model of 1936 treats $n$ as prime with probability $1/\ln n$. Bateman and Horn extended the Hardy–Littlewood predictions to polynomials in 1962, and Maier showed in 1985 that Cramér's model fails in short intervals.

Experimental mathematics took shape with fast computers and integer relation algorithms. LLL (1982) and PSLQ (Ferguson and Bailey, early 1990s) find small integer relations among real numbers. The journal *Experimental Mathematics* was founded in 1992, Plouffe found the BBP formula in 1995 with a PSLQ search, and Sloane's collection of sequences, begun in 1964, went online in 1996. Programs that propose conjectures began with Lenat's AM in the 1970s and Fajtlowicz's Graffiti from the mid-1980s. The Ramanujan Machine (2021) proposed continued fractions for constants, and FunSearch (2023) and AlphaEvolve (2025) searched spaces of programs for good constructions.

Physical arguments are older than proof by exhaustion. Archimedes' *Method*, a letter to Eratosthenes, was identified by Heiberg in a palimpsest in 1906; it shows how he balanced slices on a lever to find areas and volumes, which he then proved by exhaustion. Riemann's use of Dirichlet's principle, justified by the physics of potentials, was criticized by Weierstrass and made rigorous by Hilbert around 1900. Plateau's experiments with soap films led to the problem of minimal surfaces, which Douglas and Radó solved around 1930.

## Further reading

- George Pólya, *Mathematics and Plausible Reasoning*, 2 volumes (Princeton University Press, 1954). The source of the patterns of plausible inference, with many examples from Euler.
- Jonathan Borwein and David Bailey, *Mathematics by Experiment: Plausible Reasoning in the 21st Century* (A K Peters). Integer relation methods, high-precision computation and many worked discoveries.
- Richard K. Guy, "The strong law of small numbers", *American Mathematical Monthly* 95 (1988). Thirty-five patterns, some true and some false, with the reader asked to tell them apart.
- Mark Levi, *The Mathematical Mechanic* (Princeton University Press, 2009). Physical arguments for theorems in geometry and calculus, each followed by a proof.
- Terence Tao, "Structure and randomness in the prime numbers" (lecture and essay). A short account of why random models of the primes predict so well and where they must be corrected.
