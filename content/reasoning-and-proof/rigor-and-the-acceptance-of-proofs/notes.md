## History

Around 300 BC Euclid's *Elements* set the pattern of definitions, postulates and propositions, each with a proof,
and for two thousand years it was the model of rigour. Its gaps were found only much later: the very first
proposition assumes that two circles meet, which no postulate guarantees. David Hilbert's axioms for geometry of
1899 supplied the missing axioms of order and continuity. Calculus, meanwhile, grew for more than a century on
arguments with infinitesimals that worked without clear foundations, and George Berkeley attacked those
foundations in *The Analyst* in 1734.

The reform of analysis in the nineteenth century answered such doubts with definitions. Bolzano proved the
intermediate value theorem in 1817 and Cauchy in his *Cours d'analyse* of 1821, where he also stated that a
convergent series of continuous functions has a continuous sum. Abel gave a series against this claim in 1826,
Seidel located the failure in 1847, and uniform convergence became a standard notion. Weierstrass presented a
continuous nowhere differentiable function in 1872, and in the same year Dedekind constructed the real numbers by
cuts, so that completeness became a theorem rather than an assumption.

Logic then made the notion of proof itself exact. Frege published his formal language, the *Begriffsschrift*, in
1879, and Whitehead and Russell derived much of mathematics in a formal system in *Principia Mathematica*
(1910–1913). Gödel's incompleteness theorems of 1931 showed that no consistent formal system of this kind with
enough arithmetic proves every true statement of arithmetic. From the 1930s the group writing as Nicolas Bourbaki
presented mathematics from set-theoretic foundations, and Zermelo–Fraenkel set theory became the usual base.

Computers raised new questions. Appel and Haken announced their proof of the four colour theorem in 1976, with a
computer check of many configurations, and Thomas Tymoczko argued in 1979, in "The four-color problem and its
philosophical significance", that the proof could not be surveyed by any person. In the same year De Millo,
Lipton and Perlis described acceptance as a social process. Hales announced a proof of the Kepler conjecture in
1998 that the referees could not check in full; the Flyspeck project completed a formal proof in 2014. Gonthier
and Werner checked a proof of the four colour theorem in Coq in 2005, and in 2016 Heule, Kullmann and Marek solved
the Boolean Pythagorean triples problem with a certificate of about 200 terabytes.

The purpose of proof was debated alongside its correctness. In 1993 Arthur Jaffe and Frank Quinn proposed in the
*Bulletin of the American Mathematical Society* that speculative "theoretical mathematics" be labelled as such,
and in 1994 William Thurston replied in "On proof and progress in mathematics" that proofs serve human
understanding. Paul Erdős spoke of a Book in which the best proof of each theorem is kept, and Martin Aigner and
Günter Ziegler published *Proofs from THE BOOK* in 1998,
a collection that Erdős had helped to plan and that is dedicated to him.

## Further reading

- Imre Lakatos, *Proofs and Refutations: The Logic of Mathematical Discovery*: a dialogue on Euler's formula for
  polyhedra that shows proofs and definitions changing under counterexamples.
- Philip J. Davis and Reuben Hersh, *The Mathematical Experience*: essays on how mathematicians work, and on what
  proof and certainty mean in practice.
- William P. Thurston, "On proof and progress in mathematics", *Bulletin of the American Mathematical Society*,
  1994: a short essay on what proofs are for, still widely read.
- Roger B. Nelsen, *Proofs Without Words: Exercises in Visual Thinking*: a collection of picture proofs, good
  practice in turning a picture into an argument.
- Martin Aigner and Günter M. Ziegler, *Proofs from THE BOOK*: short proofs from many fields, chosen for clarity
  and the single idea behind each.
