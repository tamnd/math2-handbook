What is $\log 8$? A number theorist answers $2.079$, an engineer $0.903$, and a computer scientist $3$, because each takes a different base. All three are right by the convention of their field, and a formula copied from one book into another can change its value without any error in either. In the same way, $\N$ starts at $0$ in some books and at $1$ in others, and $A \subset B$ allows $A = B$ in some books and forbids it in others. Before you use a result, you need to know which convention it was stated in.

Mathematical text also has words with special meanings that no definition gives: a function "vanishes" at a point, a step is "trivial", a map is "canonical", a statement holds "for $n$ sufficiently large" or "for almost all $x$", a bound is "sharp", a proof is "elementary". Some of these words are exact and hide quantifiers: "$2^n > n^3$ for all sufficiently large $n$" means that there is an $N$, here $N = 10$, such that the inequality holds for every $n \geq N$. Others are judgements that the reader must weigh. The chapter collects both kinds, says what each claims, and gives the choices that this book makes.

## Before you start

You need the basic language of [sets](/0BSJ7RQ9BW/) and [functions](/0EPAT51DXQ/), and the chapter on [the language of mathematical statements](/WMY13MXY3W/), which treats quantifiers, conditionals and negation; "eventually" and "frequently" are statements with two quantifiers, and their negations follow its rules. The proofs that use this notation are in [proof methods](/G9JFJGY4TQ/). Some examples use limits, groups, vector spaces or measure; each says so where it starts, and you can skip it on a first pass.
