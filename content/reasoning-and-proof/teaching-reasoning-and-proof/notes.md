## History

The place of proof in school was long the place of Euclid. Geometry with two-column proofs became the standard American course for proof in the early twentieth century, and Herbst (2002) traced how that format arose. In the late 1950s Pierre van Hiele and Dina van Hiele-Geldof described levels of geometric thinking, from recognizing shapes to formal deduction. After Sputnik (1957) and the National Defense Education Act (1958), the New Math brought sets, logic and structure into school, with Dieudonné's call of 1959 to abandon Euclid. It met strong criticism, from Feynman's report on textbooks in 1965 to Kline's *Why Johnny Can't Add* (1973), and it was mostly withdrawn by the mid-1970s.

Research on how students learn to prove grew from the 1980s. Wason's selection task (1966 and 1968) had shown how people test a conditional, and Tall and Vinner (1981) introduced the distinction between concept image and concept definition. Mason and Pimm (1984) described generic examples, and Balacheff distinguished levels from naive empiricism to the thought experiment. Martin and Harel (1989) found that many future teachers accepted examples as proof, Ernest (1989) described the instrumentalist, Platonist and problem-solving views of mathematics held by teachers, and Hanna (1990) separated proofs that prove from proofs that explain. Toulmin's *The Uses of Argument* (1958) became a tool for analysing argument in class, through Krummheuer and later Inglis, Mejía-Ramos and Simpson (2007).

The study of proof at university took shape in the 1990s. R. C. Moore (1994) listed the difficulties of students in a transition course, Harel and Sowder (1998) gave the taxonomy of proof schemes, and Selden and Selden (1995, 2003) studied how students unpack statements and validate proofs. Weber (2001) showed that students who knew the facts often lacked the strategies to use them. Mejía-Ramos and colleagues (2012) proposed a model for assessing comprehension, and eye-tracking studies by Inglis and Alcock (2012) compared how students and mathematicians read proofs. The ICMI Study on proof and proving, held in Taipei in 2009, was published in 2012.

Curricula followed. The NCTM *Principles and Standards for School Mathematics* (2000) made reasoning and proof a process standard for all grades, the Common Core standards of 2010 included "construct viable arguments and critique the reasoning of others" among the mathematical practices, and the English A level content published in 2014 listed methods of proof. The Moore method, named after R. L. Moore, who taught at Texas from 1920 to 1969, was revived as inquiry-based learning, and Laursen and colleagues (2014) measured its effects. From the late 2010s proof assistants entered courses, with Lean exercises such as the Natural Number Game.

## Further reading

- Guershon Harel and Larry Sowder, "Students' proof schemes: results from exploratory studies", in *Research in Collegiate Mathematics Education III* (American Mathematical Society, 1998). The source of the taxonomy of proof schemes.
- David Tall and Shlomo Vinner, "Concept image and concept definition in mathematics with particular reference to limits and continuity", *Educational Studies in Mathematics* 12 (1981). The paper that introduced the distinction.
- Gila Hanna and Michael de Villiers (editors), *Proof and Proving in Mathematics Education: The 19th ICMI Study* (Springer, 2012). A survey of the field, from primary school to university.
- Daniel J. Velleman, *How to Prove It: A Structured Approach* (Cambridge University Press, third edition 2019). A transition course text built around the logical form of statements.
- Keith Weber, "Student difficulty in constructing proofs: the need for strategic knowledge", *Educational Studies in Mathematics* 48 (2001). A study of why students who know the facts still cannot prove.
