## History

The question of how proof should be taught became urgent with the reform of school mathematics in the 1950s and 1960s. In the United States the School Mathematics Study Group began work in the 1950s, and the launch of Sputnik in 1957 and the National Defense Education Act of 1958 brought money and pressure for change. At the Royaumont seminar of 1959 Jean Dieudonné called out "Down with Euclid!", and the New Math put sets and logical structure at the start of school mathematics in several countries. In the Netherlands, Pierre van Hiele and Dina van Hiele-Geldof completed their dissertations on levels of geometric thinking in 1957. Morris Kline summed up the criticism of the New Math in *Why Johnny Can't Add: The Failure of the New Math* (1973). Imre Lakatos published *Proofs and Refutations* as articles in 1963 and 1964 and as a book in 1976, giving teachers a picture of proof as conjecture and criticism.

Research on how students think about proof grew in the 1980s. David Tall and Shlomo Vinner introduced the terms concept image and concept definition in 1981, with examples from limits and continuity. Gary Martin and Guershon Harel (1989) found that many preservice elementary teachers accepted inductive arguments as proofs. Nicolas Balacheff (1988) described a hierarchy of students' arguments from naive empiricism to the thought experiment. Alan Schoenfeld (1988) showed that a well-taught geometry class could still treat proof and construction as unrelated activities, Paul Ernest (1989) described three views of mathematics held by teachers, and Gila Hanna (1990) separated proofs that prove from proofs that explain.

In the 1990s the work turned to university students and to the classroom as a community. Robert Moore (1994) followed students through a transition course and named their difficulties with definitions, notation and getting started. Erna Yackel and Paul Cobb (1996) introduced sociomathematical norms, and Götz Krummheuer used a reduced form of Stephen Toulmin's model from *The Uses of Argument* (1958) to analyse classroom talk. Harel and Larry Sowder (1998) published their taxonomy of proof schemes, and Lisa Healy and Celia Hoyles (2000) surveyed about 2,500 high-attaining students aged 14 to 15 in England and Wales. The NCTM *Principles and Standards for School Mathematics* (2000) made reasoning and proof one of five process standards for all grades.

Andreas Stylianides (2007) proposed a definition of proof for the school classroom. The nineteenth ICMI Study held its conference in Taipei in 2009 and published *Proof and Proving in Mathematics Education*, edited by Hanna and Michael de Villiers, in 2012. The Common Core State Standards (2010) listed "Construct viable arguments and critique the reasoning of others" among their eight practices. Work on reading proofs produced models and tests of proof comprehension, and Sandra Laursen and colleagues (2014) reported on inquiry-based courses in the tradition of R. L. Moore, who taught at the University of Texas from 1920 to 1969. Since the Natural Number Game of Kevin Buzzard and Mohammad Pedramfar (2019), proof assistants have entered teaching, and studies of their effect have begun.

## Further reading

- Gila Hanna and Michael de Villiers (eds.), *Proof and Proving in Mathematics Education: The 19th ICMI Study*. The broadest survey of the field, from school curricula to the transition to university.
- Guershon Harel and Larry Sowder, "Students' proof schemes: Results from exploratory studies", in *Research in Collegiate Mathematics Education III* (1998). The source of the taxonomy of proof schemes, with many transcripts of students.
- David Tall and Shlomo Vinner, "Concept image and concept definition in mathematics with particular reference to limits and continuity", *Educational Studies in Mathematics* (1981). The short paper that introduced the terms.
- Andreas J. Stylianides, "Proof and proving in school mathematics", *Journal for Research in Mathematics Education* (2007). Gives the definition of proof for the classroom and shows how to apply it to lessons.
- Imre Lakatos, *Proofs and Refutations: The Logic of Mathematical Discovery*. A dialogue in a classroom that shows proof growing through counterexamples, and a model for inquiry-based teaching.
- Daniel J. Velleman, *How to Prove It: A Structured Approach*. A standard textbook for transition-to-proof courses, useful for seeing how such a course is organized.
