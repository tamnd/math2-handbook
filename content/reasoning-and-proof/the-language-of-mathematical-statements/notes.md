## History

The analysis of statements is older than algebra. Aristotle's On Interpretation studies affirmation and denial and pairs a universal statement with the particular statement that contradicts it, which is the rule that the negation of "every" is "some … not". Around 300 BC Philo the Dialectician held that a conditional is false only when its antecedent is true and its consequent false. That is the reading of "if … then" used throughout mathematics today, and it was disputed already in antiquity.

For most of its history mathematics was written in words. The algebra of al-Khwarizmi in the 9th century states its rules and solutions in prose. Symbols came slowly: Robert Recorde introduced the sign $=$ in 1557, François Viète wrote letters for known and unknown quantities in the late 16th century, and René Descartes's La Géométrie of 1637 fixed much of the notation of school algebra. The connectives and quantifiers stayed in words for another two centuries.

In the 19th century logic was turned into mathematics. George Boole's The Mathematical Analysis of Logic (1847) and An Investigation of the Laws of Thought (1854) treated statements as quantities with an algebra; his "or" behaved like the exclusive or, and William Stanley Jevons argued in 1864 for the inclusive reading that mathematics now uses. Augustus De Morgan stated the laws for negating "and" and "or" that carry his name. Gottlob Frege's Begriffsschrift (1879) gave the first complete notation for quantifiers. The symbol $\exists$ goes back to Giuseppe Peano, and Gerhard Gentzen introduced $\forall$ in 1935. Bertrand Russell's theory of definite descriptions, in "On Denoting" (1905), analysed "the $x$ such that …" as a claim of existence and uniqueness, and Whitehead and Russell's Principia Mathematica (1910–1913) wrote it with an inverted iota.

Writing about mathematical English as a subject of its own came later. Paul Halmos's essay How to Write Mathematics (1970) gave advice that is still followed, and Halmos said that he had introduced the abbreviation "iff". On the psychological side, Peter Wason's selection task, published in 1966 and studied further in 1968, showed that fewer than about one person in ten picks the right cards for an abstract conditional; Richard Griggs and James Cox showed in 1982 that a version about drinking age is solved far more often. Mohan Ganesalingam's The Language of Mathematics (2013) describes mathematical English with the methods of linguistics.

## Further reading

- Daniel J. Velleman, How to Prove It: A Structured Approach. Its first chapters on sentential logic, quantifiers and the structure of proofs cover this chapter with many exercises on translating and negating.
- Paul R. Halmos, How to Write Mathematics. A short essay on writing mathematical English clearly, with advice on "any", "the" and the placing of quantifiers.
- Kevin Houston, How to Think Like a Mathematician: A Companion to Undergraduate Mathematics. A first-year guide to reading definitions and theorems, with chapters on quantifiers, implications and negation.
- Mohan Ganesalingam, The Language of Mathematics: A Linguistic and Philosophical Investigation. A study of mathematical language as a language, for readers who want to see why its conventions work.
