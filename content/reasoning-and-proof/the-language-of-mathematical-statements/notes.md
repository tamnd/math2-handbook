## History

The logic of mathematical sentences is older than mathematical symbols. Aristotle, in *On Interpretation*, separated sentences that are true or false from sentences such as prayers, and he built his syllogisms from sentences with "all", "some" and "no". His square of opposition pairs "every $A$ is $B$" with "some $A$ is not $B$" as negations of each other. Philo the Dialectician, a Greek logician of about 300 BC, held that a conditional is false only when its antecedent is true and its consequent false. This is the conditional of modern mathematics.

For a long time mathematics itself was written in words. The algebra of al-Khwarizmi, in the ninth century, has no symbols for unknowns. Robert Recorde introduced the sign $=$ in 1557, François Viète used letters for unknowns and for given quantities near the end of the sixteenth century, and René Descartes, in *La Géométrie* of 1637, wrote $x$, $y$, $z$ for unknowns and $a$, $b$, $c$ for known quantities, close to the modern use.

The nineteenth century made logic algebraic. George Boole gave an algebra of statements in 1847 and in *The Laws of Thought* of 1854; his addition of classes required them to have no common member, so his "or" behaved like the exclusive "or". William Stanley Jevons argued for the inclusive reading in 1864, and later logic followed him. Augustus De Morgan, a British logician of the same century, gave his name to the laws that swap "and" and "or" under negation. Gottlob Frege's *Begriffsschrift* of 1879 introduced the quantifier with a bound variable, which made statements with several quantifiers precise. Giuseppe Peano used the symbol $\exists$, and Gerhard Gentzen introduced $\forall$ in 1935. Bertrand Russell analysed "the $x$ such that $P(x)$" through existence and uniqueness in 1905.

The twentieth century turned to the reader. Paul Halmos wrote that he introduced "iff" for "if and only if", and in *How to Write Mathematics* (1970) he advised writers to avoid "any". The psychologist Peter Wason published his selection task in 1966, and it showed that few people read a bare conditional correctly; In a version by Griggs and Cox from 1982, with a rule about drinking beer and age, most people give the right answer. Mohan Ganesalingam, in *The Language of Mathematics* (2013), studied mathematical English with the methods of formal linguistics.

## Further reading

- Daniel J. Velleman, *How to Prove It: A Structured Approach*, third edition (2019). Chapters 1 and 2 treat connectives, conditionals and quantifiers with the aim of reading and writing proofs.
- Paul R. Halmos, *How to Write Mathematics* (1970). A short essay with direct advice on words such as "any", "if" and "iff".
- Kevin Houston, *How to Think Like a Mathematician* (2009). A first-year guide to reading definitions, statements and proofs, with many exercises on quantifiers and negation.
- Mohan Ganesalingam, *The Language of Mathematics: A Linguistic and Philosophical Investigation* (2013). A research monograph on how mathematical text is structured and why its ambiguities are usually harmless.
