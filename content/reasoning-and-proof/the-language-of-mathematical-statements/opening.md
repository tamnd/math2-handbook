A sequence $(a_n)$ of real numbers is bounded when there is a number $M$ such that $\abs{a_n} \leq M$ for every $n$. Swap the two quantifiers and the sentence reads: for every $n$ there is a number $M$ such that $\abs{a_n} \leq M$. That is true of every sequence, since $M = \abs{a_n}$ works, so it defines nothing. The two sentences use the same words in a different order, and only one of them says what "bounded" means. Most errors in reading and writing proofs are of this kind: the mathematics is right, and the sentence says something else.

The chapter is about the sentences of mathematics: what counts as a statement, how "and", "or", "not", "if" and "for every" combine, and how to negate the result. These rules are not formal logic, which the book treats elsewhere, but the logic that sits inside ordinary mathematical English. They decide what a definition demands and what a theorem promises. A reader who cannot tell "$P$ only if $Q$" from "$P$ if $Q$" will prove the [converse](/09A3N7ZN5G/) of the theorem asked for, and a reader who negates "for every $\varepsilon > 0$ there is $N$" wrongly will build a counterexample to the wrong statement.

## What the chapter covers

The chapter is in four groups, read in order.

1. **Statements and the words that join them.** [The language of mathematics](/661V0DH7V3/) describes how words and symbols mix. A [mathematical statement](/S9MHMRAX88/) has exactly one truth value, and an open sentence becomes a statement once its variables are fixed or quantified. [Connectives](/12QQ51VK01/) build compound statements; "or" is inclusive and "but" means "and".
2. **Conditionals and quantifiers.** [Conditional statements](/FA6YZGR2SS/) cover "if ... then", "only if", "necessary", "sufficient" and "if and only if", and the conditional that is true whenever its hypothesis is false. [Quantifiers](/EGTZPA6QH3/) cover "for every" and "there is", including the ones that a text leaves unwritten.
3. **Negation, existence and uniqueness.** [Negating statements](/K930V33BSC/) gives five rules for pushing "not" inward, tested on real definitions. [Existence and uniqueness](/ACSC75R7R5/) explains "exactly one" and "at most one".
4. **Where ordinary language misleads.** [Ambiguity](/8BZPKYF912/) collects the traps of "a", "the", "any", scope and grouping, and the [Wason selection task](/7GYZJ2MGY9/) shows the most famous of them in action.

## Before you start

No earlier chapter is required. School algebra, with inequalities and the integers, is enough for nearly all the examples; the few that use limits, [continuity](/0X3WGX95FE/) or groups say so at the start. Chapter 1.1 on [mathematical proof](/0B94TKFQJX/) helps but is not needed. The formal laws behind these pages are in [propositions, connectives and quantifiers](/PM1AYWABA7/), and the proof methods that use them, such as proof by [contrapositive](/0BB5TPGQ1N/) and by [contradiction](/0J0XBKG64S/), are in [proof methods](/G9JFJGY4TQ/).
